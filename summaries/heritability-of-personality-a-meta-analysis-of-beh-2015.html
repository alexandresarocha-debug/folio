If two people share more than 99 percent of their DNA, how much of the difference in their personalities comes from the rest? Behaviour geneticists answer with heritability: the share of the differences in a trait, within a population, that tracks genetic differences. They estimate it by comparing identical twins, who share almost all their DNA, with non-identical twins, siblings, parents and adopted children.

Tena Vukasović and Denis Bratko, psychologists at the University of Zagreb, pooled this literature in a 2015 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin. They found 134 primary studies with 190 possible effect sizes. After applying their rules, for example self-reported personality and independent samples, 62 independent effect sizes remained, with more than 100,000 participants of both sexes and all ages.

The average heritability was 0.40. In other words, about 40 percent of the differences between people's personalities were linked to genetic differences, and about 60 percent to the environment. Correcting for possible publication bias did not change that conclusion. Neither the personality model used, such as the Big Five, nor the sex of participants made a significant difference.

Study design did. Twin studies gave higher estimates, 0.47 on average, than family and adoption studies, 0.22. The authors say this is the first study to test and confirm this design effect for personality. It matters because the twin method rests on assumptions, for example that identical twins are not treated more alike than non-identical twins, and different designs break those assumptions in different ways.

This fits a much larger picture. A 2015 meta-analysis by Tinca Polderman and colleagues in Nature Genetics combined almost every twin study published over fifty years, covering 17,804 traits and 14,558,903 partly overlapping twin pairs. Across all traits the average heritability was 49 percent, and the data did not support a large role for the shared family environment.

What this means in real life: genes matter for personality, but in these data they account for less than half of the differences between people. For most traits the twin data leave little room for the shared family home, so the environmental part is mostly experiences that differ even between siblings.

Caution: DNA-based methods find much less. Genome-wide studies that use measured variants, such as the 2018 neuroticism study by Nagel and colleagues, explain only about 10 percent of the differences. This gap between twin and DNA estimates is known as missing heritability, and it is still argued over.

Source. Vukasović, T., and Bratko, D. (2015). Heritability of personality: A meta-analysis of behavior genetic studies. Psychological Bulletin, 141(4), 769-785. https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000017
