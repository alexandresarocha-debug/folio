Wayne C. Booth, in The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961), needed a name for a narrator who does not speak or act "in accordance with the norms of the work (which is to say, the implied author's norms)." He called that narrator unreliable. The reliable narrator stands with those norms. The unreliable one is mistaken, or claims virtues the work will not grant. Between the two, Booth says, there is "a confused variety of more-or-less reliable narrators, many of them puzzling mixtures of sound and unsound." His long case is Jane Austen's Emma. The novel stays so close to Emma that the reader rides along inside her mistakes. The same novel keeps another measure in the room, above all Mr. Knightley's, so that Emma's certainty is never the book's certainty. We are inside the mistake and outside it. Distance, for Booth, is that gap, and Austen controls it on purpose. She is not, he argues against Henry James, an unconscious spinster whose best effects just happened.

The book is also an argument with the rule, inherited from Percy Lubbock and from James, that a novelist should show and not tell. Booth does not think showing is a fraud. He thinks the ban on telling is. A narrator who steps forward and judges, or summarizes a year, or tells you what a choice cost, is using a rhetorical tool, not confessing a failure of art. The implied author is his name for the version of the writer that the book itself constructs, the chooser of those tools, who may share very little with the man or woman at the desk. You trust or distrust a narrator by how far that voice drifts from the book's own norms, not by whether the voice is dramatic enough.

The test is ordinary once you have it. When a story is told by the person who did the harm, ask what the book knows that the teller is spending energy not to know. Emma can be corrected by Knightley and by the plot. A teller who never meets a fact he cannot swallow is a different problem, and the book has to plant the contradiction where you can trip on it. Later narratologists, Ansgar Nünning among them in the 1990s, argued that readers do not have a private line to an "implied author." They notice unreliability when the narration contradicts itself, or contradicts knowledge the reader brings. The objection trims Booth's metaphysics and keeps his examples. You still do not take Emma's word for Mr. Elton.
