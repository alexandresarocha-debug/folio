Fiction as an act of persuasion

Wayne C. Booth's The Rhetoric of Fiction, published in 1961, argues that novels are rhetorical even when they pretend not to be. A novel asks a reader to notice some things, to care about some people, to judge some acts, and to withhold judgement on others. Those requests are the rhetoric. They can be made by a narrator who speaks directly, by a scene that has been ordered so that a conclusion feels inescapable, or by a silence where a comment might have stood. Booth's target is the orthodoxy, strong in the wake of Henry James and of Percy Lubbock's The Craft of Fiction, that the highest fiction shows and does not tell, that the author should disappear, and that commentary, summary, and a visible moral intelligence are relics of a clumsier age. He thinks the orthodoxy confuses one powerful technique with the whole art. Telling is not the failure of fiction to be dramatic. Telling is one of fiction's instruments, and some of the greatest novels are great because of the quality of their telling.

The argument begins by refusing a picture of the reader as a witness who simply observes a world the novelist has set going. Even a scene is chosen, framed, and limited. Someone has decided whose perception we share, how long a quarrel lasts on the page, which gesture is included, and which consequence is skipped. Those decisions are persuasive acts. They make a response probable. Booth calls the sum of them rhetoric in order to insist that the persuasion does not start only when a narrator turns to the audience and lectures. The lecture is the obvious case. The composed scene is the case the orthodoxy had managed to misdescribe as pure presentation, as if presentation had no designs on us. Once the designs are admitted, the critical question changes. It is no longer whether the author intrudes. It is whether the intrusion, or the refusal to intrude, serves the particular book.

The implied author and the norms of the work

Booth's most lasting term is the implied author. The person who wrote the book, with a biography and a set of private opinions, is not the figure a reader meets. The reader meets the author the book implies, a version constructed from the choices of the whole. That version has norms. It prefers some goods to others, laughs at some follies rather than others, and holds the characters to standards it may never state as a creed. Two books by one historical person can imply different authors. One book can imply an author wiser, crueller, or more generous than the narrator who speaks its sentences. The implied author is the place where the book's values settle when the narrator cannot be trusted to settle them. Without some such place, Booth thinks, a reader has no way to say that a narration is skewed, because skewed means skewed relative to a standard the work itself provides.

This is the setting for his definition of reliability, which later criticism has argued over and has not managed to do without. A narrator is reliable when he or she speaks and acts in accordance with the norms of the work, the norms of that implied author. A narrator is unreliable when he or she does not. Unreliability is therefore not a charm that any colourful voice possesses, and it is not simply a matter of getting facts wrong. A narrator can report the events accurately and still be unreliable in judgement, praising what the book, taken as a whole, gives us reason to deplore, or missing a moral fact that the arrangement of scenes has made plain. Conversely, a narrator who comments freely, even sententiously, can be reliable, a guide whose judgements the implied author stands behind. Fielding and George Eliot are, for Booth, the obvious demonstrations that commentary can be an instrument of extraordinary precision, and that the wish to silence such narrators was a loss disguised as a technical advance.

Distance, and the control of irony

Reliability is one axis. Distance is the larger subject that contains it. Booth asks the reader to notice several gaps that a novel can widen or close: between the narrator and the implied author, between the narrator and the characters, between the narrator and the reader, between the reader and the characters. Irony lives in those gaps. When we are close to a character and the narrator is close too, the book invites identification. When we are pulled back, by a joke, a cool adjective, or a contradiction the narrator does not see, the book invites judgement. The art is the continuous management of the gaps, not a single decision to be ironic or sincere. A novel that holds one distance for hundreds of pages is making a rhetorical choice as definite as a novel that shifts every chapter. Booth wants the criticism of fiction to be able to describe the choice, sentence by sentence if necessary, rather than to sort books into the shown and the told and then stop.

The ethical stakes of distance are the part of the book that later arguments about dangerous narrators are still having. If rhetoric works, a novel can make us enjoy a judgement we would reject if it were stated as a proposition. It can also make us complicit with a charming voice whose acts, coolly regarded, are cruel. Booth does not conclude that fiction should only offer narrators who are safe to agree with. He concludes that the critic and the reader have a duty to know when they are being asked to agree, and with whom. The implied author is what makes that knowledge possible. We compare the voice in our ear with the pattern of the whole. Where they coincide, we are in the hands of a reliable guide, whether that guide talks a lot or a little. Where they diverge, we are in a dramatic irony, and the pleasure of the book includes the pleasure of not being the narrator.

Against a purity that fiction cannot use

Booth's polemic has a constructive side that is easy to miss if he is remembered only as the man who defended telling. He admires the impersonal techniques of James and of the writers who followed. A limited point of view, a scene that carries its own judgement, a refusal of easy commentary, can be rhetoric of the highest kind, more demanding than a lecture because the reader must complete it. His objection is to the claim that these techniques are pure and that other techniques are cheating. Purity, in this debate, usually means the disappearance of a designing author. Booth thinks the disappearance is always a performance. Someone designed the vanishing, and the design shows in what remains. Once that is granted, the old hierarchy collapses. Summary can be beautiful when a stretch of time needs compression and the narrator's mind is worth hearing. Scene can be lazy when it records talk that has no rhetorical purpose. The craft question, which he shares with the tradition he criticises, is the question of function. The difference is that he will not let function be answered in advance by a ban on whole classes of narrative act.

The book also leaves a problem it cannot quite close, and later narratology has lived in the gap. If the norms of the work are inferred from the work, then a reader whose own norms differ may infer a different implied author. Booth writes as if a careful reader can find the norms the book really has. Readers such as those who followed him have asked whether unreliability is sometimes in the encounter rather than in the text alone, a clash between a narrator and a reader who brings other standards. The question does not erase his distinction. It says that applying the distinction is an act of reading, with the ordinary risks of reading. A definition that makes reliability a relation between narrator and implied norms still gives those risks a place to be argued. A definition that says only "I do not trust this voice" does not. Booth's achievement was to make the argument discussable in the language of craft, at a moment when craft had been narrowed to the single virtue of showing.

Why it matters here

Two ideas in Folio depend on this book. The first is the quarrel with "show, don't tell". Lubbock had praised the scenic method and treated the art of fiction as beginning when the story is placed before the reader as something shown. Booth answers that telling, commentary, and a reliable narrator's direct intelligence are essential resources, used by novelists from Fielding to George Eliot, and that all fiction is rhetoric, a designed guidance of the reader's response. The question is how well the guidance is done. The second idea is the unreliable narrator, a term Booth put into criticism. A narrator is reliable when speaking and acting in accordance with the norms of the work, and unreliable when not. That definition is what makes the differences among later examples intelligible. A narrator may lie, may naively fail to see, or may report facts in a vocabulary that misjudges them. The Remains of the Day and The Murder of Roger Ackroyd are those differences in practice. This book is the shared ground. It refuses a purity that would silence half the art, and it gives a precise name to the gap between a voice and the book that contains it.
