Rod Bond and Peter B. Smith asked whether Solomon Asch’s line experiment was a human constant or a result with an address and a date. Their 1996 paper in Psychological Bulletin gathered 133 studies that used some version of the line-judgment task, from Asch’s own work in the early 1950s through 1994. The studies came from seventeen countries. In each, a person had to match the length of a line after a unanimous majority had given a wrong answer, the same obvious task Asch had used, in which people judging alone almost never miss.

Two patterns held across that pile. Among the studies run in the United States, conformity was smaller in later years than it had been in Asch’s decade. The effect shrank as the calendar moved on from the 1950s. Across countries, three separate surveys of national values, the ones associated with Geert Hofstede, Shalom Schwartz, and Fons Trompenaars, each lined up with the size of the effect. Places scored as more collectivist produced larger conformity on the line task. Places scored as more individualist produced smaller conformity. Bond and Smith’s point was that a single percentage from Swarthmore in the 1950s could not be treated as the rate at which people yield.

For a manager, a teacher, or a clinician, the useful translation is modest. Expect more public agreement with an obvious error in a setting where standing out is expensive and the group is the unit that matters. Expect less of it among people trained to treat a perceptual or technical call as their own, in a period and a place where disagreement is cheap. A hiring panel, a safety briefing, or a classroom in one country is not a reading of “human nature” taken from a college in 1956. If you want the dissent, you still have to make it affordable. Culture shifts the average. It does not remove the pressure of a unanimous room.

The meta-analysis also explains why a British student replication could come out almost empty and still leave Asch’s method standing. S. Perrin and C. Spencer, reporting in 1981, found a conforming answer on only one of 396 critical trials with student subjects. The same line task produced Asch-like compliance when the people facing the majority were youths on probation and the majority were probation officers. Bond and Smith’s 133 studies are the wider map of that fact: the task is stable, and the rate is not.
