Background

Rod Bond and Peter Smith's meta-analysis, Culture and conformity, appeared in Psychological Bulletin in 1996. It gathers studies that used Asch's line-judgement task, or a close descendant, and asks whether the famous pressure is the same everywhere and in every decade. Asch had shown, in the 1950s, that a unanimous wrong majority can draw errors on an obvious perceptual judgement, that the errors are a minority of responses, and that a single ally largely restores independence. What he could not show, because he had studied one place and one period, was whether that pattern was a fixed human response or a historical and cultural one. Bond and Smith treat the line task as an instrument that had, by the 1990s, been used often enough, and in enough countries, to ask the question quantitatively.

Folio's card states their answer in one sentence. Conformity in the Asch paradigm had declined in the United States since the 1950s, and it was higher in collectivist than in individualist cultures. The walkthrough is of how a meta-analysis can say a thing like that, and of what it cannot add to Asch's own levers, the ally and the break in unanimity.

The question

The question is whether the rate of yielding in the line task moves with culture and with era, once the obvious methodological differences among studies are taken into account. Culture, for Bond and Smith, is not a list of national stereotypes. They use the dimension of individualism and collectivism that cross-cultural psychology was then developing: the extent to which a setting values personal independence, as against embeddedness in a group whose expectations are part of the self. A plausible prediction runs in both directions, which is why the question is empirical. A more collectivist setting might produce more yielding, because public disagreement is more costly and harmony more valued. A more individualist setting might produce more yielding of a different kind, or the task might be so elementary, and the lines so obvious, that culture never gets a grip. Era is the other factor.

They were also asking a methods question that any meta-analysis of this paradigm has to ask. Studies differ in the size of the majority, in whether the response is public, in how clear the stimulus is, in who the majority is to the participant, and in whether the participant is a woman or a man. If those differences predict yielding, they belong in the account. If culture still predicts yielding after they are considered, the cultural claim is stronger. Bond and Smith organise the literature so that both kinds of predictor can be seen.

How the meta-analysis was set up

A meta-analysis of this kind starts by defining the family of studies tightly enough that the numbers mean the same thing. Bond and Smith looked for experiments in which a person made a perceptual line judgement in the presence of a majority that, on critical trials, gave a unanimous wrong answer. The outcome they needed was a rate of conforming errors, something that could be compared with Asch's finding that about a third of critical responses matched the majority. Studies that merely talked about conformity in the abstract, or that used a different task, could inform the discussion and could not enter the quantitative pool.

The cultural classification is the step that carries the most judgement. Countries and samples had to be placed on an individualism-collectivism dimension using the schemes then available. That placement is coarser than an ethnography. It treats a national student sample as a carrier of a cultural orientation, which loses region, class and the particular university. Bond and Smith use it because the alternative is to refuse the comparison altogether. Their readers are expected to remember that a dimension is a summary, and that a summary can be associated with a conformity rate without being the mechanism inside any one participant's head. Era, for the American studies, is easier to code and easier to interpret. A year is a year. If later American studies, using the same task, show lower rates, something in that society over those decades has changed the cost or the meaning of public dissent on a trivial question.

What they found

The central cultural result is the one the card reports. Conformity was higher, on average, in studies from more collectivist settings than in studies from more individualist settings. The line task, despite its obvious right answer, was not immune to the value a culture places on standing with the group. The effect is an average across heterogeneous studies, so it describes a tendency in the literature rather than a law that every collectivist classroom will conform more than every individualist one. It is still a tendency with a direction, and it matches the theoretical prediction rather than its opposite. People more embedded in groups that prize consensus were more likely, in this paradigm, to announce the group's line.

The historical result is specific to the United States, where the task had been repeated often enough to see a trend. Later studies found less conformity than the studies of Asch's era. The decline is the reason a 1950s percentage should not be taught as the current American rate. It also suggests that the original finding was never a pure window onto perception. It was a window onto perception inside a particular climate of agreement and authority. As that climate shifted, the same wrong majority, facing the same obvious lines, moved fewer people. Other features of the studies behaved much as Asch's own variations would lead one to expect. Larger majorities, public responses and more ambiguous stimuli were associated with more yielding. The meta-analysis is at its best when it shows culture and era sitting alongside these older, local levers, rather than replacing them.

How the authors read the results

Bond and Smith read the findings as a cultural psychology of a classic effect, not as a demolition of it. Asch's phenomenon replicates often enough to be a real feature of the paradigm, and it replicates at different average levels in different places and times. That combination is more interesting than either half alone. A constant would have suggested a perceptual reflex. A chaos of unrelated rates would have suggested that the original was a fluke. An orderly difference, lined up with individualism and collectivism and with decade, suggests that the pressure Asch isolated is channelled by what a public disagreement means. In a setting where the self is defined partly by harmony with the group, announcing a lone perception is a larger act.

They are appropriately modest about mechanism. A meta-analysis cannot listen to participants the way Asch listened in his interviews, and so it cannot say how much of the cultural difference is informational, a greater trust in the group as a source of knowledge, and how much is normative, a greater reluctance to be the person who splits the group. Both are compatible with the average. The paper's contribution is to show that the average moves, and to show the direction. Anyone who wants the mechanism has to go back to interviews, or to experiments that separate the two influences, as Deutsch and Gerard tried to do. Bond and Smith make that return more urgent, because a mechanism that ignores culture will mis-predict the rate.

Limits

The pool is only as good as the studies in it. Many are small, many are students, and the published record favours studies that found a conformity effect worth reporting. A cultural comparison built on published experiments inherits the publication habits of every country in the set, which may themselves differ. The individualism-collectivism scores are blunt, and a country is not a person. Finding that studies from settings classified as collectivist show higher rates is not the same as finding that a collectivist orientation in a given participant produces a yield. That inference is ecological, and it is a real limit on how finely the result can be used. The meta-analysis stops in the mid-1990s, so the American decline it establishes is a decline up to that point.

Stimulus clarity and public response remain confounded with culture in some of the studies, despite the authors' efforts to code them. A tradition of research in one country may have preferred a particular variant of the method. If that variant is independently harder, the cultural score will pick up the method. Bond and Smith do the standard things a careful meta-analysis can do about this, and a reader should still not treat the cultural coefficient as if it were a randomised assignment of cultures. It is a pattern in a literature.

Why it matters here

Folio's card uses this paper to stop the line study hardening into a universal percentage. Asch found a real pressure and a real residue of independence, in one place, in one decade, with one kind of group. Bond and Smith show that the pressure has been stronger where group embeddedness is more valued, and weaker in later American replications than in the original period. The practical lesson of the card, that one ally makes dissent far easier, sits on top of that variation rather than being cancelled by it. Asch's own variations, the ally and the private response, are about the structure of the immediate situation. Culture, on Bond and Smith's evidence, changes the baseline rate those levers act on. An ally in a high-conformity setting and an ally in a low-conformity setting are both allies. They are not starting from the same place.

Read with the card's other texts, the meta-analysis is the comparative frame and Deutsch and Gerard are the mechanism. Asch remains the method and the interviews. The thing to refuse is the single mental image of a sheepish student and a timeless majority. The student was often independent, the majority's power saturated quickly, and the whole pattern has moved with culture and with the calendar. That is a more useful fact than the parable, because it tells a person both that the pressure is real and that it is not destiny.
