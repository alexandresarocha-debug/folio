Background

Phillippa Lally, Cornelia van Jaarsveld, Henry Potts and Jane Wardle published How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world in the European Journal of Social Psychology in 2010. It is the study Folio's card cites against the popular claim that a habit takes twenty-one days. Lally and her colleagues asked volunteers to repeat a new behaviour every day, in response to a cue, and to report each day how automatic it felt. They then fitted a curve. The curve rises quickly and levels off, the time to the plateau varies enormously from person to person, and the middle of that variation sits at about sixty-six days. The paper is a modelling study of ordinary people doing ordinary new acts, which is both its value and its limit.

The question

The question is about the shape of formation, not about whether habits exist. If automaticity jumped to its maximum after a fixed small number of repetitions, the folklore of three weeks, or of any other magic number, would be a reasonable summary. If it rose in a straight line for as long as one kept practising, advice would look like a training plan with no end. Lally and colleagues expected neither. They expected an asymptotic curve, the shape associative learning usually takes: large gains early, smaller gains later, and a plateau at which further repetitions add little to the feeling that the behaviour happens without deliberation. The practical content of the expectation is that there is no click. A habit, on this view, is not installed on a day. It approaches a ceiling, and the approach is different for different people and different acts.

They also wanted to know whether a missed day ruins the approach. Popular advice often treats a lapse as a return to zero, which makes formation feel brittle and encourages people to abandon a streak. If the curve is a slow associative accumulation, one missed occasion should be a small subtraction, not a collapse. The study is built to see the shape, the typical time, the spread around the typical time, and the effect of a gap.

How the study was set up

Ninety-six volunteers each chose a behaviour they wanted to make daily, tied to a cue that already existed in their day. The behaviours were simple and specified: a kind of eating, a kind of drinking, or a brief activity, done in response to a regular event. This is a real-world study in the strict sense. The experimenters did not assign the habit, did not watch it happen, and did not reward it. They asked people to choose something they could repeat, and then to report, each day, whether they had done it and how automatic it felt. Automaticity was measured with items from the Self-Report Habit Index, the ones that ask whether a behaviour is done without thinking, without having to remember, and with a sense that it would feel odd not to do it. A daily score on those items is the outcome the model is fitted to.

The modelling choice is the heart of the method. For each person, the daily automaticity ratings were fitted with a curve that is allowed to rise towards an asymptote. From the fitted curve one can estimate how long it takes that person to reach most of the way to his or her own plateau. The summary of those estimates is a median, not a mean, precisely because the distribution is expected to be skewed by people who take a very long time or who never clearly level off. The study ran long enough that a two-month process could be seen, and not so long that a year-long process could be followed to its end in every case. People who stopped filling in the diary, and people whose ratings never settled, are part of the result. A formation paper that only reported the tidy completers would have flattered the phenomenon.

What they found

The curves had the shape the authors expected. Automaticity increased quickly over the early repetitions and then flattened. The median time to reach the plateau was sixty-six days. The range was very wide, from eighteen days at the short end to two hundred and fifty-four days at the long end. Both numbers are in the card because both are required for the moral. Sixty-six days is a description of the middle, useful as a correction to twenty-one and useless as a new superstition. The range says that a person who is not there at two months is not a person who has failed a law. Some behaviours, and some people, were still climbing, or had only just arrived, long after others had levelled off.

A missed day did not reset the process. People who skipped an occasion did not, in the fitted curves, fall back to the beginning. The accumulation was tolerant of ordinary life, in which days are missed because the cue did not occur or the person was interrupted. That finding is easy to under-quote and easy to over-quote. It does not say that lapses are free, or that a behaviour done twice a week will become automatic on the same schedule as a daily one. It says that the model of formation as a brittle streak, ruined by a single gap, is a worse description of these data than a model of gradual strengthening. The twenty-one-day rule is retired twice. It is too short for the median, and it is too precise for the range.

How the authors read it

Lally and colleagues read the asymptote as the signature of habit formation in the wild. Early repetitions do the most to make a new act feel automatic, which is encouraging, because the first weeks are when a person most wants evidence that the effort is doing something. Later repetitions consolidate, which is less dramatic and easily mistaken for a stall. A person who understands the shape will not expect a morning on which the behaviour suddenly costs nothing. She will expect a diminishing return that eventually feels like the behaviour belongs to the cue. The authors tie this to the associative definition Wood and others were defending. A context, a response, many pairings, a growing likelihood that the context starts the response without a decision. The self-report of automaticity is their proxy for that likelihood, and the curve is what the proxy did over calendar time.

They also read the individual differences as a warning against recipes. The same instruction, "do this every day after this cue", produced plateaus at very different times. Some of that spread will be the behaviour itself. A simple drink is an easier association than a bout of exercise. Some will be the stability of the cue, the person's life, the consistency they actually managed. The study was not powered, and not designed, to explain the spread in full. Its message is that the spread is the fact a recipe has to live with. Advising everyone to expect a habit in three weeks produces false failure. Advising everyone to expect it in exactly sixty-six days would produce the same false failure, a little later. The honest advice is the shape, the median as a rough scale, and the range as the reason to continue past an arbitrary date.

Limits

The volunteers chose the behaviours, chose to join a study about habits, and reported their own automaticity. People who sign up for such a study are more motivated than the average person making a resolution in January, and self-report of "I do this without thinking" can drift because the person wants to see progress. The curve may partly be a curve of optimism about one's progress. The behaviours were small. Eating a piece of fruit with lunch, or doing a short exercise, is not stopping smoking and not rewriting a working day. A finding about simple daily acts should not be stretched into a claim about the most entrenched habits, which were built over years and which are cued in more ways than one. The study also ends.

There is no measure of whether the behaviour was still there a year later, and no measure of health or of any outcome beyond automaticity. The paper answers "how does the feeling of habit grow while people are trying?" It does not answer "what makes a habit survive once the trying stops?" Those are Wood and Neal's questions about cuing and context, and they need different designs. The card's advice about stable cues is the other half of the story, and this paper does not test it.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on habits uses this study to retire a number and replace it with a curve. Ninety-six volunteers, a daily behaviour of their own choosing, a median of sixty-six days to a plateau of automaticity, a range from eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four days, and a missed day that did not wipe the process out. The popular claim that a habit takes twenty-one days is not supported. Neither is any new claim that it takes sixty-six, said as if the median were a rule. The useful picture is early gains, a long and variable approach to a ceiling, and tolerance of the odd gap. That picture is what a person needs if he is not to abandon a repetition that is working more slowly than a magazine promised.

Read beside Wood and Neal, the study supplies the time constant their theory left unspecified. Context-response associations form by repetition. Lally and colleagues show what repetition felt like, day by day, for people who were willing to try. Read beside Galla and Duckworth, it supplies a reason why beneficial habits are worth the dull work of installation. A habit that will eventually fire without a decision is an asset, and the asset takes longer, and more variably, than motivation culture has liked to say. The card's practical line, attach new behaviours to stable cues and expect the process to be measured in months for many people, is this paper translated. The sixty-six days are a landmark, not a contract.
