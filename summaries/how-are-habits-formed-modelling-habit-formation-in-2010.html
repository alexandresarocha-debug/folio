In 2010 Phillippa Lally, Cornelia van Jaarsveld, Henry Potts, and Jane Wardle asked 96 volunteers, mostly graduate students with an average age of 27, to pick one new behavior and do it every day in the same context for 12 weeks. Twenty-seven chose something to eat, 31 a drink, often water, 34 an exercise, and four something else, such as meditation. Each day they logged whether they had done it and answered items from the Self-Report Habit Index about doing the act without thinking and without having to remember.

Eighty-two people gave enough data to analyze. A curve could be fitted for 62, and the fit was a good one for 39. For those 39, the time to reach 95% of their own plateau of automaticity had a median of 66 days, with a range from 18 days to 254. Ten were working on eating, 15 on drinking, and 13 on exercise. Missing a single chance to perform the behavior did not materially set the curve back. The authors also wrote that about half of these motivated volunteers did not repeat the behavior consistently enough for the model to count them as having reached a habit.

The 66 days describe the middle of the people whose scores fit the curve. They are a poor candidate for a new rule. The shape is the part worth keeping. Automaticity rose faster in the early repetitions and then flattened, and the time to the flat part varied by many months from person to person. Someone who is still working at it after three weeks, or after two months, is inside the spread this study found.

In ordinary life the useful pieces are the cue and the missed day. Tie the new act to something that already happens, expect the feeling of automaticity to arrive on a curve, and treat a gap as a gap. A 2024 review of later trials reported a 2021 experiment by Keller and colleagues in which the median time, among people who reached that study's habit threshold, was 59 days, the range was 4 to 335 days, and only 23% of participants reached the threshold. A single number keeps failing to describe most people.
