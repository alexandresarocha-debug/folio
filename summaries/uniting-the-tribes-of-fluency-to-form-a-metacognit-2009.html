Adam Alter and Daniel Oppenheimer reviewed a large body of research on processing fluency, the subjective ease or difficulty of taking in information. Fluency can come from many sources: clear or blurred print, easy or hard pronunciation, familiar or unfamiliar words, simple or complex syntax. They argued that these different kinds of fluency, studied by separate research communities, have strikingly similar effects on judgment.

The examples range widely. In their own earlier work on roughly 700 stocks on the New York Stock Exchange between 1990 and 2004, the 10 stocks with the simplest names earned 11% more on their first day of trading than the 10 with the most complex names, a gap that grew to 33% over a year. Rhyming sayings, such as 'Woes unite foes', are processed more easily than non-rhyming versions with the same meaning, and earlier research found them judged more accurate. Oppenheimer's long-words study appears as an example of lexical fluency: needless complexity lowers judgments of the writer.

Across domains, fluent information tends to seem truer, more familiar, more likeable and more valuable, while disfluency can signal that something is wrong or needs more thought. The authors count roughly 20 different ways researchers have manipulated fluency.

What this means in real life: names, slogans, interfaces and documents that are easy to process gain an advantage that has little to do with their quality, which matters for writers, designers and anyone judging information.

Caution: some celebrated fluency effects have not held up. A famous experiment found that a hard-to-read font helped people solve tricky reasoning problems, but when Andrew Meyer and colleagues pooled it with 16 attempts to replicate it, they found no effect on solution rates. Effects that are reliable in one setting may be small or absent in another.
