The habit the essay wants to break

Roland Barthes's The Death of the Author is a short essay, published in English in 1967 in the American magazine Aspen and in French in 1968. Its target is a habit of reading so ordinary that it does not feel like a theory. We explain a work by the person who wrote it. We ask what the author meant, what in the author's life produced the book, what passion or intention stands behind the sentence and guarantees its meaning. Criticism, on this habit, is the recovery of a message from a sender. The author is the sender, a kind of owner and a kind of god, and the text is the place where the god has deposited a meaning. Barthes calls this figure the Author, with the capital that marks a theological role, and he argues that the role has had its day. Writing is not the expression of a prior interior. A text does not contain a single theological meaning, the message of an Author-God. The consequences for criticism, for the classroom, and for the ordinary pleasure of biography are large, which is why a few pages have organised decades of argument.

The essay belongs to the passage from structuralism to post-structuralism, and the belonging is part of the argument rather than a label added later. Structuralism had already shifted attention from the person to the system, from the speaker to the language. Barthes takes the shift into the act of interpretation. If language is a system no speaker invented, then a writer who sits down to express a self is already handling materials that do not belong to the self. The essay's polemical edge is that criticism which hunts the self is not merely incomplete. It is a way of refusing the way texts are made. It restores a father to a discourse that has no father, and it does so in order to stop the discourse from meaning more than one thing.

The text as a tissue of quotations

Barthes's positive description is as important as the attack. A text is a tissue of quotations, drawn from innumerable centres of culture. The writer mixes a script that is already written, in words that have been used, in forms that carry other occasions inside them. Originality, in the sense of a meaning born for the first time from a person's depths, is the illusion the Author-role requires. What a writer can do is combine, arrange, and set going. Barthes points to writers who had already, in different ways, handed the initiative to language. Mallarmé puts language itself in the place of the speaking subject. Valéry insists on the verbal nature of literature against the myth of spontaneous expression. Proust makes the narrator a vast construction rather than a confession, so that the life does not explain the book and the book reorganises the life. Surrealism, he thinks, tried to escape the Author by automatic writing and did not fully escape, because the attempt still imagined a depth, now unconscious, from which the words would genuinely come. The examples are a short history of modern literature read as a series of assaults on the same figure.

Linguistically, the argument is that enunciation is an empty process. The person who says I in a sentence is constituted by saying it, and does not stand behind the sentence as its guarantor. To write is to perform this empty process in a way that detaches the saying from a voice that could be answered. A spoken conversation has a body in the room. A written sentence has departed from any body, and the departure is the condition of its being read by strangers later. Barthes treats that departure as fatal to the Author as an explanatory principle. Once the sentence is written, there is no one behind it to ask. There is the sentence, and there are the cultural codes it mobilises, and there is the person who gathers them by reading. The death in the title is this removal of the guarantor. It is not a claim that the human being who typed the words has physically died, and it is not a claim that writers do not work. It is a claim about where meaning is allowed to be located.

The birth of the reader

If the unity of a text is not given by its origin, it has to be given, if it is given at all, by its destination. Barthes locates the destination in the reader. The reader is the space on which the multiple writings that make up a text are inscribed without any of them being lost. He is careful, and easy to misread, about what sort of reader this is. The reader is not a biographical person with a history and a psychology that would simply replace the author's history and psychology as the new key. That would be the old habit with a new incumbent. The reader, in the essay's terms, is a function, the place where the text's multiplicity is held together. The birth of the reader must be paid for by the death of the Author. Folio's card paraphrases the closing claim in those terms, and the paraphrase keeps the structure. A beginning here requires an ending there. As long as the Author is treated as the owner of the meaning, the reader is a consumer of a message. When the Author is removed, reading becomes the production of the text's unity, and the production is not obliged to come out the same way twice.

This is the step that makes the essay more than a warning against biographical fallacy. Wimsatt and Beardsley had already, in The Intentional Fallacy in 1946, told critics not to judge a poem by what the poet meant to do, and to look at the poem as a public use of language. Barthes is after something more disruptive. He is not only saying that intention is a poor standard. He is saying that the text is plural, a space where writings blend and clash, and that the imposition of a single authorial meaning is a political as well as a hermeneutic act, a way of limiting discourse by appealing to an authority. The late 1960s context, in which authority of several kinds was under attack, is audible. The essay is a theory of reading and a refusal to let a person, however gifted, close a text by having written it.

What the death does not license

The essay is short enough to be asked to do work it refuses. It does not say that texts mean anything at all, or that any reading is as good as any other, or that historical knowledge is useless, or that the name on the title page is a lie in the ordinary sense. A reader who uses it to end an argument, on the grounds that the author is dead and therefore nothing can be said against a personal association, has inverted the essay. Barthes is trying to make reading more responsible to the text's multiplicity, not less responsible to anything. The tissue of quotations is a constraint. You cannot find a quotation in a text that the text does not, in some way, mobilise. You can find more than the author would have acknowledged, and you can find combinations the author did not foresee, because combination is what language does once a person is no longer there to police it. Multiplicity is not randomness. A reading that cannot say which writings the text is blending has not used the essay. It has used the title.

Nor does the essay abolish the practical uses of authors. Libraries, contracts, reputations, and the ordinary gratitude one feels towards a writer who has made something are not the target. The target is the critical habit of treating those practical persons as the meaning of their works. A biography can be true and still be the wrong explanation of a chapter. An author's comment in an interview can be interesting and still have no authority over the sentences, which have already left. Barthes is severe about this, more severe than most readers will want to be on a given afternoon. The severity is the essay's value. It draws a line that later, milder positions can decide to soften. Without the line, the milder positions have nothing to be milder than. E. D. Hirsch's defence of intention, published in the same year, is one refusal of the line. Michel Foucault's essay on the author, two years later, is a different and more historical response, less interested in crowning the reader and more interested in the functions the author's name still performs after the theology has collapsed.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The death of the author, and the birth of the reader, is a reading of this essay, and it keeps the essay's terms. The attack on explaining a work by the author's life, intentions and psychology. The rejection of a single theological meaning. The text as a tissue of quotations drawn from innumerable centres of culture. The unity of a text lying in its destination, the reader. The closing exchange, the birth of the reader paid for by the death of the Author. The card then places the essay in its argument, the shift towards post-structuralism and the critique of authority, and sets two answers beside it. Foucault's What Is an Author? examines the author as a function that organises discourse, rather than simply celebrating the reader's succession. Hirsch's Validity in Interpretation defends authorial intention as the standard of a valid reading. The card's own conclusion is the middle position most readers now occupy, biography is not the only key, and the middle position is intelligible only if one has seen how far Barthes wanted to go. This essay is the far end. It is short on purpose. The argument is the removal of the guarantor, and the removal is the whole of the claim.
