Roland Barthes opens "The Death of the Author" with one sentence from Balzac's story Sarrasine. The story is describing a castrato who is dressed as a woman, and believed, by the sculptor who loves him, to be a woman. Balzac writes: "This was woman herself, with her sudden fears, her irrational whims, her instinctive worries, her impetuous boldness, her fussings, and her delicious sensibility." Barthes asks who is speaking. Is it the hero, determined not to know what the dress conceals? Is it Balzac the man, with a philosophy of Woman drawn from his life? Is it Balzac the author, offering a literary idea of femininity? Is it universal wisdom, or romantic psychology? "We shall never know," Barthes says, "for the good reason that writing is the destruction of every voice, of every point of origin." The sentence about "woman herself" is false to the body in the room. That is why it is his example. A line that sounds like a final truth about women is spoken over a person who is not a woman, and no one inside the sentence can be made to own it cleanly.

From that refusal he draws a history in miniature. The author, a person we imagine standing behind the book and guaranteeing it, is a modern figure, tied to the prestige of the individual. Writing, once it is not a direct act on the world, cuts the voice off from a source. "The voice loses its origin, the author enters into his own death, writing begins." To give a text an author is to impose a limit, a final meaning, the author's explanation. Barthes wants the other end of the line. A text is tissue, drawn from innumerable centers of culture, not from one person. The place where the details are collected is the reader. The essay's last claim is the one people repeat: "the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author." The essay itself first appeared in English, translated by Richard Howard, in the magazine Aspen, numbers 5–6, in 1967, and in French the next year.

In ordinary reading the claim is narrower than the slogan. You do not need Balzac's biography to feel that the sentence about "woman herself" is doing something cruel in that scene. The words work, or fail, in the story. Looking up the life can still tell you about the man and his time. It does not turn the sentence into the property of one intention that ends the argument. E. D. Hirsch argued, the same year, that a reading is valid only when it is the meaning the author willed. Foucault asked, two years later, what job the author's name still does. Barthes's demonstration is the prior question. If you cannot say who speaks, you are already reading.
