In this paper Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky set out the idea that people judge probability by representativeness: how closely an outcome resembles the process that produced it. Most of their data came from questionnaires given to about 1,500 students in grades 10 to 12 of college-preparatory high schools in Israel, aged 15 to 18.

One section asked whether people understand how sample size affects variation. The best-known problem describes a town with two hospitals. About 45 babies are born each day in the larger one and about 15 in the smaller one. Over a year, each recorded the days on which more than 60% of the babies born were boys. Which recorded more such days? Of those who answered, 12 chose the larger hospital, 10 the smaller and 28 said about the same. A second group was asked about days with fewer than 60% boys, where the correct answer is the larger hospital: 9 chose it, 11 chose the smaller and 25 said about the same.

The students answered two similar problems the same way, one about the average word length of pages versus single lines of a book, and one about the heights of men checked in a medical survey. In each case most judged that the size of the sample made no difference, because a proportion of 60% looks equally representative in a small sample or a large one.

Kahneman and Tversky concluded that the notion that sampling variance shrinks as samples grow is not part of people's intuition. A day with a lot of boys looks equally surprising whichever hospital it happens in.

What this means in real life: an extreme result from a small group, such as one shop, one school or one small trial, is weak evidence on its own.

Caution: the respondents were high-school students answering short written problems, and later research found that some ways of asking, for example with frequencies, improve performance. The problem was reprinted in the authors' widely cited 1974 Science article on heuristics and biases.
