If unique experiences make siblings different, it should be possible to find them. Eric Turkheimer and Mary Waldron at the University of Virginia went looking. They described the 1987 Plomin and Daniels review as perhaps the most influential article ever written in developmental behaviour genetics, and then pulled apart its two claims.

The first claim is an observation: much of the variation between people is explained neither by genes nor by the shared family. That part, they agreed, is uncontroversial. The second claim is a hypothesis: that this leftover is caused by real, measurable differences in siblings' lives, such as how parents treat each child. That needed testing.

They searched the research from 1967 to November 1997 and found 43 studies that measured a specific difference between siblings' environments and linked it to a difference in how the siblings turned out. The measures included birth order and age gaps, differences in parents' behaviour, how siblings treated each other, and differences in friends and teachers.

The effects were small. The typical measured difference explained 1.6% of the variation in outcomes (a median r-squared of .016), with a weighted average of 4.1%. In studies that controlled for genes, the median fell to 1.0%. Birth order and age spacing explained about 1.1%. Differences in peers and teachers did best, at about 5.3%. Studies that reported only their significant results showed effects over eight times as large as studies that reported everything.

Their conclusion took up what Plomin and Daniels had called the gloomy prospect: that each event has a very small, unsystematic effect, and only the build-up of many small differences makes siblings diverge. They added chance in development itself. Random processes at the level of cells produce differences even between identical twins, and small amounts of early randomness can snowball.

What this means in real life: no single experience, not even being the firstborn, explains why siblings differ.

Caution: many of the 43 studies were small and measured different things, and several reported only significant results. Direct observation of the environment gave larger effects than reports from others, so better measures might find more. The review reports small effects; it does not prove that chance is the full answer.

Source. Turkheimer, E., and Waldron, M. (2000). Nonshared environment: a theoretical, methodological, and quantitative review. Psychological Bulletin, 126(1), 78-108. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.126.1.78
