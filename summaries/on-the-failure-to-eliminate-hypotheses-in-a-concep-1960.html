Background

Long before confirmation bias was a household phrase, Peter Wason wanted to watch a person try to discover a rule and see whether the attempt was built to succeed. His 1960 paper, On the Failure to Eliminate Hypotheses in a Conceptual Task, is the report of that watch. Participants were told that the triple 2, 4, 6 fitted a rule, and they were invited to discover the rule by offering other triples and being told, each time, whether the triple fitted. The rule he had in mind was deliberately modest. Any three numbers in ascending order would do. What people did with that modesty is the finding Folio's confirmation-bias card retells. They guessed a narrow rule, tested triples that fitted the guess, and announced the guess as the rule.

The paper is easy to confuse with another of Wason's tasks, the four-card problem in which a person must turn cards to test a conditional. That later puzzle is a different experiment, with a different moral about logical form. This one is about hypotheses. The card pairs it with Nickerson's review and with Mercier and Sperber's argumentative theory because it is the clean laboratory picture of a mind that collects yeses.

The question

When people are trying to find a rule, do they look for cases that would show the rule to be wrong, or do they look for cases that fit the rule they already like? Philosophy of science, in the Popperian mood of those years, had a sharp answer. A hypothesis earns its keep by surviving attempts to eliminate it. A test that can only come back positive is not a test. Wason's question was whether ordinary, intelligent people, given a tiny formal task with immediate feedback, would eliminate or would confirm. The task is conceptual rather than factual. Nobody needs to know history or chemistry. The only material is a rule about numbers, and the only virtue required is a willingness to propose a triple that might be rejected.

How the task was set up

Each person learned that 2, 4, 6 was an instance of the rule the experimenter had chosen. They could then propose further triples, one at a time. After each proposal the experimenter said whether the triple conformed. When a person felt ready, they announced the rule. If the announcement was wrong, they could continue. The design gives confirming tests and eliminating tests an equal opportunity. A person who thinks the rule is even numbers ascending by two can propose 8, 10, 12 and hear that it conforms, which feels like progress and is not. The same person can propose 1, 2, 3, or 2, 4, 5, or a descending triple, and learn something the narrow rule cannot survive. The feedback is unambiguous. The only scarce resource is the imagination to offer a triple one's own hypothesis forbids.

The true rule, three ascending numbers, was chosen because it sits inside the rule almost everyone first names. Increasing by two is a subset of ascending. Every confirming instance of the narrow rule is also an instance of the wide one, so a strategy of testing only the narrow rule can run for a long time without a single no. The task is a trap for positive testing, and the trap is the point. A rule that people were unlikely to name first would have produced a different, less telling, conversation.

What he found

Most participants named a hypothesis in the neighbourhood of even numbers, or numbers increasing by two, and then proposed triples that the hypothesis already allowed. They received a string of conformities, grew confident, and announced the narrow rule. The announcement was wrong. The triples had been obedient to a guess rather than addressed to the question of what else the experimenter's rule might permit. Few people, in the early stages, offered a sequence that would have embarrassed the guess: an ascending triple that was not evenly spaced, or a triple that broke the pattern outright. The no, when it eventually came, often came late, after the wrong rule had already been treated as discovered.

Wason's title is his interpretation, and the interpretation fits the behaviour. The failure is a failure to eliminate. People were not unable to think of other sequences. They were organised around proving the idea in hand, and the task rewarded that organisation with an unbroken series of yeses until they stepped outside it. Confirmation was not a feeling added at the end. It was the search policy.

How Wason read the result

He read the result as a fact about conceptual thinking, not as a trick with the number six. Intelligent people, set a problem whose solution requires them to risk a hypothesis, prefer tests that their hypothesis will pass. The preference is costly exactly when the first hypothesis is too narrow, which is a common shape for a first hypothesis. A rule such as increasing by two is salient, memorable and consistent with the seed triple. It is also smaller than the truth. Only an instance that fits the truth and violates the guess can reveal the difference. A policy that refuses to generate such an instance cannot, in principle, find the rule, however many yeses it collects.

The paper is correspondingly stern about what counts as a test. A conforming triple is information only if it was capable of failing. Once a person has decided to propose only conforming triples, the experimenter's yes is a courtesy, not evidence. Wason's hope, typical of the period, was that the habit could be seen and therefore corrected, that a person shown the structure would start to eliminate. The paper itself is the showing. It is not a training programme, and it does not claim that a single demonstration cures the policy.

Limits

The task is strange on purpose. Rules about number triples, with an experimenter who already knows the answer, are not the way hypotheses are tested in a laboratory or a newsroom. People may have assumed that the rule would be elegant in the way 2, 4, 6 is elegant, because the seed looked like a puzzle from a magazine rather than like a deliberately loose net. If the presentation suggested a narrow rule, some of the confirming tests were obedience to a conversational hint. That possibility qualifies the drama of the result. It does not remove the structural point. Even under a hint, an eliminating test was available, cheap, and rarely used.

The paper also predates the distinction, drawn later, between a positive test strategy and a motivated defence of a belief. Wason's participants had no stake in even numbers. They were not protecting an identity. They were using a search policy that happens to confirm. Nickerson's review, on this card, is careful about that difference. Confirmation bias in 1960, on this evidence, is a habit of testing what you expect to be true. It is not yet the whole modern catalogue of wishful interpretation, selective memory and tribal argument. Those guises are real. This experiment isolates one of them.

What the task is not

It is not the selection task. In the selection task a person is shown a conditional and four cards, and must decide which cards could falsify the conditional. Failure there is often a failure to look for the combination the rule forbids. The family resemblance is genuine, and it is a resemblance, not an identity. Quoting 2, 4, 6 as if it were the card-turning problem, or the reverse, mixes two demonstrations and loses both. Mercier and Sperber's later claim that groups solve the selection task better than individuals is a claim about that other problem. It is part of the card's larger story. It is not a finding of the 1960 paper.

Nor does the paper show that people can never falsify. Some participants did propose awkward triples, and people who were told their announced rule was wrong sometimes widened it. The result is a strong tendency, in the conditions Wason set, to announce a narrow rule on the back of confirming instances. Tendency is the right size of claim. A universal law of the mind would have been a larger conclusion than one conceptual task can hold.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on confirmation bias begins its history here, with a triple and a rule that was simply ascending order. The demonstration earns its place because the bias has nothing to hide behind: no ideology, no ambiguity of evidence, no reward for being right about the narrow rule. People still tested the guess they hoped was the answer. Nickerson's 1998 review is the map of how many other guises that policy wears, in science, law, medicine and ordinary belief. Mercier and Sperber ask why a mind would be built to argue this way, and they answer that reasoning is for argument with other people, where someone else can supply the eliminating test one fails to supply alone. Read Wason for the original failure, in its simplest costume. The rest of the card is what was done with the failure afterwards.
