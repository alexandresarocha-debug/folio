Peter Wason sat 29 psychology undergraduates at University College London, 17 men and 12 women, in front of three numbers. He told them that 2, 4, 6 fitted a rule, and that they should discover the rule by offering other triples. After each triple he said only whether it fitted. The rule was any three numbers in increasing order. People mostly guessed something narrower, often numbers rising by two, and then offered further triples that already fitted the guess. Six of the 29 announced the right rule the first time. Thirteen announced one wrong rule first. Nine announced two or more wrong rules. One person announced no rule at all. Wason read the nine who kept missing as unable, or unwilling, to test the idea they already held. A session could run to 45 minutes.

The design makes a string of yeses easy to collect. Every triple that rises by two also rises. Someone who tries 8, 10, 12 and then 20, 22, 24 hears that both fit, and never learns that 1, 2, 3 fits as well. The experimenter's yes tells you something only when the triple was capable of coming back no. Wason's point was about scientific thinking: a confirming instance does not prove a rule, and a single contrary instance can kill it. His students had that contrary instance available, at no cost, and most of them did not use it until a wrong announcement had already been made.

The same habit shows up whenever a plan, a diagnosis, or a product idea is "tested" with examples the idea already allows. A useful check is a case the idea forbids: a customer who should have bought and did not, a patient with the symptom and not the disease, a week in which the new schedule should have failed. If that case is never put on the table, agreement around the table is a long run of yeses. Ask for the example that would force a change of mind, and ask for it before the announcement.

Later work narrowed what the result means. In a 1987 review, Joshua Klayman and Young-Won Ha argued that trying cases your hypothesis says should work is often a sensible search, and that it fails here because Wason's true rule is much wider than the obvious one. Positive tests of "up by two" cannot fail. The experiment shows that trap. It does not show that people can never look for a counterexample.
