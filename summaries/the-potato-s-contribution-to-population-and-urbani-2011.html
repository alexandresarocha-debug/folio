The question the stories leave open

Historians have long suspected that the potato did something enormous after it left the Andes. William Langer argued that, within Europe, the spread of potato culture and the rapid growth of population went together. William McNeill gave the plant a still larger role in the rise of European power. Observers outside Europe noticed the same pairing, from Nepal to Japan. None of that is a measurement. Places that adopted the potato may have been places that were already going to grow, for reasons of commerce, peace, or medicine, and the potato may have been a passenger. Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian’s 2011 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics is an attempt to separate the passenger from the engine. They ask how much of the rise in Old World population and urbanisation between 1700 and 1900 can be attributed to the introduction of the potato, using a feature of the plant’s biology that people in 1700 did not choose: the suitability of their land for growing it.

The Old World, as in their companion essay, means the Eastern Hemisphere. The potato is a good instrument for the question because its agronomy is unusually favourable to people. It yields more calories and a wider set of nutrients from a given area than the staple grains it supplemented, and a diet of potatoes plus a little milk or butter can keep a person alive through a winter that would defeat a wheat shortage. It also arrived late and spread unevenly. If the crop mattered, populations in places where it can be grown should have diverged from populations in places where it cannot, and that divergence should appear after introduction, not before. That is the shape of a historical experiment, which is the phrase in the paper’s subtitle. Nature assigned the soils. History assigned the date.

The design

The design is a difference in differences, built from two kinds of variation. The first is geographic. Regions differ in how suitable their climate and land are for potato cultivation, a suitability the authors take from agro-climatic data rather than from the later decision to plant. Suitability is not the same thing as adoption. It is a capacity that was sitting in the ground before any European had seen a tuber. The second kind of variation is temporal. Before the Columbian Exchange the capacity was useless, because the plant was not there. After the exchange it became valuable, and more valuable where the land was more suitable. The interaction of the two is the treatment. A highly suitable country in 1400 is a control for itself. A highly suitable country in 1800 is a place where the new crop could change the calorie constraint. Countries with poor potato land help to absorb whatever general forces, from trade to public health, were raising populations everywhere.

The outcomes are population and urbanisation, observed across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the period in which the potato moved from a botanical curiosity to a field crop in much of Europe and beyond. Urbanisation matters because a crop that frees labour from the search for calories, or that supports a larger rural surplus, should show up in cities as well as in headcounts. Nunn and Qian do not stop at country totals. They look inside countries, at city populations, to see whether the same pattern appears when national institutions are held more nearly constant. They also look at adult heights, a measure of net nutrition in childhood, on the view that a genuine improvement in the food supply should appear in bodies and not only in birth rates. Heights are a check on the mechanism. A population rise that was only a statistical artefact of borders or of census quality would not make people taller.

What they find

The paper’s headline, stated as a conservative reading of their estimates rather than as the largest number they can produce, is that the introduction of the potato accounts for approximately one quarter of the growth in Old World population between 1700 and 1900, and approximately one quarter of the growth in urbanisation over the same two centuries. That is a contribution, not the whole increase. The world was also gaining from trade, from other American crops, from changes in disease and child care, and from the slow unfolding of commercial agriculture. The claim is that if one removes the potato’s piece, a large slice of the demographic modernisation of the Eastern Hemisphere goes with it. The within-country evidence on city populations lines up with the cross-country pattern, which makes it harder to argue that the result is an accident of which nations had which laws. The height evidence lines up as well: where conditions favoured the new crop, adult stature is consistent with better net nutrition. Bodies corroborate censuses.

The authors treat the quarter as a lower-bound style summary, and they are explicit that baseline specifications can attribute a little more of urbanisation growth to the crop than of population growth. The number a careful reader should carry away is the one they are willing to call conservative: about a quarter of both. Two features of the result do a lot of the persuasive work. First, suitability does not predict unusual population growth in the centuries before the potato could have been the cause. Pre-trends of that kind are the usual way a difference-in-differences design fails, and the paper’s claim is that this particular failure is not what the data show. Second, the logic does not require that every suitable acre was planted, or that Ireland stand in for the world. Ireland is the famous case, and the famine of the 1840s is the famous warning about what a potato-dependent society looks like when the crop fails. The identification here is broader. It uses the whole map of suitability, including places where the potato was one crop among several.

How to read the mechanism

The mechanism they defend is agricultural and straightforward, which is a virtue in a paper that could have been tempted by a dozen stories. More calories from a given parcel of land raise the number of people that land can support. They also change the cost of feeding a city. Urbanisation in this period is not yet the industrial urbanisation of the late nineteenth century everywhere, but a crop that increases rural surplus makes towns more feasible, and towns then pull further demographic change behind them. Heights matter because population growth can be a sign of misery, of people living shorter and worse lives at higher density, or a sign of a loosened constraint. A nutrition measure that moves in the same direction as the headcount is evidence for the second reading. The potato, on this account, was not merely associated with more people. It helped produce people who had been better fed as children.

There is a historical subtlety the design handles better than a narrative case study can. Adoption of the potato was itself a choice, influenced by landlords, wars, custom, and the price of grain. If the authors had compared places that planted potatoes with places that refused them, they would have been comparing choices, and the choosers differ in many ways. Suitability is upstream of the choice. It does not guarantee that a suitable region adopted the crop early or fully. It does say where adoption was agronomically cheap and where it was agronomically hopeless. The effect they estimate is therefore closer to the effect of having the option than to the effect of a government potato policy. That is the right quantity for a question about the Columbian Exchange, which delivered the option whether or not a particular village took it at once.

What the design cannot do

A historical experiment is not a laboratory experiment. Suitability for potatoes is correlated with suitability for other crops, with ruggedness, with latitude, and with the ways states happened to expand. Nunn and Qian’s answer is the usual economist’s answer: control for what can be measured, compare the same places with themselves before and after, and show that the potato-suitability relationship is a post-introduction fact. A sceptic can still worry about other New World crops whose suitability maps are not identical but are not independent either, and about changes in institutions that might have favoured temperate zones in the same centuries. The paper’s comparative statics are more convincing for the potato as a calorie shock than for any claim that institutions did not matter. They are estimating a contribution holding a great deal else equal, in a world where not much else was equal.

Two further limits are worth stating without drama. The result is about population and urbanisation, and about heights as a nutritional check. It is not a full account of welfare. A larger, slightly taller population can still be a population of tenants and labourers, and the Irish famine sits inside the same century as the estimates, a reminder that a crop which raises carrying capacity can also create a new point of catastrophic failure. The paper is also silent, by design, on the American side of the exchange. It does not measure the demographic catastrophe among indigenous peoples that made the Atlantic world of plantations possible. It measures one of the Eastern Hemisphere’s gains. Reading the quarter as a verdict on 1492 as a whole would be a misuse. It is a verdict on one plant, in one hemisphere, over two centuries.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on the Columbian Exchange reports this result in a single sentence, and the sentence is the one the authors themselves mark as conservative: about a quarter of Old World population growth and urbanisation from 1700 to 1900. The card needs the number because Crosby’s 1972 book could show that American crops mattered and could not say how much. This paper is the “how much” for the crop with the clearest claim. It also explains why the card pairs the book with later economics rather than only with more narrative history.
