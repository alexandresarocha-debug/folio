Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian treat the potato as a historical experiment. The plant was domesticated in the Andes and was unknown in the Old World before Columbus. It did not become a widespread field crop until the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, so the authors take the years after 1700 as the period when its effects should show up. They do not compare countries that chose to plant potatoes with countries that chose not to. They compare countries by how much of their land was suitable for the crop, using a suitability measure from the Food and Agriculture Organization, and they watch population and city growth in suitable places pull away from unsuitable places after 1700. Country and year effects absorb permanent differences, such as institutions that change slowly, and worldwide changes, such as better sanitation.

On their baseline estimates, the introduction of the potato accounts for about 25 to 26 percent of the increase in Old World population between 1700 and 1900, and about 27 to 34 percent of the increase in urbanization. Urbanization here means the share of people living in cities of 40,000 or more. Their most conservative reading is about a quarter of the growth in both. A second test looks inside countries. Cities in more suitable locations grew faster. In French villages fully suitable for the potato, they estimate that adoption raised average adult height by about half an inch. Height is their check that the population increase was not just more people living worse. The result holds when they control for suitability for wheat and rice, for ruggedness, elevation, and the tropics, and when they look within continents, so it is not only a disguised story of Europe's rise.

The practical meaning is that a single crop can move the size of a society when it yields more calories per acre than the grains it supplements, and when it can be planted on land the grains were resting. Famine becomes less regular. Cities become easier to feed. The same dependence creates a new risk, which Ireland learned in the 1840s, but that risk is not what this paper measures. What it measures is the gain, from 1700 to 1900, in the places whose soil could take the plant.

The design has a soft spot the authors state themselves. Much of the world's potato-suitable land is in Europe, and Europe diverged from the rest of the world after 1700 for reasons besides potatoes. If some other European advantage lined up with potato suitability, the estimates would give the tuber credit it did not earn. Their controls and the within-continent results are the answer they offer. They are not a proof that no such advantage remains.

Source. Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian, "The Potato's Contribution to Population and Urbanization: Evidence from a Historical Experiment," Quarterly Journal of Economics 126, no. 2 (2011): 593–650. https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjr009
