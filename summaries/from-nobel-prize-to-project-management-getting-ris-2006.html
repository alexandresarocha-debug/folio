Background

The planning fallacy had a Nobel attached to it before it had a standard tool for a transport ministry. Kahneman and Tversky had described the inside view, the habit of forecasting a project from a story about its own future, and the outside view, the habit of starting from how similar projects turned out. Buehler, Griffin and Ross had shown the habit in students who missed their own thesis dates. Bent Flyvbjerg's 2006 paper, From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right, is the translation of that psychology into a procedure for large projects. It appeared in the Project Management Journal. Folio's card points to it for the practical form of the remedy: reference-class forecasting, used on the kinds of schemes that overrun in public.

The paper is an argument about method, illustrated by the record of megaprojects, rather than a new laboratory study. Bridges, rail lines, Olympic Games and large information-technology systems are the examples the card names, and they are the right scale. At that scale a missed date is not a private embarrassment. It is a budget, a political promise and a risk imposed on people who did not write the plan.

The question

How should a planner estimate the cost, the duration and the risk of a project when the inside view is known to be flattering? Flyvbjerg's question is operational. It is not enough to warn that optimism exists. A ministry that has been warned can still approve a forecast built from a hopeful schedule, because the warning did not replace the schedule. He asks what document should replace it. The answer he defends has a name from Kahneman's work, reference-class forecasting. Identify a class of projects already finished, take the distribution of their outcomes, locate the proposed project in that distribution, and only then adjust for any specific reason this one truly differs. The paper asks why that sequence is so rarely the sequence in the official case for a scheme, and what the record looks like when it is not.

How the argument is set up

Flyvbjerg begins from the psychology rather than from engineering. A project team in the middle of a design is the ideal producer of an inside view. The drawings are particular, the obstacles are imagined one by one, and each obstacle has an owner who intends to defeat it. The resulting estimate is a story about intentions. Reference-class forecasting refuses to open with that story. It opens with a class: other bridges, other rail lines, other games, other large public systems built under political deadlines. The class has a distribution. Some members came in close to their budgets. The great majority, in the record Flyvbjerg has spent his career assembling, ran over budget or over schedule, often dramatically. A new project, before anyone has explained why it is special, should be presumed to sit in that distribution rather than at the flattering edge of it.

Only after the distribution is on the table does the inside view get a hearing, and then as an adjustment, not as a replacement. A specific, evidenced reason to expect a better outcome can shift the forecast along the distribution. A general confidence that this team is serious cannot. The paper's procedure is a rule about the order of operations. Outside first, inside second, and the inside view admitted only to the extent that it carries information the class does not already contain. Flyvbjerg also separates two reasons the official number so often sits at the flattering edge. One is the planning fallacy itself, a sincere underestimate. The other is strategic misrepresentation, an estimate bent because a truthful estimate would not survive the competition for approval. Getting risks right means designing a procedure that is robust to both.

What the paper claims

The claim about the record is the one the card states, and it is deliberately coarse. The great majority of megaprojects overrun, often by a margin that would have changed the decision to build. The claim is not that every scheme fails, or that engineering is theatre. It is that the typical forecast used to justify a scheme is not an unbiased preview of the typical outcome. If it were, overruns and underruns would scatter. The scatter, in Flyvbjerg's account, is lopsided. Costs and durations come out worse than promised far more often than they come out better. A decision procedure that treats the promise as the expected value is a procedure that has already accepted the inside view.

The claim about the remedy is that reference-class forecasting is the outside view made into paperwork. It does not require the planner to feel less enthusiastic. It requires the forecast to begin from a table of finished projects rather than from a narrative of this one. Where governments have started to demand that table, the paper argues, the approved budgets move towards the historical distribution and away from the bid that was designed to win. The psychological mechanism and the political mechanism are thereby handled by the same instrument. A sincere optimist and a strategic lowballer both dislike a procedure that will not open their spreadsheet until the class has spoken. That dislike is, for Flyvbjerg, evidence that the procedure is aimed at the right target.

How to read the link to the Nobel

The title is a claim about transmission. Ideas that Kahneman carried into the Nobel lecture, the inside view and the outside view, are not automatically ideas a project office knows how to use. Transmission fails in a characteristic way. The lecture is applauded, the phrase planning fallacy enters the slide deck, and the next estimate is still a bottom-up sum of hopeful activity durations. Flyvbjerg's paper is an attempt to make the transmission mechanical. A reference class is a set of projects one could name. A distribution is a set of outcomes one could tabulate. An adjustment is a reason one could defend in front of someone who has seen the table. None of these steps depends on the planner achieving a moment of humility. The Nobel is in the paper as the authority for the psychology. The contribution is the sequence that makes the psychology hard to applaud and then ignore.

Read against Buehler, Griffin and Ross, the paper is an increase in scale and a change of cast. Students underestimate theses because the plan crowds out the record. Promoters underestimate viaducts for the same reason, and also because the audience for the estimate, a funder choosing among rivals, rewards the crowding-out. Flyvbjerg's addition of strategic misrepresentation does not cancel the cognitive account. It says that in public projects the cognitive account is not the whole of the risk. A procedure aimed only at sincerity will be gamed by insincerity. Reference-class forecasting is his candidate for a procedure that does not need to decide which of the two it is facing.

Limits

A reference class is a judgement, and the paper's remedy inherits that judgement. Choose a class of unusually doomed projects and the forecast becomes a counsel of despair. Choose a class of unusually lucky ones, or a class defined so narrowly that the only member is the project at hand, and the outside view collapses back into the inside view wearing a table. The method is only as honest as the class. Flyvbjerg's defence is that the class should be wide enough to include the boring failures, and fixed before the preferred answer is known. That defence is a professional norm. It is not a mathematical guarantee, and a walkthrough should not pretend a hostile analyst cannot shop for a class.

The paper is also an advocate's paper. It argues from a record Flyvbjerg has been assembling for years, and a reader who wants the project-by-project tables will need the empirical papers that sit behind this one. The 2006 article is the bridge from the psychology to the management prescription. It is not, by itself, the database. Treating a single review essay as if it were the last word on every rail line ever built would overread it. The card's use of the paper is the right use. It is the account of how the outside view is supposed to be practised when the project is large and the incentives are wrong.

What the argument leaves open

Reference-class forecasting tells a planner where a project sits in the historical cloud. It does not tell a society whether to build. A project that will overrun and is still worth building is a possible outcome of an honest forecast, just as a project that looks cheap and is not worth building is a possible outcome of a dishonest one. Flyvbjerg's target is the forecast, not the appetite for public works. Readers who take the paper as a general hostility to large projects are reading a moral it does not need. The moral it does need is narrower. If you are going to decide, decide on the distribution you would have believed before this week's rendering arrived.

The later book with Dan Gardner, How Big Things Get Done, pushes the same research towards a wider set of habits: slow planning, fast delivery, modular designs that have a record because they have been repeated. The 2006 paper is the narrower instrument. It says, of risk, get the class before you get the story. The book asks what else, besides the forecast, has to change if the class is consistently grim. They are sequential, and they should be read that way.

Why it matters here

The planning-fallacy card would be a curiosity about students if it ended with the thesis study. Flyvbjerg is the reason the card can talk about bridges, rail, Olympic Games and information systems without changing the subject. The mechanism is still the inside view. The setting is a setting in which the inside view is rewarded. Reference-class forecasting is the outside view turned into a step that a funder can demand. Read Buehler, Griffin and Ross for the experiments that isolated the fallacy. Read this paper for the procedure that takes the fallacy personally, as a risk to be estimated rather than as a moral to be admitted. The book that follows is what the procedure looks like when it is taught as a craft.
