Bent Flyvbjerg's paper opens with a record that had not improved. In the transportation projects he and his colleagues had counted, 258 of them worth about 90 billion dollars, rail came in 44.7 percent over budget on average, bridges and tunnels 33.8 percent over, and roads 20.4 percent over, all in constant prices. Across roughly 70 years of cost data, the forecasts had not gotten more accurate. Demand was off too. Rail passenger forecasts were short by 51.4 percent on average, and 84 percent of rail projects were wrong by more than 20 percent in one direction or the other. Road traffic forecasts were off by 9.5 percent on average, and half of them were wrong by more than 20 percent. Thirty years of demand data showed no improvement either.

He uses Daniel Kahneman's account of a curriculum project for Israeli high schools to show the inside view at work. Everyone on the team wrote down how many months the work would take. The answers ran from 18 to 30 months. Asked about similar projects at a similar stage, the curriculum expert on the team said that about 40 percent of those teams had eventually given up, and that he could not think of one that finished in less than seven years or more than ten. He did not think the present team was any better equipped. The group went ahead. They finished eight years later, and the curriculum was rarely used.

The remedy in the paper is reference class forecasting, which the UK Department for Transport and the Treasury began requiring for large transport schemes in 2004. You place the project in the distribution of finished projects of the same kind, and you add the overrun that distribution implies. A road team that wanted the risk of a cost overrun kept under 20 percent would add 32 percent, so a 100 million pound estimate becomes 132 million. A rail team willing to accept a 50 percent chance of going over would add 40 percent. The first recorded use was Edinburgh Tram Line 2, in October 2004. On a base of 255 million pounds, the figure with an 80 percent chance of staying inside the budget came out at 400 million, and the reviewer called the promoter's estimate of about 320 million optimistic.

A budget that has never been set next to finished projects of the same type is a story about how this one will go. Those stories have been short for decades, for rail especially. The uplift is the bill for what went wrong last time. Flyvbjerg also warns that a reserve can be spent because it is there, so the uplift has to sit with a contract that still rewards coming in under it.
