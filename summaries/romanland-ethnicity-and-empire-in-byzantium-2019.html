In 968 Liudprand, the Lombard bishop of Cremona, arrived in Constantinople as the envoy of the German king Otto I. He was received by the emperor Nikephoros II Phokas, and he wrote up the visit as a catalogue of insults. One of them was a name. The pope had addressed Nikephoros as emperor of the Greeks. Liudprand told the emperor that this was meant as a courtesy: Constantinople had been founded by Romans, but its people had changed their language, their customs, and their dress, so "Roman" might give offense. Anthony Kaldellis, in Romanland: Ethnicity and Empire in Byzantium (Harvard University Press, 2019), reads the episode the other way around. Liudprand knew perfectly well what these people called themselves. They called themselves Romans. He was pretending to be surprised, and the reasons he gave, language, customs, and dress, are the reasons a person gives when he is talking about a people, not about a costume.

Kaldellis's second exhibit is a word modern books usually omit. The medieval Greek sources call the country Romania, Romanland, and they call its inhabitants Romans, Romaioi. Arabic writers called the empire Rum. The word "Byzantine" is a later scholarly label, taken from Byzantion, the old name of the city, and fixed in European usage by works such as Hieronymus Wolf's Corpus Historiae Byzantinae of 1557. Kaldellis argues that the people inside the empire were a premodern nation: a shared language, a shared name, a homeland, and a state they understood as theirs. The Roman name was not, on this account, an empty imperial slogan pasted over a random mix of peoples.

The argument changes the sentence a person is allowed to write. "The Byzantine empire was Greek, and only nostalgic about Rome" is a western description, and Liudprand is one of the people who helped build it. "The medieval Roman state was still there, and its subjects said so" is what the Greek sources say. Which sentence is used decides whether 1453 is the fall of a curious eastern kingdom or the end of a Roman polity that had never accepted the western relabeling.

Ioannis Stouraitis, in the Byzantinische Zeitschrift in 2014, had already argued the contrary. Roman identity, in his reading, was the language of a small elite in Constantinople, a homogenizing slogan of government, not a nation shared by provincial farmers. A review of Kaldellis's book made the same caution in another form: even a text written in the provinces was still written by an educated man, close to the literary habits of the capital. The sources call a great many people Romans. How far down the social scale that name was someone's own word for himself is the part still argued.
