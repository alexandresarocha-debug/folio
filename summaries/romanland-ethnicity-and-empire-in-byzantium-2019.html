In 968 Liudprand, bishop of Cremona, arrived in Constantinople as an envoy of the German emperor Otto I. The pope had addressed the emperor Nikephoros II Phokas as emperor of the Greeks, and Nikephoros was angry. Liudprand's report pretends the pope had been trying to flatter him. The city had been founded by Romans, Liudprand says he explained, but its people had changed their language, their customs, and their dress, so the name Roman might give offense. Anthony Kaldellis, in Romanland (Harvard University Press, 2019), reads the scene as a slip. Liudprand knew what these people called themselves. They called themselves Romans. A western visitor who wanted to slight them called them Greeks, and then had to invent a reason.

Kaldellis asks who used the name inside the empire. The evidence he stacks is not limited to the court. He cites passages in which soldiers, peasants, icon painters, freedmen, and provincial landowners are called Romans, and passages in which the country itself is called Romania. Arab and Latin observers used the same name for them. The modern word Byzantine does a different job. No emperor styled himself a Byzantine. The label was built by later scholars, and it makes a Roman majority look like an exotic successor people. Kaldellis's thesis is that the medieval empire was a Roman polity with a Roman ethnic majority, plus minorities, among them Slavs, Bulgarians, Armenians, Jews, and Muslims, whom the sources themselves distinguish from the Romans.

The caption changes the question. Under the name Byzantine, "Roman" can look like a costume worn by a Greek-speaking elite. Under the name the sources use, the slow making of new Romans, the absorption of outsiders, is the process a historian has to explain, and the western refusal to grant the name looks like a political act. Critics answer that a shared political name is not yet an ethnic nation, and that Armenian and Slavic communities inside the empire stayed distinct for centuries. Kaldellis's use of Liudprand is his reply in miniature. Even a hostile outsider argued from language, dress, and custom. That is an argument about a people.
