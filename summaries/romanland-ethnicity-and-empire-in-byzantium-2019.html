The people modern books call Byzantines called themselves Romans. Their state was the empire of the Romans, and their country was Romania. Anthony Kaldellis's Romanland (2019) treats the refusal of that name as a decision with a history. Western medieval politics had reasons to keep "Roman" for the papacy and for a Latin emperor, and early modern scholarship inherited the refusal. Hieronymus Wolf's corpus of Greek histories, published in 1557, helped fix the learned label Byzantine, taken from Byzantion, the older name of Constantinople. Once the subjects carried a different name from the one in their own sentences, "Roman" could be filed as court costume.

Kaldellis argues that the word was an ethnicity. In the historians and the legal texts he reads, Roman is the ordinary name of the in-group: a shared homeland, a shared law, and a Greek speech that came to be called romaïka. Armenians, Slavs, and Latins appear as other peoples, able to serve the empire and still marked as other. He traces the making of this Roman people in part to 212, when free inhabitants of the empire were granted citizenship. Joining was possible. He gives a chapter to Armenians who entered the army and the high command. A door in, on his argument, leaves the people standing. The sentence he wants back is the one on his page 17: the Byzantines were Romans.

The name settles a date. Read as a multinational brand with no Roman people inside it, the empire that the Ottomans took in 1453 is something other than Rome, and Rome has already ended with the western court in 476. Read as the sources speak, 476 ended that court, and a Roman people lasted until the city fell in 1453. Anger in those sources at the loss of land, including the Latin sack of Constantinople in 1204, reads as the loss of a country. A theory of a wandering imperial logo has a harder time with the grief.

Reviewers have kept the sources and fought the word nation. A 2021 review in Studies in Late Antiquity calls the book powerful and still withholds the last word, because the argument turns on how wide "ethnicity" is allowed to be. Armenians, Slavs, and others could live in the empire under a Roman political name and still be described, in the same sources, as peoples of their own. The working rule is simpler than the word nation. Translate the name they used. Then explain why a thousand years of that name should be set aside.
