They called themselves Romans

Anthony Kaldellis's Romanland: Ethnicity and Empire in Byzantium, published in 2019, is an argument about a name. The people whom modern books call Byzantines called themselves Romans. Their state was the empire of the Romans, their land was a Roman land, and their Greek language did not, in their own view, cancel the Roman name. Kaldellis thinks the modern habit of refusing them that name is not a neutral convenience. It is the residue of a western decision, formed in the later Middle Ages and fixed in early modern scholarship, to keep the word Roman for the papacy, the Latin tradition, and eventually a western European story, and to give the eastern empire a different label so that it could be discussed as something else. His opening claim is that the label has distorted the history. If you start from the premise that there were no Romans in medieval Anatolia and the Balkans, only a multinational empire with a vague imperial brand, you will misread the sources, which are full of Romans.

The book is both a philological protest and a theory of premodern ethnicity. Kaldellis argues that Roman identity in this society was not a hollow title claimed by a court and ignored by everyone else. It was an ethnicity, a shared sense of peoplehood, carried by language, law, religion, custom, and the belief that outsiders were other. He knows that ethnicity is a word historians fight over, and he spends the early chapters saying what he is not claiming. He is not claiming a modern nation-state. He is claiming that the ancient and medieval world had peoples, and that the Romans of the east were one of them.

How the name was taken away

The historiographical case comes first because the evidence cannot be heard until the vocabulary is repaired. Western medieval writers had political reasons to deny that the emperor in Constantinople was the emperor of the Romans. The Frankish and German imperial title, and the papal theory that went with it, worked better if the eastern claimant was a Greek. After 1453 the scholarly tradition inherited the denial. The sixteenth-century editor Hieronymus Wolf helped to fix the word Byzantine, drawn from the old name of the city, as the learned label for a body of Greek histories. The label was useful for librarians and fatal for interpretation. It suggested a civilisation distinct from Rome, available to be admired as exotic or despised as stagnant, and it licensed historians to translate the sources' Roman as if the word were a curious relic rather than a self-description.

Kaldellis traces the effects into modern Byzantine studies. A field that calls its subjects Byzantines by default is predisposed to theories in which identity is flexible, layered, and mostly elite, a matter of court titles and Orthodox faith, with no ethnic core. He thinks those theories were attractive partly because they matched the renamed subject. A multinational empire of many peoples under a Christian emperor is a familiar model, and it saves the historian from saying the awkward sentence: this was a Roman people, and it lasted a thousand years after the date when textbooks say Rome fell. The book asks for the awkward sentence back.

What the sources call the people

The empirical core is a reading of how writers inside the society used ethnos, genos, and Roman. Kaldellis draws on historians and essayists across the middle Byzantine and later centuries, among them Michael Psellos, Michael Attaleiates, Anna Komnene, and Niketas Choniates, and on legal and rhetorical texts in which Roman is the ordinary word for the in-group. These writers distinguish Romans from foreigners. They notice Armenians, Slavs, Latins, and others as peoples who may serve the empire, live in it, or fight it, without thereby becoming the definition of it. They describe a Roman homeland, a Roman way of fighting, a Roman law, and a Roman past that includes the ancient state and the conversion to Christianity as chapters of one people's history. Kaldellis's claim is that this usage is too consistent, and too little confined to the palace, to be dismissed as official cant.

Religion is the alternative he most wants to demote. A common scholarly move is to say that Byzantine identity was Orthodox Christianity, so that Roman was a religious-imperial adjective. Kaldellis argues that the sources do not collapse the two. They know of Christians who are not Romans, and they know of Romans in contexts where the relevant contrast is ethnic and political rather than doctrinal. Conversion did not, in his reading, make a foreigner a Roman overnight, any more than speaking some Greek did. There was a core, and there was a process of joining it, and the existence of a process does not mean there was no core. Empire, on this view, is not the opposite of ethnicity. The Roman empire of the Middle Ages was an empire that had a Roman ethnic majority and ruling people, and that also ruled others.

A premodern nation, stated carefully

The theoretical chapters place this evidence against the modern dogma that nations are recent. Kaldellis engages the tradition, associated with historians of nationalism, which treats the nation as a product of print, industry, and the nineteenth century, and which warns medievalists not to project it backwards. He accepts the warning against anachronism and rejects the conclusion that premodern people had only dynasties, faiths, and localities. The Romans of the east, he argues, had a collective name, a shared vernacular culture, a historical memory, a legal order, and a sharp sense of the foreigner. If that combination is not allowed to count as a people, the word people has been defined so that only modern cases survive. He is willing to say nation, with the historical content he has specified, rather than hide behind identity, a word he thinks has been used to avoid the evidence.

The geographical argument follows. Romanland is his way of saying that there was a country, not just a capital and a wandering court. Asia Minor, for long stretches of this history, was the reservoir of soldiers, taxpayers, and Roman speakers. Losses of territory were felt as losses of Roman land and Roman people, not as the shedding of interchangeable imperial possessions. The catastrophes of the late eleventh century, when Turkish settlement transformed much of Anatolia after Manzikert, and of 1204, when a Latin army took the city, are read as national disasters in this sense. A theory that sees only a cosmopolitan empire has trouble explaining the grief and the anger in the sources. A theory of a Roman people has less trouble.

What specialists have disputed

The book has been argued with, which is a sign that it shifted the terms. Some reviewers think Kaldellis flattens a society that really did contain durable Armenian, Slavic, Syriac, and other communities, and that Roman could function as a political umbrella over people who would not have recognised his ethnic core. Others think the legal and literary sources he privileges are still elite sources, even when they speak of the people, and that village identity may have been more local and more religious than the histories imply. There is a separate disagreement about strategy. Historians who already called the state the eastern Roman empire, and who already avoided the decadence cliché, sometimes feel that the attack on a caricature of Byzantine studies is too broad. The conceptual claim can be right even where the polemic is harsh.

None of the criticism returns the field to the old comfort. After this book it is harder to write as if Byzantine were the subjects' own primary name, and harder to treat Roman as a piece of antiquarian flattery. The positive theory, that ethnicity was thick and national in something like his sense, remains a position rather than a consensus. It is a position with the sources quoted, which is more than the older evasion could say. A reader can reject the word nation and still accept the practical rule Kaldellis wants: translate them as they spoke, and then explain, if you can, why a thousand years of self-description should be set aside.

Why it matters here

The card byzantium-survival says that the people we call Byzantines called themselves Romans, and that their empire lasted until 1453. It names this book for the further claim that historians should take that Roman identity at face value and call the state what its people called it. Romanland is the argument behind that sentence. Herrin's book, which the same card uses as its portrait of Byzantine institutions, shows why the survival was impressive. Kaldellis shows what the survivors thought they were. Together they stop the card from becoming a mere correction of a date, 476 rather than 1453. The correction of the date depends on a correction of the name. Folio uses Romanland so that the Rome which did not fall is not only a capital and a bureaucracy, but a people who knew themselves as Roman and were written out of the name by a later scholarly tradition.
