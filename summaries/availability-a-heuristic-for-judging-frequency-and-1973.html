Background

Frequency and probability look, in a textbook, like quantities a careful person can tally. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman had already begun to argue that ordinary judgement does not work like that. People substitute a simpler question for the hard one. In 1973, in Cognitive Psychology, they set out the substitution they called availability. Asked how frequent or how likely a class of events is, people consult the ease with which examples come to mind, and they treat that ease as the answer.

The shortcut is often a good one. Common things usually are easier to recall, so availability tracks the world much of the time. It fails when ease is moved by something other than frequency: the way memory is organised, the ease of picturing a scene, or the strength of an association. The 1973 article is the experimental statement, and it is the paper Folio's availability card is built on.

The question

Do people judge frequency by sampling memory and counting, or by how fluent the sampling feels? If fluency is the cue, which features of a class, besides its true size, will make it feel large? Tversky and Kahneman named several sources of bias. Instances may be easy to retrieve for reasons that have nothing to do with how many of them there are. The search one happens to run may suit one partition of a class and miss another. A scene never stored may still be easy or hard to imagine. Two ideas that are already associated may seem to have occurred together more often than they did. Each source is a different way for ease to part company with the statistic being asked for.

How the studies were set up

One line of studies asked people both to produce examples and to guess how many they could produce. In a word-construction task, people faced small grids of letters, estimated how many words they could build, and then tried. A class that is easy to build in the mind should be judged larger than an equally large class that is awkward to build.

A second line attacked the search itself. People were asked, for several consonants, whether the letter is more often the first letter of an English word or the third, and they estimated the ratio of the two frequencies. The letters included K, L, N, R and V. The design works because the truth is not what intuition suggests. These letters are more common in the third position. Searching by first letter is simply easier, because words are filed in the mind from the left. If people answer a frequency question by running the easy search, they will get the language backwards.

A third line held the recent past fixed and varied fame. Listeners heard recorded lists of thirty-nine names. In every list, fame and number pointed in opposite directions: nineteen famous people of one sex, and twenty less famous people of the other. Some listeners then wrote down every name they could recall. Others only judged whether the list had contained more men or more women. If frequency is a count of what was stored, the slightly larger group should win. If frequency is availability, the famous group should feel larger, because famous names are easier to retrieve.

A fourth line left lists behind and asked for imagination. From ten people, the number of possible committees of two equals the number of committees of eight, because every pair leaves a complementary group of eight. A similar arithmetic governs paths through a diagram of routes. Nothing has been experienced. The person has to construct instances. Tversky and Kahneman predicted that the structures easier to construct, small committees rather than large ones, would be judged more numerous even when the arithmetic says they are not.

The last line concerned co-occurrence. People overestimate how often two events appeared together when the pairing is distinctive or the items are already associated. The authors treated this illusory correlation as availability: associated pairs come to mind more easily, so they feel more frequent. One study presented pairs of personality traits, some naturally related and some not, in a design that did not actually give the related pairs a higher rate of joint occurrence. If association inflates judged co-occurrence, the related traits will seem to have kept company more than they did.

What they found

The letter task followed the search-set prediction. Of 152 people, 105 judged the first position more likely for a majority of the letters, and 47 judged the third position more likely. Each of the five letters was judged, by a majority, to be more common at the start of a word, and the median estimated ratio was two to one in favour of the first position. All five letters are in fact more common in the third position. The error is a specific confusion between an easy search and a frequency in text.

The name lists separated recall from judged frequency and showed the same ordering in both. People recalled on average 12.3 of the 19 famous names and 8.4 of the 20 less famous names. Among the 99 people who only judged which sex had been more numerous, 80 wrongly chose the sex with the famous names. The class that was easier to remember was the class that felt larger, even though it was the smaller class.

Imagination and co-occurrence completed the pattern. Committees and paths that were easier to construct were treated as more numerous than equally numerous structures that were harder to picture. Related trait pairs were overestimated as joint occurrences relative to unrelated pairs. Across these tasks, people did not behave like counters. They behaved like people who had noticed how readily an example arrived, and who had reported that readiness as a magnitude.

How the authors read the results

Tversky and Kahneman did not treat availability as a defect to be scolded out of people. Frequency, probability, and even the size of a set one has never seen are hard questions. Ease is a question the mind can answer quickly, and it is correlated with the hard question often enough to be useful. The paper's contribution is the catalogue of conditions under which the correlation breaks: when memory's filing system does not match the partition the question requires, when a scene is simple to build, and when an association makes one pairing loom.

The judge need not be aware of the substitution. People do not announce that they are using ease as a stand-in for frequency. They feel, directly, that more words begin with K, or that more women were on the list. The experience of ease is not labelled as a cue. It is lived as a fact about the world. That is why the bias is hard to notice from the inside, and why a warning to be careful is a weak remedy compared with changing the question, or supplying a count the person does not have to construct.

Limits

The studies are short problems, mostly given to students, about words, names and committees rather than the risks people fear. The step from a letter grid to floods and news is an inference. The designs also do not measure ease independently of the judgement in every study. Sometimes ease is inferred from the fact that the predicted bias appeared. Where recall was measured separately, as with the famous names, the link is tighter. Where it was not, another feature of the class may have done the work.

Ease is also not the only path by which a class grows in the mind. Repetition, emotion and a story told with a face all change what is available, and they are not one mechanism. The paper groups them because they share a final feeling that examples are ready. Later work had to pull that feeling apart. A large set of examples that is difficult to retrieve can make a class feel small. The 1973 paper predicts that result. It does not run the experiment that separates difficulty from the number of examples recalled.

Replications and critiques

The letter result became the textbook illustration, and later attempts to repeat it have not always reproduced the bias. That is a reason to treat this one demonstration as famous rather than as settled for every wording. The broader claim has worn better. Judged frequency does track how readily examples come to mind, and salience, recency and ease of picturing do shift judged frequency and judged risk. The famous-name pattern, a more memorable subset judged to be the larger one, has the same logic and depends less on a quirk of English spelling.

The important refinement came in 1991, when Norbert Schwarz and colleagues pulled the feeling of ease apart from the number of items retrieved. People who had just listed many examples of their own assertive behaviour sometimes judged themselves less assertive, because the listing had felt hard. That finding does not cancel Tversky and Kahneman. It specifies the cue. Availability, on the later reading, is not the count of what you managed to recall. It is the fluency of the recall. A different caution is political. Used carelessly, availability can slide into the claim that public fear is only a failure to recall the right statistics. Paul Slovic's work on risk perception, which the card pairs with this paper, is the correction. Lay judgements also track dread, catastrophe and unfairness, qualities a death count does not capture.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Availability: what comes to mind feels more common, is a reading of this paper and of the moves that followed it. The letter demonstration, the inflation of vivid causes of death, and the advice to ask for a base rate rather than for a story all sit on the 1973 claim that ease is being used as a frequency. Schwarz on ease of retrieval, Kuran and Sunstein on cascades, and Slovic on the qualities of risk are the card's further reading because each takes a different slice of the same idea: the feeling of effort, the social amplification of whatever is currently easy to imagine, and the map of hazards that results. Read this paper for the original procedure and the experiments that fixed its shape.
