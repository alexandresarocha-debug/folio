Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked people a question that sounds like a trivia item and is really a test of memory. Take the letter K. In a typical English text, is K more often the first letter of a word or the third? Words that start with K come to mind easily. Words with K in the third spot do not. The count goes the other way: a typical text has about twice as many words with K third as with K first. The same is true of L, N, R, and V.

They asked 152 people about all five letters. One hundred five judged that the first position was more common for a majority of the letters. Forty-seven judged that the third position was. For every one of the five letters, a majority said "first," and the median guess of the ratio was 2 to 1. Paying people for accuracy, in a further version of the task, did not remove the bias.

A second study used fame instead of spelling. People heard a recorded list of 39 names, one every two seconds. Some lists had 19 famous women and 20 less famous men. The others had 19 famous men and 20 less famous women. The famous class was always the smaller one. Of 99 people asked which sex appeared more often, 80 picked the famous class. A separate group of 86 people tried to recall the names and remembered, on average, 12.3 of the 19 famous names and 8.4 of the 20 less famous ones. The names that came back easily were the names that felt more numerous.

The same shortcut runs through news and ordinary worry. A crash, a rare disease, or a crime you can picture will feel more common than a routine cause of harm you cannot call up a case of. In a shop, a brand you have just seen advertised feels like the popular one. The feeling of ease is being used as a count.

Later work split that feeling from the count. In 1991 Norbert Schwarz and colleagues found that people who had to list 12 examples of their own assertive behavior, a hard task, rated themselves as less assertive than people who listed only 6. And in 1998 Peter Sedlmeier, Ralph Hertwig, and Gerd Gigerenzer asked 114 students in Salzburg to judge German letter positions and found that the estimates tracked the real frequencies, which cuts against treating the 1973 letter result as a general law of how frequency is judged.
