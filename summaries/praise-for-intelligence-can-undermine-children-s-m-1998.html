Praise as a theory, delivered in a sentence

Praise for intelligence can undermine children's motivation and performance, published by Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck in 1998, is the experiment behind the most practical warning in the mindset literature. Adults praise children in the hope of encouraging them. Mueller and Dweck argue that the content of the praise teaches a theory of ability, and that one very common content, the attribution of success to intelligence, teaches the theory that later makes children brittle. The paper is a series of studies, not a single demonstration, because the claim is about a pattern of consequences: the goals children choose, the way they explain failure, the pleasure they take in hard work, the honesty of their reports, and the level of their subsequent performance. A result on any one of those would be a curiosity. A result on all of them, after one sentence of praise, is an argument about what that sentence means.

The sentence is delivered at a carefully chosen moment. Children work on a set of problems and do well. They have just received evidence of success, and an adult names the success. For some children the adult names it as intelligence: the child must be smart at this. For others the adult names it as effort: the child must have worked hard. A further group, in some of the studies, receives praise that does not press either attribution. The problems are then followed by a harder experience, arranged so that the children do poorly, and by measures of what they want next and how they perform next. The design separates the warmth of being praised from the theory the praise contains. Everyone in the two main groups has been praised. What differs is the implied account of why the first problems went well.

What intelligence praise teaches

Intelligence praise, on Mueller and Dweck's reading, tells the child that the performance revealed a trait. Traits of that kind are generally taken to be stable. If success revealed intelligence, failure will reveal its absence, and a difficult task is a risk to the trait rather than an opportunity to practise. The rational response, once a child has accepted the theory, is to prefer tasks that will display the trait again, to treat effort as unnecessary for someone truly able, and to find a failure deeply unwelcome because it revises the flattering measurement. The praise feels like a gift at the moment it is given. Its cost appears when the work stops going well, which is exactly the moment at which encouragement was supposed to help.

Effort praise teaches a different theory from the same platform of success. The adult names a cause the child can reproduce. Working hard, choosing a strategy, staying with a problem, are actions, and actions can be repeated after a failure without first having to defend a reputation. The child is not being told that ability is unlimited, and is not being told that outcomes do not matter. The child is being told that the cause of the success just observed is the sort of cause that failure does not permanently revoke. Mueller and Dweck's prediction is that this difference in theory will show up before it shows up in any speech about mindsets. Children will choose different next tasks, explain a setback differently, and score differently on a later set of problems. The theory is visible in the behaviour.

The consequences the studies record

After the praise, children were offered choices that distinguish a learning goal from a performance goal. They could take on something challenging, from which they might learn and on which they might look less impressive, or something safer, on which they could show what they already knew how to do. Children praised for intelligence were more likely to avoid the challenge. Children praised for effort were more likely to seek it. The adult's sentence had not mentioned goals. It had named a cause, and the cause implied a goal. If intelligence is what was praised, the next task is an occasion to confirm it. If effort is what was praised, the next task is an occasion to apply it, and a harder occasion is a better one.

The studies then put the children into difficulty. On a set of problems constructed so that they would fail, the two groups did not respond alike. Children praised for intelligence enjoyed the hard set less, were more likely to explain the failure as a matter of ability, and persisted less productively. Children praised for effort were more likely to treat the failure as a matter of effort and to stay oriented towards the work. When a further set of problems was given, comparable to the first success, the intelligence-praised children performed worse than the effort-praised children. The undermining in the paper's title is literal. Relative to a form of praise that was equally positive and equally brief, praise for being smart depressed later performance. The encouragement did not merely fail to help after the setback. The particular encouragement made the setback more damaging.

One further result shows how far the protection of a trait can go. When children praised for intelligence were asked to report their scores, they were more likely to misrepresent them. The paper treats this not as a mysterious collapse of morals but as a continuation of the same logic. If the score is a measurement of a valued trait, and the measurement has come out badly, a reported score is another performance, and it can be edited. Effort-praised children, for whom the score measures a process rather than a self, have less reason to edit it. The dishonest report is evidence about the meaning of the praise, not a separate sermon about lying.

Why the finding is about theory and not about kindness

It is tempting to read the paper as a scolding of adults who praise children, or as a preference for sternness. That reading misses the comparison the studies actually make. The effort-praised children were praised. They were told something approving about a real success. The argument is not that approval is dangerous. The argument is that approval names a cause, and the cause becomes the child's working theory of what the success was made of. A theory in which success is made of a fixed trait makes failure informative in a threatening way. A theory in which success is made of effort makes failure informative in a way that still leaves an action available. Kindness that names the trait is the condition under which motivation and performance were undermined. Kindness that names the process is the condition under which they were not.

The paper also begins to show why later, vaguer advice about praising effort can go wrong without contradicting these results. In the studies, effort is a true and relevant cause of a success the child has just had, and it is named specifically. Praise for effort in the abstract, offered when the child is in fact using a bad strategy, or offered as consolation rather than as information, is a different sentence. Dweck's later warning about a false growth mindset is a warning about that different sentence. Mueller and Dweck's evidence is about the contrast between two attributions after a genuine success, followed through into challenge, failure, reporting and later performance. It supports a careful claim. It does not support the idea that the word effort is a charm.

What the studies leave open

These are experiments with children, on a particular kind of problem, over a short span of time. They show that a sentence can shift goals, explanations and performance in the session and in the next problems. They do not show that one sentence of intelligence praise sets a child's academic future, and they do not estimate how large such effects are when the outcome is a year of grades rather than a laboratory score. That longer, smaller picture is what the later meta-analyses measure, and it is a picture in which mindset effects on achievement look weak on average. There is no inconsistency if a powerful short-run demonstration and a small average field effect are both true. Laboratory control makes a mechanism visible. Classrooms add all the other causes of achievement, and they dilute any one sentence.

The studies also use praise that is unambiguous. Real adults mix attributions, praise talent in one breath and strategy in the next, and are interpreted by children who already have a theory of their own. The paper's contribution is the isolation of the message, not a map of ordinary talk. Isolation is what makes the result attributable. If the intelligence-praised children had also been praised less warmly, or had succeeded less clearly, the later fragility could have been blamed on disappointment. The design gives both groups a success and a compliment, and then shows that the compliment's content is doing the work.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on growth mindset points to this paper when it says that children praised for being smart were more likely to avoid challenges and to perform worse after failure than children praised for effort. That sentence is the popular form of Mueller and Dweck's finding, and it is accurate as far as it goes. The paper underneath the sentence is richer than a tip for parents. It shows a single attribution spreading into task choice, enjoyment, explanation, honesty and subsequent score, which is what you would expect if the attribution installed a theory rather than a mood. The card then follows the theory out into the world, through Dweck's book, through the disappointing average effects in the 2018 meta-analyses, and through the more encouraging targeted effects in the 2019 national trial. This 1998 paper is the beginning of that arc: the demonstration that praise for intelligence can backfire, and the reason anyone thought a belief about ability was worth testing in schools at all.
