Claudia Mueller and Carol Dweck sat 128 fifth graders, ages 10 to 12, down with puzzles from Raven's Progressive Matrices. After four minutes every child was told they had done very well. The experimenter said they had solved at least 80% of the problems they answered, whatever the real score. The actual average was 5.2 out of 10. Forty-one children then heard, "You must be smart at these problems." Forty-one heard, "You must have worked hard at these problems." Forty-six heard nothing further.

Asked what they wanted next, 67% of the children praised for intelligence picked problems that would display ability. Ninety-two percent of the children praised for effort picked "problems that I'll learn a lot from, even if I won't look so smart." The control group split evenly. Then came a harder set, and every child was told they had done a lot worse, solving no more than half of the problems they answered. On a third set of ordinary difficulty, scores fell by 0.92 problems in the intelligence group and rose by 1.21 in the effort group. The control group moved by 0.13. After the failure, children praised for intelligence also wanted to keep going less and said they enjoyed the problems less.

A third study, with 88 fifth graders, asked the children to tell unfamiliar peers how many problems they had solved. Thirty-eight percent of those praised for intelligence stated a higher number than they had earned. Thirteen percent of the effort group did so, and 14% of the control group. If the score is a reading of a fixed trait, a bad score is worth editing.

These are one-session experiments, and the shifts inside them are large. They measure the next set of puzzles, not a year of school. When Victoria Sisk and colleagues averaged the link between a growth mindset and academic achievement in 2018, across 273 estimates and 365,915 people, the correlation was r = .10. Lessons meant to teach a growth mindset, 43 estimates and 57,155 people, averaged d = 0.08 on achievement. A sentence of praise can move a child for the puzzles that follow. On grades, the average in the studies they reviewed is small.
