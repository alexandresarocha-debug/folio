Around AD 625 an East Anglian king was buried in a ship at Sutton Hoo with gold shoulder-clasps set with garnet, and also with a pottery bottle. Bryan Ward-Perkins asks the reader to look at the bottle. In seventh-century Britain it was a high-status object, brought in from abroad, because it had been shaped on a wheel at a time when potters in Britain were making vessels by hand. Set in any ordinary Roman farmhouse of the fourth century, the same bottle would have been unremarkable, and noticeable mainly for its rough fabric. The potter's wheel disappeared from Britain in the early fifth century and was not used there again for almost three hundred years.

The same direction shows up in the animals. Ward-Perkins cites measurements of leg bones from 21 Iron Age sites, 67 Roman sites, and 49 early medieval sites. Cattle grew larger in the Roman centuries, when specialized pasture and winter fodder could put weight on them, and they shrank back toward prehistoric size afterward. On his own excavation at the port of Luna in northern Italy, sherds of third- and fourth-century African tableware were everywhere, while sixth-century pieces were rare enough to be exciting. By the seventh century the standard pot of northern Italy was a plain cooking vessel, where a Roman kitchen had held plates, bowls, jugs, lids, and amphorae. African workshops still exported in the sixth century, but the pots reached fewer places and, increasingly, only richer households.

What this means in a household is simple. Roman production had made decent manufactured goods ordinary, well down the social scale, because sea routes, a single tax system, and a large army kept distant workshops employed. When that demand broke, the skill and the product went with it. A bishop could still write a learned letter in a town where the roofs were no longer tiled and the market no longer received African bowls. The letter is real. So is the missing bowl.

Britain is the steepest case, and Ward-Perkins says so. In parts of Italy and the Rhineland, plain wheel-turned kitchen wares continued. Chris Wickham's Framing the Early Middle Ages, published in the same year, 2005, described an early medieval economy with sharper differences from region to region, and with more surviving exchange in some districts than a single phrase, "the end of civilization," suggests. The western drop in mass-produced goods is widely accepted. How far it measures hunger, rather than the loss of a particular industry, still has to be argued site by site. Wood and leather leave less behind than pottery does.
