In Roman Britain, households well below the villa class used wheel-thrown pottery, coin, and tiled roofs. Bryan Ward-Perkins's The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization (Oxford University Press, 2005) follows those things into the fifth century and finds the industries gone. Quarries go quiet. There is no sign that anyone is still mixing mortar or firing brick and tile. New buildings, in areas under Anglo-Saxon kings and in areas still British, are timber or dry stone, with roofs of wood or thatch. The loss lasted. Late in the seventh century the Northumbrian abbot Benedict Biscop wanted churches at Monkwearmouth and Jarrow built in the old Roman way, and he sent to Gaul for masons and glaziers. Bede, who recorded the work, describes the glaziers as practitioners of a craft unknown in Britain at the time. The churches that survive are small beside a Roman basilica. A skill that had been ordinary had become an import.

The dishes tell the same story on a wider map. African red-slip pottery, made in North Africa, shows up on ordinary rural sites around the western Mediterranean in the Roman period and then thins out. At Rome, rubbish dumped at the Crypta Balbi in the sixth and seventh centuries still contains fine imported pottery and amphorae. Ward-Perkins reads that dump as the waste of a rich monastery, not of a whole city. Excavation in the countryside just north of Rome found imported tableware in both modest and grand houses of the imperial centuries, and almost none from the sixth and seventh. A common good had become a luxury. In the east the curve runs the other way. Through the fifth and sixth centuries, tableware from Cyprus and Phocaea, and amphorae of eastern wine and oil, remain abundant even at humble rural sites.

The practical point is about a specialized economy. Roman households could buy a decent pot, a roof tile, and a jar of oil because other regions made them in bulk and the sea lanes were safe enough to move them. When the western state could no longer tax, pay, and protect that network, producers lost their buyers and buyers lost the goods. Chris Wickham's Framing the Early Middle Ages, published in the same year, answers that the west was a patchwork, and that some countrysides and some aristocracies kept more exchange than one collapse story allows. The eastern abundance is part of that answer. A missing pot is not a happiness index. It is evidence that ordinary purchases had changed, and a history of the fifth century that cannot explain the change is missing what households would have noticed.
