A protest against a soothing word

Bryan Ward-Perkins's The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization, published in 2005, is a short, polemical book by an archaeologist and historian who thinks that a generation of scholarship talked itself out of a fact. The fact, as he sees it, is that in much of the western Roman world the fifth and sixth centuries brought a sharp fall in the complexity and comfort of material life. The soothing word is transformation, the organising idea of the late-antique scholarship associated with Peter Brown and with a large European research effort on the post-Roman kingdoms. Ward-Perkins does not deny that those kingdoms inherited Roman law, Latin, and Christian institutions. He denies that this inheritance is the main thing to say about the period if, at the same time, ordinary people stopped being able to obtain goods that Roman production and Roman security had made ordinary.

The opening claim is methodological. Texts written by elites talk about identity, theology, and the legitimacy of kings. Pots, tiles, coins, and animal bones talk about what households used. Ward-Perkins trusts the second archive when the question is standard of living. He argues that the late twentieth-century preference for continuity was partly a moral preference. After a century of European war and of decolonisation, historians were uneasy with stories in which barbarians ruined a civilisation. They redescribed the end of the western empire as a change of management. The redescribing, he thinks, required them to look away from the rubbish pits.

What Roman material life looked like

The book begins by insisting on how unusual the Roman economic system was. In the early and middle imperial centuries, regions specialised. North Africa and other favoured zones produced surplus grain, oil, and wine. Workshops turned out tablewares in quantities large enough that decent pottery reached not only villas but modest rural sites. Roof tiles, glass, metal tools, and a reliable small-change coinage circulated widely. Transport by sea and river, protected by a single political order and paid for in part by the needs of the annona and the army, made specialisation rational. A farmer could concentrate on one product because other goods could be bought, and because a buyer somewhere else could be reached without each district having to be self-sufficient.

Ward-Perkins asks the reader to notice how far down the social scale this reached. The comparison he cares about is not between a senator and a medieval baron. It is between a Roman rural household and a post-Roman one. If African red-slip bowls, or their regional equivalents, show up in ordinary western settlements, then the integration of the economy is not a fact only about the rich. If those wares disappear, the disappearance is not a change in elite taste. It is a change in what ordinary people could get. He uses the same test for building materials. Stone and tile give way, in many districts, to wood, earth, and thatch. The change can be described neutrally as a new architectural tradition. He describes it as a loss of competence and of surplus, because the older materials required quarries, kilns, transport, and craftsmen that a poorer and more local world could not keep employed.

The fifth century as an economic unravelling

The central case is that this system was fragile precisely because it was specialised. It depended on a state that could tax, on roads and sea lanes that were safe enough, and on markets large enough to support full-time producers. When the western imperial structure broke under war, usurpation, and the settlement of armed groups, the demand that had held the network together shrank. Frontier armies were no longer paid from a central treasury in the old way. The African tax base passed to the Vandals. Long-distance orders for pottery and foodstuffs fell. Producers who had specialised found no buyers. Skills that had been maintained by constant production were practised less often and, in some places, were lost. The economy did not step down one gentle grade. In Ward-Perkins's image, a complex machine came apart, and people were thrown back on local, cruder, less productive ways of making things.

Britain is his sharpest example, and he is open about its being an extreme. Roman Britain's material culture, including its pottery industries and its coin use, does not taper smoothly into a medieval future. It drops away. Buildings cease to be replaced in the Roman manner. The archaeological signature of the early post-Roman centuries is, in his account, poorer in manufactured goods than the Roman period and, in some respects, poorer than the late pre-Roman Iron Age that Rome had conquered. He uses that comparison to block a comforting thought: that the post-Roman west merely returned to a normal European simplicity which Rome had briefly and artificially exceeded. If the post-Roman level falls below the pre-Roman level, something has been destroyed that was not only imperial froth.

He extends the argument beyond Britain without pretending that every province is Britain. In Italy, Gaul, and Spain the trajectories differ, and some regions retain more Roman technique for longer. The western Mediterranean as a whole, he argues, still shows the same direction of travel. Mass-produced tableware becomes rare. New building in the old materials becomes exceptional. Coinage, especially the small bronze that greased everyday exchange, thins out. Literacy, measured roughly by inscriptions and by the casual writing that a commercial society produces, contracts. Cattle diminish in size, which he reads as a sign of worse husbandry and worse diet rather than as a cultural preference for smaller animals. None of these proxies is perfect. Together they are his answer to the claim that the end of the western empire was mainly a change in political vocabulary.

What he is not saying

Ward-Perkins is sometimes summarised as if he thought culture stopped and barbarism began. The book is more precise than that. He accepts that the successor kingdoms were Roman in important ways. They used Roman law, Roman administrative words, and the Roman church. Their elites could be sophisticated. A Vandal or a Goth who issued Latin legislation was not a cartoon invader. The point of the pottery is not to unmask such people as philistines. It is to separate the survival of elite and ecclesiastical culture from the survival of a mass material civilisation. Both can be true. A bishop can write a learned letter in a town where the houses are no longer roofed with tile and the market no longer receives African tableware.

He also distinguishes his case from Gibbon's. Gibbon explained decline largely through civic virtue, the army, and Christianity. Ward-Perkins explains a fall in welfare through the breakdown of an economic network whose political support had failed. Christianity is not his villain. Barbarian identity is not his villain either. War and the loss of the imperial framework are the triggers, and economic complexity is the casualty. In that sense he is closer to a modern argument about fragile supply chains than to an eighteenth-century argument about effeminacy. A specialised economy is efficient while the links hold and exposed when they snap. People do not choose a lower standard of living as a cultural experiment. They arrive at it because the old way of getting goods has ceased to function.

Where the argument can be pressed

Specialists who disagree with him rarely dispute that the western ceramic landscape changes. They dispute the leap from that change to a single story called the end of civilisation. Chris Wickham and others have argued for a more regionally varied early Middle Ages, in which some countrysides simplified while some elites and some exchange networks remained substantial. Parts of the eastern Mediterranean did not follow the western curve at all, a contrast Ward-Perkins himself uses, since the survival of complexity in the east shows that there was nothing inevitable about the western drop. Critics also note that absence of a Roman-style pot is not a direct measure of happiness, nutrition, or social order. A society can eat well from wood and leather and leave less for an archaeologist to find. Animal bone, pollen, and settlement archaeology have to be read site by site, and a polemical book necessarily flattens some of that variation.

There is a further risk in the rhetoric of civilisation. The word does moral work that the data do not quite authorise. A fall in the quality of tableware and roofing is a fall in complexity and, very probably, in welfare. It is not, by itself, a demonstration that post-Roman people were less humanly accomplished, or that their political arrangements were contemptible. Ward-Perkins is usually careful. The title is not. Readers should keep the care and treat the title as a provocation aimed at the transformation school. The durable core is the empirical one. Whatever one calls the period, the western material record changes in a way that a story of smooth cultural succession does not predict. Any account of the end of the western empire that cannot explain the pots has omitted something that households would have noticed even if chroniclers did not.

Why it matters here

The card ward-perkins-pottery is built on this book. Its claim that economies of specialisation are efficient and fragile, and that historians should look at what ordinary households owned, is Ward-Perkins's claim in plain language. The card gibbon-decline lists him as the modern writer who restores a real fall without simply repeating Gibbon's moral causes. The card brown-late-antiquity lists him as the counterattack on the idea that late antiquity was only creative transformation. Read against those two cards, the book is the moment where the argument stops being a choice between an Enlightenment moralist and a cultural historian and becomes a dispute about evidence. Texts and shrines show one kind of continuity. Kilns, coins, and roof tiles show a discontinuity that, in much of the west, was severe. Folio's use of the book is to keep that discontinuity visible whenever a later card is tempted to treat the fifth century as a mere change of style.
