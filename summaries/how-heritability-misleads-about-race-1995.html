Ned Block, a philosopher then at MIT, wrote this paper in 1995 as a reply to The Bell Curve, a bestseller that used the heritability of IQ to argue about group differences. Its lasting value is a very clear account of what heritability is and what it is not.

Block starts by separating two ideas. Genetic determination is the everyday notion: a trait is coded in the genes and develops in any normal environment. Heritability is a technical fraction: the genetically caused variation in a trait divided by the total variation, within a particular population. Genetic determination can apply to one person. Heritability only makes sense for a group of people who differ. You cannot ask what the heritability of your own IQ is.

His best example is the number of fingers and toes. Genes code for five in almost everyone, and five develop in any normal environment, so the trait is genetically determined. Yet its heritability is almost certainly very low, because most of the differences we see are not genetic. They come from problems in fetal development, such as babies exposed to the drug thalidomide, and from accidents that cost adults a finger. When the genetic variation is small next to the environmental variation, heritability is low, even for a trait the genes control.

The opposite can happen too. Block notes that when only women wore earrings, having an earring was highly heritable, because who wore one tracked a chromosomal difference, XX versus XY. Nobody would call earrings genetically determined. As earrings became common among men, that heritability no doubt fell.

Because heritability is a ratio, it moves when either part moves. Block's thought experiment: give half of a population random brain damage that lowers IQ, and the environmental variation grows, so heritability drops. Raise a million clones in different environments, and the genetic variation is zero, so heritability is zero. With more uniform environments, he writes, heritability goes up. He also cites real data showing that the heritability of IQ rises from childhood into adulthood.

His most famous illustration is borrowed from the geneticist Richard Lewontin. Plant seed corn from one bag in two uniform environments, one with a normal nutrient solution and one with a poor one. Within each group height differences are all genetic, so heritability is 100 percent. But the gap between the groups is entirely environmental. High heritability within groups says nothing about the cause of differences between them.

What this means in real life: when you read that a trait is 80 percent heritable, read it as a statement about differences in a particular population and setting, not about how fixed the trait is in you.

Caution: this is an argument paper, not a new study. Block's examples are thought experiments or rest on figures from other researchers, and his view that heritability is an uninteresting measure is a strong opinion that many geneticists would not share.
