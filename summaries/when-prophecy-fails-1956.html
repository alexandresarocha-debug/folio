Leon Festinger, Henry W. Riecken, and Stanley Schachter spent weeks inside a small Midwestern circle that expected the world to flood. The book, published in 1956, calls the woman at the center Marian Keech and the city Lake City. She is Dorothy Martin, and the messages she wrote down, which she said came from beings she called the Guardians, set the catastrophe for December 21, 1954. A local paper ran the warning: a flood from the Great Lake would swamp the city on that day, and the believers would be taken off by flying saucer. The researchers and a few hired observers joined the group, went to the meetings, and wrote down what people said as the date approached. On the nights the saucers were supposed to come, the inner circle waited in private and kept outsiders away.

The flood did not come, and neither did the saucers. As December 21 wore on, Keech seized on news of earthquakes in Nevada, Italy, and California. She told the group that Lake City had been spared because of the light this little group had shed, and that the disasters elsewhere showed the prediction had been real. The book’s central observation is what happened next. Before the failure, the group had been secretive. Its maxim was that “those who are ready will be sent,” and members were told not to force the belief on anyone. After December 21 they called the press, gave interviews, and on Christmas Eve stood on the sidewalk in front of the house singing carols, under instructions, as they understood them, to invite the public. About two hundred people gathered. No saucer landed. Festinger and his coauthors read the burst of publicity as the group’s way of living with a prophecy that had just failed: if more people could be brought to believe, the sacrifice and the waiting would still make sense.

The everyday version is familiar. A team that has staked a launch date, a congregation that has staked a prediction, or a household that has spent its savings on a sure thing will often explain the miss in a way that keeps the plan alive, and will look for company in that explanation. Social support is the condition the book insists on. People who had given the most, and who were still surrounded by other believers, were the ones who went out to recruit.

That climax is now disputed. A 2026 paper in the Journal of the History of the Behavioral Sciences, using newly unsealed archives, reports that Martin recanted, that the group dissolved, and that proselytizing stopped, and that the group had already been recruiting before the failure. The paper also reports that Henry Riecken later said he had precipitated the climactic events. The book’s night-by-night record is still the document. Its claim that failure produced a new surge of recruiting is the claim the archives are now used to deny.
