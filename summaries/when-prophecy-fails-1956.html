The question the book starts from

When Prophecy Fails, published in 1956 by Leon Festinger, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter, is a field study written to test an idea that did not yet have its laboratory form. The idea is dissonance. People who have committed themselves to a belief suffer when the world clearly contradicts it, and they will work to reduce that suffering. One way, the way a rational caricature expects, is to give up the belief. Another way, the way the book is about, is to strengthen the belief and to seek new believers, so that social support makes the disconfirmation bearable. Folio's card on cognitive dissonance begins here, with a small group whose leader predicted a flood and a rescue that did not come. The book is the story of that night and the theory that made the authors go looking for it.

Five conditions, before the story

Festinger and his colleagues open with the conditions under which they expect disconfirmation to increase fervour rather than to end it. The belief has to be held with deep conviction, and it has to bear on what the person actually does. The believer has to have taken actions that are hard to undo, so that abandoning the belief means admitting that those actions were a waste or a humiliation. The belief has to be specific enough, and sufficiently about the real world, that events can refute it cleanly. A vague hope cannot fail on a Tuesday. There must then be undeniable disconfirmation. And the believer must have other believers around him or her. Social support is the condition the whole study turns on. A person alone with a broken prophecy, they argued, is likely to drift away. A person surrounded by others who have staked as much as he has can be offered a reinterpretation, and the group can set about persuading outsiders, because each new convert is a fresh reason to think the group was right.

These conditions are the part of the book that can be taken away even if one distrusts the story. They are a theory of when a public failure produces proselytising. They explain why the authors needed a group that had already cut ties, spent money, risked ridicule and named a date, and why a group of mild sympathisers would not have been a test. They also explain the ethical and methodological trouble the authors walked into. To watch the social support, they had to be inside it. Observers who join a tiny group become part of the support they are there to measure.

The group and the prophecy

The book uses pseudonyms. A woman in a midwestern city, presented as a housewife, received messages through automatic writing, which she experienced as communications from beings elsewhere, warning of a catastrophe and promising that the faithful would be saved. The catastrophe was a flood, dated, and the rescue was to be by flying saucer. Around her gathered a small circle: people who had found her through a newspaper notice, through prior interests in flying saucers and unconventional religion, and through each other. The authors and a small number of hired observers made contact, presented themselves as interested seekers, and were admitted. For the period before the date they recorded meetings, messages, instructions about what to do with possessions and jobs, and the growing cost of membership. Some believers made preparations that would be painful to reverse. The group was not, for most of this time, a mass movement. It was a living-room circle, which is important, because the theory's mechanism is the face-to-face support of people who can see one another's commitment.

The authors are unusually explicit, for the period, about the rule they tried to follow inside the group. Observers were not to plant the prophecy, not to propose the reinterpretation, and not to lead. They were to write down what was said as soon as they could leave the room. Anyone who has done fieldwork will recognise both the ambition and the leak in it. A new, serious, apparently believing presence changes a group of that size. The book asks to be read with that leak in view, and it remains the most detailed contemporary record of how this particular circle talked itself up to a date and through the failure of the date.

The night it failed, and the morning after

As the hour approached, the circle gathered, followed the instructions they understood the messages to require, and waited for rescue. The hour passed. The writing that was supposed to summon the rescuers did not summon them. Festinger, Riecken and Schachter describe the hours as a study in a prediction dying in public: refreshment of the instructions, checking of the clock, attempts to find a procedural mistake that would save the prophecy without abandoning it, and a rising discomfort that matches the dissonance they had postulated. The disconfirmation was as clean as a social event can be. The flood did not come. The saucers did not come. People who had sat up all night waiting for them were still in a living room in the morning.

The theoretical interest is in the fork after that. A new message, received through the same channel as the old ones, offered a way to understand the non-event as a success. The group's vigil, on this account, had been the point. Their faith had averted the disaster, or had satisfied the beings who had issued the warning. The committed members who were together in the house accepted the reinterpretation. They did not quietly fold the group and go home. They moved, with an energy the authors had not seen in the same way before the failure, to tell the press and the public. Phone calls, statements, an urgency about spreading the explanation: this is the increased proselytising the theory predicted. Conviction, having been expensive and having been refuted, was repaired by making other people share it. Meanwhile, believers who had been less embedded, or who faced the same news away from the circle, were more likely to drop away. The comparison the authors need, between social support and isolation, is rough and naturalistic, and it runs in the predicted direction.

What kind of evidence this is

The book is not an experiment. There is no control group of prophecies, no random assignment to social support, no measurement beyond careful qualitative record and the authors' judgement of what counted as increased fervour. The group is tiny. The observers were inside the phenomenon. Later critics have argued that the surge of publicity was shorter and less uniform than the textbook retelling, that some members had been eager to publicise the prophecy before the failure, and that the night admits of more ordinary descriptions: embarrassment, a leader producing a face-saving message, followers accepting it because the alternative was a brutal loss of face. Those criticisms are fair comments on a design this soft. They do not invent a different night. Something failed, a reinterpretation was offered, and the people who had been most deeply and mutually committed were the ones who carried the reinterpretation out to the public.

The authors' own conclusion stays close to the five conditions. Where conviction, costly action, a definite prediction, clear failure and a supportive group coincide, disconfirmation can produce more preaching rather than less. Where the supportive group is missing, the same failure is more likely to end the belief. They present the circle as an existence proof of the first path, not as a sample from which rates can be estimated. The theoretical chapter that frames the story is, in that sense, more portable than the story. It became one of the roots of Festinger's 1957 statement of dissonance, and it remains the field illustration the card uses because no laboratory can ethically, or practically, ask people to stake their lives on a date and then watch the date fail.

Why it matters here

Folio's card treats When Prophecy Fails as the fieldwork from which dissonance theory grew. The summary is exact. A small group predicted destruction and rescue, the prediction failed, and committed members who had one another's support became more active in seeking converts, which the theory reads as a way to reduce the discomfort of having been wrong. The five conditions are the part worth keeping in a usable form. Deep belief, irreversible commitment, a prediction that can really fail, failure that cannot be dodged, and a group: remove any one, and the surprising prediction, that failure will increase the preaching, is no longer what the theory expects. That is a sharper claim than "people double down".

The card places the book beside the 1957 theory, the one-dollar experiment, and Tavris and Aronson's later essay on everyday self-justification. This is the member of the set in which the stakes are highest and the method is weakest. A reader who wants a countable result should lean on Festinger and Carlsmith, and should also notice the card's caution that a 2024 multi-lab replication of induced compliance did not find the classic attitude change. A reader who wants to see dissonance as something groups do, at night, with a clock on the wall, has to read this book, leaks and all. The lesson the card takes is not that every failed forecast produces a sect. It is that social support can turn a refutation into a mission, which is a fact about belief that a purely individual psychology of error will miss.
