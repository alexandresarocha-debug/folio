In early 2008, three teams in three countries landed on the same small piece of DNA. One was in Australia. Another, led by Hans Eiberg at the University of Copenhagen, started from a single large Danish family.

By following how eye colour passed down through that family, Eiberg's team narrowed the blue-eye region to 166,000 DNA letters inside the HERC2 gene. Then they compared blue-eyed and brown-eyed people. Two single-letter differences matched eye colour perfectly in their samples. One of them, rs12913832, sits about 21,000 letters before the start of the OCA2 gene, in a stretch of HERC2 that has barely changed over evolution. The brown version is the one shared with many other species.

They then tested the stretch in cells grown in the lab. Linked to the OCA2 starting point, it clearly reduced OCA2 activity, and the blue and brown versions bound different proteins. That fits the idea that the region controls how much pigment OCA2 makes.

The most quoted finding is about ancestry. Around this spot, 155 blue-eyed people from Denmark carried exactly the same pattern of six nearby DNA letters. So did 5 blue-eyed people from Turkey and 2 from Jordan. The authors argue that such a shared pattern points to a single founder: one ancient change, inherited by blue-eyed people today.

The third team, led by Manfred Kayser in Rotterdam, scanned the whole genome of 1,406 Dutch people in three studies, plus 1,292 relatives. The same region on chromosome 15 dominated, and no other region showed consistent evidence. Kayser's team also noted that iris colour was one of the first human traits for which Mendelian inheritance was established, and that a HERC2 letter changed in frequency gradually across 23 European populations, matching their eye colours.

What this means in real life: many blue-eyed people may share one distant ancestor through this single change.

Caution: the perfect match held in their samples, but the larger Australian study found rare exceptions, such as brown eyes in about 1% of people with two blue copies. The founder argument rests on only 7 people from outside Denmark.
