A diagnosis in both directions

Politics and the English Language appeared in Horizon in 1946. George Orwell's essay begins with a commonplace, that English is in a bad way, and then refuses the commonplace explanation. Cultural pessimists, he notes, blame the state of the language on politics and economics and then behave as if nothing can be done. He thinks the influence runs in both directions. Ugly political and economic conditions leave their mark on prose. Ugly prose makes it easier to have foolish thoughts, and foolish thoughts are a political resource. The essay is a pamphlet about that loop, written in the first year of the peace, with the war's official language still ringing and the century's worst official language still being excused.

He proceeds by specimens. Five passages of contemporary prose are set out so a reader can feel the trouble before the categories arrive: a mix of political journalism, scientific popularisation, and academic criticism, chosen because each has a public manner and each, in his view, has stopped choosing words. The exercise is unfair in the way demonstrations often are. The authors are caught on bad days, and Orwell is a hostile editor. The unfairness is useful if you treat the passages as types. What he wants you to see is a shared texture. The sentences are long, the agents disappear, the metaphors are dead, and the vocabulary has been borrowed from a professional air that sounds like thinking.

Four habits

From the specimens he names four habits. The first is dying metaphors. Phrases that once pictured something, a line of battle, a grindstone, a rough ride, are used by people who no longer see the picture and who would be embarrassed if they did. The second is verbal false limbs. Simple verbs are replaced by phrases, often in the passive, with nouns doing the work and pads of the in-connection-with kind filling the joints. The third is pretentious diction. Foreign tags, scientific-sounding words, and adjectives like historic and inevitable dress up a small claim as a large one, or give a brutal claim the air of a process. The fourth is meaningless words. In political writing, he argues, words such as democracy, freedom, justice, and patriotic often do little more than signal approval, while words such as fascist or reactionary signal disgust. The word has been relieved of any discoverable object.

These habits can be mere slackness. Orwell's sharper claim is that they are convenient. A writer who reaches for a prefabricated phrase does not have to decide what he thinks, and a reader who receives one does not have to picture a fact. The essay's examples of political euphemism are specific. Defenceless villages bombed, inhabitants driven out, cattle machine-gunned: the operation can be called pacification. Millions of peasants robbed of their farms and sent walking: transfer of population, or rectification of frontiers. People imprisoned without trial, or shot: elimination of unreliable elements. The point of the list is the gap between the concrete scene and the abstract noun. Euphemism, in this essay, is a device for naming an act while keeping the mental picture from forming.

What he thinks a writer can do

Orwell does not propose a golden age to return to, and he is scornful of the idea that the cure is a theatrical revival of archaic English. He wants concrete language, fresh images, and the discipline of cutting. He offers a small catechism for a scrupulous writer. What am I trying to say. What words will express it. What image will make it clearer. Is that image fresh enough to have an effect. Then two more, which are the editorial pair: could I put it more shortly, and have I said anything avoidably ugly. The catechism is a procedure for making thought visible to the person doing the thinking. If you cannot find the concrete version, you may not yet know what you mean, or you may be hiding it from yourself.

The six rules at the end are the part that travelled into classrooms. Prefer a fresh figure to a metaphor you are used to seeing in print. Prefer the short word to the long one. Cut a word if you can cut it. Prefer the active to the passive. Prefer everyday English to a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a piece of jargon. And break any of these rules sooner than say something outright barbarous. The last rule is the one that saves the list from becoming a new jargon of correctness. Orwell frames the rules as aids against the habits he has just described, useful while the habits are strong, disposable the moment they would force a false sentence. He thinks a private reform of this kind can spread, because prose is imitated, and that waiting for a better society before you will write a cleaner sentence is one more way of doing nothing.

The objections that belong with the essay

The essay has been answered, and the answers are part of reading it well. Orwell breaks his own rules in the act of stating them. The passive voice appears in a polemic against the passive. Long words appear in a polemic against long words. Some of this is the ordinary hypocrisy of a vivid writer. Some of it is evidence that the rules are heuristics with exceptions he has already licensed in rule six, and that he is inconsistent about when the licence applies. Linguists have had a more structural complaint. Geoffrey Pullum and others have argued that the blanket suspicion of the passive, which readers took from Orwell and from Strunk and White, misunderstands a construction English uses for good informational reasons, including the reason Pinker later defended: sometimes the topic of the sentence is the recipient of the action. A rule that makes every passive guilty will make accurate scientific prose look like a vice.

There is a political objection too. Orwell's specimens of bad language are often the language of his opponents on the left and in the bureaucracy, and his ear for cant is better trained on those targets than on the cant of his own side. The method, the demand that a writer picture the act, survives that bias. A sentence that cannot survive a picture is a sentence to suspect, whoever benefits. The essay's enduring claim is the two-way loop, stated without superstition about linguistic decay as a force of nature. Conditions corrupt language. Language returns the favour by making the next corruption easier to think. A writer who accepts the loop has a small, local duty, which is the duty the six rules try to make practical.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on Orwell's rules is a reading of this essay: the four habits, the euphemisms, the six rules, and the insistence that the last rule outranks the other five. The card points to the full text because the specimens and the rules need to be seen in order, and it points onward to Nineteen Eighty-Four because Newspeak is the essay's political worry turned into a state. Read the Horizon essay for the diagnosis and the procedure. The diagnosis is that bad prose and bad politics feed each other. The procedure is a set of questions and a set of rules, ending with permission to break them. That permission is the part a classroom is most likely to drop, and the part Orwell thought he could not drop without becoming the thing he was attacking, a producer of ready-made phrases.
