In "Politics and the English Language," published in Horizon in 1946, George Orwell rewrites a verse from Ecclesiastes to show what dying prose does to a picture. The verse says the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor bread to the wise, "but time and chance happeneth to them all." He counts 49 words and 60 syllables, everyday words, and six vivid images. His modern version begins "Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion" and ends by saying that success in competitive activities is not commensurate with innate capacity. That sentence has 38 words and 90 syllables. Eighteen of the words are from Latin and one from Greek. Race, battle, and bread have become "success or failure in competitive activities."

He then names the political use of the same habit. Defenceless villages are bombarded from the air, the inhabitants driven out, the cattle machine-gunned, the huts set on fire with incendiary bullets: "this is called pacification." Peasants robbed of their farms and sent down the roads are "transfer of population or rectification of frontiers." People imprisoned without trial, or shot in the back of the neck, or left to die of scurvy in Arctic lumber camps, are "elimination of unreliable elements." The phrase exists, he writes, so that a person can name the act without calling up a mental picture of it.

A status report, a press line, or a clinical note does this whenever a concrete act is swapped for a noun with nothing to see. Orwell's six rules are short: prefer the fresh image, the short word, the cut, the active, the everyday English word. The sixth is the one that keeps the others from becoming a new kind of cant. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. He also says that anyone who looks back through his essay will find him committing the faults he has just listed. The cure he offers is a picture first, and the words chosen afterward, one sentence at a time.
