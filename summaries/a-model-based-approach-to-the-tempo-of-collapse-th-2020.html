The common story of Rapa Nui holds that the building of ahu, the stone platforms that carry the moai statues, stopped around AD 1600 after an ecological, cultural and demographic catastrophe. The authors note that this narrative is especially popular outside archaeology, where collapse is often treated as historical fact. The island, about 164 square kilometres, has hundreds of ahu and nearly 1,000 moai.

Robert DiNapoli and colleagues tested the story with a Bayesian model. For 11 ahu sites around the island, they combined radiocarbon dates with the order in which parts of each platform were built and with accounts from early European visitors. This let them estimate when construction started, how fast it went, and when it ended.

The results did not show a collapse before contact. Ahu construction began soon after colonisation and increased rapidly sometime between the early 14th and mid-15th centuries AD. Construction events then continued at a steady rate beyond European contact in 1722.

The authors argue that this supports an emerging view of Rapa Nui's people as resilient communities who kept up their long-term traditions despite the impacts of European arrival, rather than a society that destroyed itself.

What this means in real life: claims that a past society collapsed should be tested with explicit models of timing, not inferred from vague dates, and the same method can be used for other famous collapse stories.

Caution: the model rests on 11 of the island's hundreds of ahu and on radiocarbon dates with wide ranges, and early European accounts are patchy. Construction activity is also only one indicator of how a society was faring.
