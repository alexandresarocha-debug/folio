Background

The Enigma of Reason, published in 2017 by Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber, is the book-length statement of a theory they had put on the table six years earlier. Reasoning, they argue, did not evolve to help a solitary thinker see the truth. It evolved as a tool for social life: to produce reasons that will move other people, and to examine the reasons other people offer. The enigma of the title is the apparent mismatch between that faculty's reputation and its record. Reason is praised as the crown of the mind, and then caught, over and over, confirming what its owner already believes. The book proposes that the mismatch is an artefact of expecting the wrong job. Folio's confirmation-bias card treats the book as the accessible form of the argumentative theory, and the 2011 target article as the version the field argued with in public.

A book is a different instrument from a journal article. It can slow down, retell experiments for a reader who has not met them, and answer objections at the length they deserve. It can also smooth a debate into a story. A walkthrough should keep those two possibilities in view. The ideas are the 2011 ideas, developed. The voice is a book's voice.

The question

Why is reason so flawed if it is for thinking well, and why did it evolve if it is so flawed? Mercier and Sperber call this a double enigma, and their move is to refuse the first half. Reason is flawed as a solitary truth-seeker. It is not flawed as a device for argument, once argument is understood as a normal condition of human life rather than as a debate club. The question the book pursues is what the mind looks like if that refusal is accepted. What is left for intuition? What, exactly, is a reason? When does an exchange of reasons improve a belief, and when does it harden one? The confirmation bias the rest of the card documents is, in this framing, the central clue rather than the central embarrassment.

How the book proceeds

The early chapters separate inference from reasoning. Most of what a mind does with evidence is inference in a broad sense: a conclusion arrives, quickly, with a feeling of obviousness and without a listed justification. Mercier and Sperber treat that arrival as the ordinary case, and as a case that is often right. Reasoning begins when a reason is produced or demanded, a consideration that can be offered to someone else as support. Reasons are social objects. They have to be portable. A private hunch that cannot be said is not yet a reason in their sense, however accurate the hunch.

From that distinction the book rebuilds the familiar biases. A reasoner asked to think about her own belief searches for considerations that will defend it, because the function of producing reasons is to defend and to persuade. The search looks like confirmation bias from the outside and like doing one's job from the inside. A reasoner handed another person's defence searches for the weakness, because the function of evaluating reasons is to avoid being talked into a falsehood. The same mind is a poor prosecutor of its own ideas and a sharp prosecutor of its neighbour's. The book spends considerable time on the experimental literature that shows this split, including the logical problems individuals fail and groups improve, and on the everyday cases, from science to politics, in which a lone intellect digs in and a quarrel sometimes digs the truth out.

What the book asks the reader to give up

The first surrender is the picture of reason as a general-purpose supervisor sitting above the rest of the mind and correcting it. In Mercier and Sperber's picture the supervisor is a specialist, and most of cognition never passes through its office. Intuitions do the inferring. Reason arrives late, often to justify an intuition already formed, sometimes to yield when another person's objection is too strong to answer. The second surrender is the hope that the way to better beliefs is a private resolution to be fair-minded. The book does not think fairness of that solitary kind is the natural product of the faculty. The natural product is a case. Better beliefs, where they come, come from the collision of cases, under conditions in which people may contradict one another and have a reason to care who is right.

The third surrender is more cheerful. The reader is asked to give up the view that human reasoning is a depressing exception to an otherwise competent animal. If most inference is intuitive and serviceable, and if reasoning is a social adaptation doing a social job, then the biases are local. They are what happens when a social tool is used without its social partner. That is a reinterpretation of the evidence Nickerson surveyed and of the behaviour Wason filmed in miniature, not a denial of it. People really do fail to eliminate their own hypotheses. The book thinks it can say why the failure is the default and why another person in the room changes the default.

How the book handles its own risks

A theory that predicts both bias and its cure has to be watched. Mercier and Sperber try to watch it by stating the conditions. Discussion helps when there is real disagreement, when reasons can be exchanged rather than performed, and when the group has a stake in the right answer rather than in victory. Discussion does not help, and can harm, when everyone begins on the same side, when status punishes dissent, or when the performance of reasons is the goal. The confirmation bias of each member then points the same way, and the evaluative half of the faculty has nothing unfriendly to bite on. The book is at its most useful when it refuses the simple slogan that groups are wiser. Groups are the habitat. Habitat can be polluted.

The evolutionary story is offered as the best available function for a puzzling mechanism, and a reader is entitled to accept the mechanism's description while remaining agnostic about the prehistory. The psychological claims do the work the card needs. People produce reasons for their own side easily. They evaluate other people's reasons strictly. Solitary reflection often rationalises. Joint argument sometimes corrects. Whether that package was assembled by natural selection for argument, or by some other history that left the same package, is a question the experiments do not settle. The book argues for the selection story. It also supplies, more robustly, the description the story is meant to explain.

Limits

The Enigma of Reason is not a new dataset. Anyone who wants the original procedures, the triples, the reviews of guises, the commentaries, has to go back to the papers the book is weaving. A synthesis can make those papers sound more unanimous than they were. Wason's task, Nickerson's catalogue and the group-advantage findings come from different traditions, with different arguments about what is being measured. The book has a right to propose a common function. It does not have the power to end the measurement arguments by housing them in one narrative.

There is a limit of comfort as well. The practical implication can be heard as a reassurance, that one's own one-sidedness is natural and that a good conversation will fix it. The book is more demanding than that reassurance. A good conversation requires a partner who is both competent and licensed to disagree, and it requires a willingness to lose. Many of the settings in which people most want the reassurance, politics, professional identity, a belief that organises a life, are settings in which those conditions fail. The theory predicts entrenchment there. It does not offer a private technique that will substitute for the missing opponent.

What to take from a book that argues for argument

The deepest point is easy to miss because it sounds modest. Reasons are for other people. A consideration that cannot be shown, challenged and either answered or withdrawn is not yet playing the role the faculty exists to play. That is why a list of supporting points, composed alone and never answered, feels like thinking and functions as defence. It is also why a disagreement with someone who is trying to get the same thing right can do what the list cannot. The card's sentence, that reasoning works best in groups that argue in good faith, is this point with the conditions left on. The book is the place to see the conditions argued rather than appended.

Readers coming from the 2011 article will find the thesis intact and the tone more patient. Readers coming from the bias literature will find their favourite effects still standing, with a different caption underneath. The caption is the contribution. Confirmation bias remains ubiquitous. On this account, ubiquity is what one should expect of a social specialist left on its own.

Why it matters here

Folio's card offers Mercier and Sperber as the explanation that turns a catalogue of bias into a claim about what reason is. The Enigma of Reason is that explanation at the length a general reader can use, and it is the companion to the 2011 target article rather than a retreat from it. Wason supplies the simplest picture of a hypothesis that was never put at risk. Nickerson supplies the range, across the institutions that officially exist to put hypotheses at risk. The book supplies the function that makes the range unsurprising, and the condition under which the function turns back towards the truth: an argument with someone who is allowed to win. Read it after the 2011 article if you want the debate, and before it if you want the full narrative the debate was about.
