In The Enigma of Reason, Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber describe an experiment, run with Emmanuel Trouche, Petter Johansson, and Lars Hall, that handed people their own arguments disguised as someone else's. Participants answered five quick problems about a shop. One premise was "none of the apples are organic." They then wrote a reason for each answer, and they were allowed to change the answer. Only 14 percent changed their minds, and a change was as likely to make the answer worse as better. One participant, called Rawan in the book, stuck with "some fruits are not organic," on the ground that an apple is a fruit and none of the apples were organic.

On one problem the experimenters lied about what the person had answered, and presented that person's own reason as the work of someone else. About half the participants did not notice the swap. Of those who were fooled, more than half rejected the argument they had been willing to give a few minutes earlier. They were harder on their own bad reasons than on their own good ones. The same sentence got a stricter grade once it seemed to belong to another person.

A second study in the book, by Lars Hall, Petter Johansson, and Thomas Strandberg, did the trick with opinions. A researcher with a clipboard had people rate moral and political statements, secretly replaced some statements with their opposites, and asked for an explanation. Fewer than half noticed. Most of the rest produced reasons for a view they had not held a minute before, especially when the original opinion had been mild. Reason, in these demonstrations, is a lawyer for the conclusion already in hand, and a much better critic of the other side.

Before you send a justification for a decision you have already made, read it as if a colleague had written it, or hand it to someone who is free to say it does not follow. You will be gentler with your own paragraph than with the same words under another name. That laxness is useful in a real disagreement, where someone else supplies the missing objection. It is a poor private conscience. The book is an argument that this double standard is what reason is for, and the two experiments are the cleanest pictures it gives of the double standard at work.
