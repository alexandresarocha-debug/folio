One day, and the decision to buy the flowers

Mrs Dalloway, published by the Hogarth Press in 1925, takes place on a Wednesday in June in London, a few years after the Great War. Clarissa Dalloway is giving a party that evening. The book opens with her decision to buy the flowers herself, and with her stepping out into the morning at Westminster. That decision is a small domestic fact and the novel's method. From it, Virginia Woolf can follow a mind across a city, because a walk is a stream of perceptions with a destination attached. The air, the sound of a car backfiring in a way that might be a pistol shot, a motor car that may contain a face of state, an aeroplane writing letters in the sky that the people below cannot quite read: the morning is shared by strangers for a minute at a time, and then each stranger's thought goes its own way.

Clarissa is in her early fifties, a hostess, the wife of Richard Dalloway, a Conservative member of parliament, and the mother of Elizabeth. She has just recovered from an illness of the heart. The party is her particular art, the making of a room in which people meet, and she knows both the vanity of the art and its necessity to her. Peter Walsh, the man she did not marry, is back from India and will visit her before lunch. The past is as present as the florist. At Bourton, the country house of her youth, she refused Peter, and she remembers a night when Sally Seton kissed her on the terrace, a moment she still counts among the happiest of her life. Woolf does not tell this as a flashback chapter. It arrives inside the errand, and the errand continues when the memory lets go.

Septimus, and the doctors

The other centre of the day is Septimus Warren Smith, a young man who fought in the war and has not come back whole. He hears the dead, in particular his officer Evans. He sits in Regent's Park with his Italian wife, Rezia, who makes hats and who is trying, with increasing despair, to keep him in a world of ordinary remarks. A sky-writing aeroplane, a terrier, a child's cry, the beauty of the park: Septimus receives them with an intensity that is both visionary and unbearable. Rezia's sections are among the book's most compassionate, because Woolf gives her the practical terror of a person living with someone else's visions, the loneliness of a foreign wife, and the wish for a child and a normal street.

Two doctors preside over his case. Holmes, the general practitioner, tells him nothing is wrong that a hobby and a sense of proportion will not mend, and treats his despair as obstinacy. Sir William Bradshaw, the specialist, is more formidable. He believes in proportion, and in a twin goddess he calls conversion, the pressure that makes the patient fit the doctor's picture of a healthy man. He arranges for Septimus to be taken away to a rest home. Woolf writes Bradshaw with a coldness she grants almost no one else in the book. The portrait carries her knowledge of rest-cures and of the authority medicine can wield over a mind it has decided to normalise. When Holmes arrives at the flat to take him, Septimus throws himself from the window onto the area railings. He experiences the jump as a last possession of his own life, a communication preserved from the men who would correct him.

How the minds are joined

The technique is free indirect style, a narration in the third person that takes on the rhythm and the judgements of whoever is being followed, and can pass to another person in the space of a paragraph. Clarissa's walk gives way to the crowd around the car, then to Septimus and Rezia, then to Peter Walsh waking from a nap on a bench near them, then to Peter's memories of Bourton and of being refused. Richard, later, comes home with roses and fails to say the sentence of love he had prepared, which is its own small tragedy of fluency. Elizabeth walks out with Miss Kilman, the religious, resentful tutor whom Clarissa hates and knows she is wrong to hate. Hugh Whitbread, Lady Bruton, the shopkeepers, a poor woman singing by the Underground: each is given a stretch of the stream and then released.

Big Ben and the other clocks cut the day into hours. The minds between the strikes are not cut. Woolf uses the clocks as the public time James's psychology had set against the private flow, whether or not a reader stops to name James. People hear the same hour and spend it on incompatible thoughts, and the novel's form is the demonstration. This is different work from a single unpunctuated monologue. The book wants the feeling of many streams in one city, touching at the motor car, the sky-writing, the song, the striking clock, and finally at the party. Continuity is real inside a person and fragile between persons. The style honours both facts by gliding and by breaking.

The party, and the death brought into it

Evening gathers everyone who can be gathered. The Prime Minister's arrival gratifies Clarissa and is quietly absurd. Peter comes, still critical, still in love with his own past. Sally Seton arrives as Lady Rosseter, unexpected, large with life, no longer the girl on the terrace. The Bradshaws are late. Sir William mentions, as a piece of dinner-party news, that a young man has killed himself. He does not say the name in a way that connects it to anyone Clarissa knows, and he does not need to. She withdraws to a small room, furious that death has been introduced at her party, and then overtaken by a stranger kinship. The young man had flung his life away while she had gone on caring about flowers and rooms. She feels both judged and understood. The feeling does not become a speech. She returns to her guests.

The novel ends on Peter, who has been waiting and talking with Sally, and who sees Clarissa come back. The last note is his recognition, the plain fact of her presence after a day of memory, jealousy, and half-meetings. Septimus and Clarissa never meet. They are connected by the city, by the doctors' world that touches her party, and by a temperamental likeness Woolf believes in: a susceptibility to beauty and to terror, answered by him with a window and by her with a room full of people. The book refuses to rank the answers cleanly. It gives Septimus the dignity of his refusal and Clarissa the dignity of her return, and it leaves the reader holding both. That double ending is the stream of consciousness used as a moral form. Many minds, one day, no single consciousness allowed to sum the city up.

Why it matters here

The card on stream of consciousness sends you to Mrs Dalloway because it is the clear case of mental flow written mainly as free indirect style, in a book that still has scenes, clocks, and a plot. William James supplies the metaphor and the claim that consciousness is continuous and selective. Joyce, in Ulysses, pushes interior monologue and a sequence of styles to extremes, including Molly Bloom's unpunctuated closing episode. Woolf's novel is the middle art, and the one many readers can feel in a single day of their own: errands, memories, other people's faces, a party, a piece of news that does not belong to you and changes the evening anyway. Read it for the way one Wednesday can hold a hostess and a soldier and refuse to let either life be a digression from the other.
