Mrs Dalloway opens on a Wednesday in June with a woman leaving the house. "Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself." Clarissa is giving a party that evening, and the errand takes her across London, through a morning of cars, shop windows, and people she has known for decades. The same hours belong to Septimus Warren Smith, a soldier who survived the war and still hears his dead friend Evans. His wife Rezia is with him. Dr. Holmes is coming up the stairs of their lodging house to take him away. Septimus looks at a bread knife with the word "Bread" carved in the handle and will not spoil it. The razors have been packed. He sits on the sill of the large window. Holmes reaches the door. "I'll give it you!" he cries, and throws himself down onto Mrs. Filmer's area railings.

That evening the Prime Minister attends Clarissa's party. She hears that a young man has killed himself, goes into a small room alone, and feels the death as something that has broken into her day. Peter Walsh, newly back from India, is among the guests, still opening and shutting his pocket-knife, the man she did not marry. Earlier, Clarissa and Septimus, who never meet, both look up at an airplane writing letters in the sky over the city. The book stays inside this one day and keeps crossing from her errand to his terror, sentence by sentence, without a narrator stepping in to sort the two lives into a plot summary.

The method is what a city feels like when you are in it: other people's thoughts running beside yours, joined only when a siren, a plane, or a piece of news happens to both of you. William James had described consciousness as a stream in which the thunder still contains the silence it broke. Woolf writes that join as a day. A reader comes away with buying flowers and a man's death on the same clock, and with the sense that an ordinary afternoon is already full of other minds.
