A Thursday in Dublin, built as a sequence of arts

Ulysses, published in Paris in 1922 by Shakespeare and Company, takes place on 16 June 1904, a date readers still call Bloomsday. James Joyce builds the day in eighteen episodes, loosely answering the Odyssey: a son looking for a father, a wanderer trying to get home, a woman at the house who has a life of her own. Stephen Dedalus, a young teacher and a grieving son, begins the morning in a tower at Sandycove with Buck Mulligan. Leopold Bloom, an advertising canvasser, begins it in the kitchen at 7 Eccles Street, frying a kidney, talking to the cat, bringing tea to his wife Molly, who stays in bed. They do not properly meet for many hours. The book is the city between them, specified down to streets, pubs, debts, songs, and the time of day.

Calling the whole of Ulysses a stream-of-consciousness novel is a shortcut that hides the design. Early episodes do use interior monologue with unusual closeness. Stephen on Sandymount strand thinks in a language that folds philosophy into what he sees and touches. Bloom's mind, once the book joins him, moves by association through hunger, sex, grief for his dead son Rudy, kindness, and a comic alertness to the bodies of other people. Even in these episodes the narration is a weave, third-person report braided with the character's words. Later episodes set that weave aside and invent a new method for each hour. The day is continuous. The art changes on purpose. Any account of the book as one technique has stopped before the newspaper office.

Through the city, hour by hour

The morning sends Stephen to a school, where he collects his pay and listens to Mr Deasy's advice and to his own sense that history is a nightmare he is trying to awake from. Bloom moves through the post office, the chemist's, and a funeral at Glasnevin for Paddy Dignam, riding in a carriage with men who do not know what he is thinking about death, his father's suicide, and his son. At the newspaper office the book breaks into headlines, rhetoric as wind. At lunch Bloom is repelled by eaters and then comforted, in Davy Byrne's, by a glass of burgundy and a cheese sandwich, and by a memory of kissing Molly on Howth Head, a seedcake passed from her mouth to his. In the National Library Stephen unfolds a theory of Hamlet in which Shakespeare is the ghost-father, not the prince, a theory that is also a young man's boast and a son's grief. The episode of wandering rocks then cuts the city into many short scenes happening at once: a priest's walk, a viceregal cavalcade, Blazes Boylan buying fruit on his way to Molly, a throwaway on the river. Dublin becomes a machine of simultaneous errands.

The afternoon and evening keep raising the stylistic bet. At the Ormond Hotel the prose becomes a musical performance, with an overture of fragments, while Bloom writes a letter to a woman he has been flirting with by post and Boylan's jaunting car leaves for Eccles Street. In Barney Kiernan's pub a spiteful unnamed narrator and a series of gigantic parodies surround the Citizen, a nationalist blowhard, and Bloom, cornered, answers hatred with a fumbling defence of love. On Sandymount strand at dusk, Gerty MacDowell is rendered in the language of the magazines and novels she has absorbed, and Bloom's tired, explicit thoughts take over when that language has done its work. At the maternity hospital, while Mina Purefoy labours, the English of the episode labours too, passing through a sequence of historical styles from early English to contemporary slang. Style itself has become the subject, a gestation of the language the rest of the book has been using.

Night, and three ways of telling it

Nighttown is a play. In the brothel district, stage directions and hallucinations replace the novel's usual prose. Bloom is tried, transformed, humiliated, and tender. Stephen smashes a chandelier at the apparition of his dead mother. Bloom takes responsibility for him, the nearest thing the book offers to the father and son finding each other, and it happens in a theatre of the unconscious. The cabman's shelter episode then writes the exhausted pair in a language of cliché and evasion, a sailor telling stories that do not hold, Bloom trying to talk like a man of the world and landing in received phrases. The choice of a tired style for a tired hour is one of Joyce's moral decisions. He will not give the reunion a lyrical privilege the hour has not earned.

The catechism episode, questions and answers in a mock-scientific voice, walks them to Eccles Street, makes them cocoa, inventories the day, and sets Bloom's situation in the bed beside Molly with a precision that is both comic and heartbreaking. The narrator of this episode seems to know everything and to feel nothing, and the feeling gets in anyway, through the facts: the water that came from the reservoir to the tap, the list of Molly's suitors swelling into legend, the kiss that is almost a kiss, the positions of two bodies. Objectivity, pushed far enough, becomes a way of protecting tenderness. It is as far from an unpunctuated monologue as a method can be, and it is still about what these people know and cannot say.

Molly, awake

The last episode is Molly Bloom's. It is eight enormous sentences, largely unpunctuated, a woman in bed moving among the day's residue and a life's memory. Boylan has been in the house that afternoon. Bloom has asked for breakfast. Molly thinks of both facts, and of Gibraltar, where she grew up as the daughter of Major Tweedy, and of earlier lovers, and of her body, and of Stephen, whom she has not met and might like to meet, and of her daughter Milly, and of the dead. The sentences follow association the way James said thought moves, by fringe and by flight, with almost no resting place marked by a full stop. The episode ends on her memory of saying yes to Bloom on Howth, among the flowers, the day the seedcake passed between them. The yes is a return to the memory Bloom had at lunch, now in her voice, and it closes a book that has spent hundreds of pages refusing to privilege any single voice.

Penelope is the part of Ulysses a reader can fairly call stream of consciousness in the fullest technical sense, an interior monologue with the joints of standard sentences removed. It earns that description because the earlier episodes have taught you who she is, who he is, and what happened in the bed before she was left alone with the night. Taken by itself, the rush of yes would be a stunt. Taken as the eighteenth art in a sequence that has included headlines, a fugue, a pub rant, a magazine romance, a history of English prose, a play, a shelter full of clichés, and a catechism, it is a choice. Joyce gives the last word to a body and a memory the city's public styles had talked around. The stream is real. It is one instrument in a book that keeps changing instruments so that no instrument can pretend to be the whole of consciousness.

Why it matters here

The card on stream of consciousness uses Ulysses as the extreme of the technique James named and Sinclair applied to fiction, and it is right to single out Molly's closing episode as the place where punctuation finally steps aside. The rest of the book is why the card also bothers to distinguish a subject from a technique. Stephen on the strand, Bloom at lunch, Gerty's borrowed style, the hospital's parade of Englishes, and the catechism are different answers to the problem of writing thought. Mrs Dalloway, in the same years, answers with free indirect glides across one city day. Read the two novels together and the phrase on the card becomes a family of arts: a river in James's sense, owned and selective and continuous, rendered by whatever sentence can keep faith with the hour.
