Ulysses is one day in Dublin, 16 June 1904. Leopold Bloom, an advertising canvasser, fries a pork kidney in the kitchen at 7 Eccles Street and carries a cup of tea up to his wife Molly, who stays in bed. She has a letter from Blazes Boylan, the man who will come to the house that afternoon. Bloom goes out, eats, works, and attends the funeral of Paddy Dignam at Glasnevin, with the hour of Boylan's visit sitting in his thoughts among ads, smells, and the memory of Rudy, the son who died in infancy. Late at night he finds Stephen Dedalus, a young writer, after a fight in the brothel district known as Nighttown, walks him back to Eccles Street, and the two of them drink cocoa in the kitchen. Upstairs, Molly lies awake and thinks through her day, her childhood, and Boylan, in a long unpunctuated run that ends "yes I said yes I will Yes."

The same hour of the city belongs to minds that do not match. Bloom's inner speech snags on a small practical thing and then on grief. Stephen's is thick with debts, the death of his mother, and lines of poetry he cannot stop turning over. Molly was never on the street with either of them, and her chapter is the day from the bed. James Joyce published the book in Paris on 2 February 1922, his fortieth birthday, through Sylvia Beach's shop, Shakespeare and Company. The eighteen episodes follow the shape of the Odyssey, with Bloom as a husband making his way home, and the prose changes its skin from episode to episode.

This is the stream William James described, written out instead of summarized: the thunder still full of the silence it broke. Reading it is slow because the page will not reduce a person to a trait. When you write a message and delete the half-thoughts, you are cutting what Joyce keeps. Those half-thoughts, the kidney, the letter on the bed, the cocoa at the end of the night, are the portrait.
