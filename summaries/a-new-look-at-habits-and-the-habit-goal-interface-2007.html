Background

Wendy Wood and David Neal published A new look at habits and the habit-goal interface in Psychological Review in 2007. It is a theoretical paper, not a single experiment, and it is the source Folio's card names for the claim that habits are associations between contexts and responses, triggered with little reference to whatever goal one currently holds. Wood and Neal were writing against two habits of thought at once. The first treats everyday behaviour as the product of current intentions, so that changing the intention should change the act. The second, older in the animal laboratory, treats habits as a residue of over-training that only appears when goals have dropped out. Their new look keeps the animal evidence and puts it back into a life in which people have goals, make plans, and also, much of the time, do what the room has come to cue. The card's title, habits live in context, not in willpower, is their thesis said aloud.

The question

The question is how a habit and a goal share a piece of behaviour. A goal is a representation of an outcome one currently wants, and goal-directed action is sensitive to whether the outcome is still wanted and to whether the action still produces it. A habit, as they define it, is a learned association between a context and a response, acquired slowly as the response is repeated in that context and typically rewarded. Once the association is in place, the context can start the response directly. The theoretical problem is the interface. Goals clearly matter in the formation of habits, because people repeat what they are pursuing and what has paid off. Do goals still matter, in the same way, once the habit is running? Wood and Neal argue that they often do not. A habit can fire when the goal that built it is absent, satisfied, or actively opposed.

The evidence they assemble

The first strand is the animal work on outcome devaluation. A rat trained to press a lever for food will, if the training has made the response habitual, go on pressing after the food has been paired with illness and is no longer something the rat will eat. The outcome's value has changed. The response, cued by the context, continues. In other training regimes, where the response is not habitual, devaluation stops the pressing. The contrast is the cleanest evidence that a response can be independent of the current goal. Wood and Neal treat it as the existence proof, and then ask whether human life contains the same dissociation in a milder form. Studies of people eating stale popcorn in a cinema, or performing a well-practised response after the reason for it has changed, are the analogues they draw on. The context starts the response. The current desire is a spectator.

The second strand is diary research from Wood's group, which supplies the everyday scale of the claim. When people are asked, through the day, what they are doing, where, with whom, and whether they are thinking about it, a large share of actions turn out to be repetitions: the same act, in the same place, at the same time, often while attention is elsewhere. The card's figure is the one to remember. Roughly 43 per cent of everyday behaviours were performed almost daily, in the same context, usually while the person was thinking about something else. That is not a laboratory curiosity. It is a description of the proportion of a day that a purely intentional psychology is not seeing. People do not experience these acts as decisions. They experience them as what happens when one is in the kitchen, or on the route, or with the particular people.

The third strand is the relation between intentions and behaviour once habit strength varies. Intentions predict behaviour well when the behaviour is not habitual, and poorly when it is. The person who has always gone for a run from that house at that hour runs, whether this morning's intention is strong or weak. The person without the habit depends on the intention, and the intention is exactly as fragile as every study of plans has found it to be. Habit strength, measured by how often and how stably the behaviour has been performed in the context, is the moderator. This is the result that makes the theory practical. It says when persuasion will work and when it will be replaced, usefully, by a change of context.

Two routes across the interface

Wood and Neal distinguish the routes so that the theory does not become "goals do not matter". On the first route, direct cuing, the context activates the response without retrieving the goal. This is the devaluation case, and the case of doing something out of habit that one has already decided to stop. The goal may still be in the person's head. It is not in the chain that started the act. On the second route, motivated cuing, a goal that has been adopted activates the habitual means associated with it. A person who wants coffee in a new city still reaches for the gestures that have obtained coffee before. Here the goal is the starter, and the habit is the method. Both routes are needed. The first explains why habits survive changes of mind. The second explains why habits are so useful to a creature with goals: they turn a repeated end into an action that does not have to be assembled from scratch each time.

Formation and performance are separated on purpose. During formation, rewards and goals do the teaching. A response that is not repeated, and that is not reinforced in a stable context, does not become a habit, however sincerely it is intended. During performance, the association can run on context alone. This is why advice that stops at motivation is, on their account, advice about the wrong phase. Motivation is how a person gets the repetitions to happen in the first place, while the context is stable enough for the association to form. Once it has formed, motivation is no longer the lever it was. The lever is the cue. Remove the cue, or change the room, and the habit has nothing to fire on. Leave the cue in place and change only the intention, and the habit fires anyway. The interface is a sequence, not a balance.

What the theory implies for change

The implications are the part the card turns into advice, and they follow from the mechanism rather than from a list of tips. Behaviour change that alters intentions and leaves contexts alone will often fail for the habitual portion of the day, which the diaries suggest is large. Behaviour change that alters contexts will often succeed without a lasting change of heart, because the response never gets cued. Moments when a context is already broken, a move of house, a new job, a change in the journey, are moments when old associations lose their triggers and new ones can be trained. Wood and Neal treat these windows as a prediction of the theory. Intentions should matter more during a disruption, and habits should reassert themselves when the context settles. Adding friction to an unwanted response, and removing friction from a wanted one, is a way of editing the cue and the ease of the response without needing the person to win a daily argument with himself.

Limits

A theoretical review is not a new demonstration, and the strands it weaves are of different strengths. Outcome devaluation in rats is clean and is a few steps from a person eating a biscuit in a particular kitchen. The human evidence is patchier: diary self-report, correlations between habit strength and the intention-behaviour link, a handful of clever field arrangements. A critic can accept the definition and dispute how often everyday behaviour is truly goal-independent rather than merely inattentive. Thinking about something else is not quite the same as a response that would survive a change in the value of its outcome. Wood and Neal are assembling a case, and an assembly can be pulled apart strand by strand. The 43 per cent figure, in particular, is a description of repetition in a diary, not a direct measure of associative cuing. It motivates the theory. It does not prove it.

There is a boundary they draw and that a popular summary sometimes rubs out. New, important, or carefully considered acts are not habits, and the theory does not say they are. Goals, plans and values remain the right language for a decision that has not been repeated in a stable place. The claim is about the large share of action that has been repeated.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on habits takes this paper as its theoretical centre. A habit is a context-response association, learned by repetition, often performed while the mind is elsewhere, and capable of running on when the goal that formed it has changed. Roughly 43 per cent of everyday actions, in the diary work the card cites, have that repetitive, contextual shape. Intentions predict the rest better than they predict these. The card's practical advice, to add friction to bad habits, remove it from good ones, and attach new acts to stable cues, is the interface translated into things a person can rearrange. Changing one's mind and leaving the room unchanged is, on this account, the most common way a resolution fails.

The card's companions fill in what a theoretical paper leaves open. Lally and colleagues time the formation curve and find a median on the order of sixty-six days, with a very wide range, which retires the folklore of three weeks. Galla and Duckworth test the suggestion that beneficial habits, rather than heroic resistance, carry the benefits of self-control. Wood's own later book, Good Habits, Bad Habits, says the whole argument for a general reader. Read the 2007 paper for the distinction that makes the rest of the card coherent: formation uses goals, performance uses context, and willpower is what you need when you have failed to arrange the second.
