Wendy Wood and David Neal's 2007 paper is a theory of how a habit runs, and the evidence they put first comes from diaries. In studies that paged people once an hour, college students and adults in the community wrote down what they were doing, thinking, and feeling. About 45% of the acts they listed were repeated in the same physical place almost every day. Studying, eating, washing, and getting ready for bed clustered in particular rooms and at particular points in the day. The repetition was tied to the setting.

Their claim is about what happens after that repetition has piled up. A habit, on this account, is a link between a response and the features of the context that have gone with it: the kitchen, the previous action, the time of day. Once the link is in place, seeing the context starts the response. A current goal does not have to sit in the middle and approve it. Goals still have three jobs. They get a person to repeat an act often enough for the link to form. They steer the person back into the places that contain the cues. And a person can sometimes guess a goal by noticing the habit. What a goal does not do, Wood and Neal argue, is rewrite a well-learned link on the spot. The association builds slowly, and an occasional effort to do the opposite leaves it standing.

That split is why a resolution and a change of route are different tools. Wanting to eat differently does little in the kitchen where the old sequence starts by itself. Moving the food, changing the order of the morning, or leaving the phone in another room changes the cue the habit is waiting for. The same logic applies at work and in medicine. A pill taken after brushing teeth is attached to a context that already happens. A plan to remember the pill by caring more about health is a request that a goal do a habit's job. The paper's practical advice is to regulate the cue, because the cue is what fires the response once the link has been learned.

Source. Wood, W., and Neal, D. T. (2007). A new look at habits and the habit-goal interface. Psychological Review, 114(4), 843–863. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.114.4.843
