In the Greek romance, the kind of ancient novel written by Chariton and Achilles Tatius, two young people meet, fall in love, and are thrown into adventures. Shipwreck, pirates, false deaths, a rival's plot, a last-minute recognition. Mikhail Bakhtin, in the essay "Forms of Time and of the Chronotope in the Novel," points at what the adventures do not do. The lovers meet at a marriageable age. They marry, at the other end of the book, at the same marriageable age, "no less fresh and handsome." The time of the adventures is intense and empty. It is "a sharp hiatus between two moments of biographical time, a hiatus that leaves no trace in the life of the heroes or in their personalities." A day earlier or a day later and the lovers would miss each other, and there would be no plot. Within the gap, nothing counts as growing up. Bakhtin calls the fusion of a time and a place a chronotope, literally time-space. The chronotope of this romance is adventure-time in an alien world, where space is interchangeable and the clock is only a device for arranging meetings and missed meetings.

He uses that empty time as a contrast, not as a complaint about old books. Other chronotopes leave marks. The road is a place where people of different ranks actually meet. The provincial town is a place where time is visible as habit, gossip, and aging. The castle carries the past as something still occupying rooms. Once you have the term, you can see that a plot is always a decision about what time is allowed to do to a body. In the Greek romance, time is a delay between desire and the wedding, and the delay is not permitted to tire anyone. In a novel of the kind Bakhtin cares about elsewhere, many social languages share the page, and time is unfinished because the present is unfinished. The four essays in The Dialogic Imagination were written in the 1930s and published in English in 1981, edited by Michael Holquist and translated by Caryl Emerson and Holquist. "Chronotope" is the piece of that work that traveled farthest, because it gives a name to a choice writers make anyway.

When you outline a story, you are already picking a chronotope, whether or not you want the word. A season of trials that leaves the hero the same age and the same person can be the right choice for a tale of ordeal, or for a comedy of delays. It is the wrong choice if you also want the ending to be earned by what the years did. Ask, of the gap between the meeting and the marriage, or between the crime and the verdict, whether that interval leaves a trace or is only there to be survived. Bakhtin's lovers are still fresh when they kiss. That is the technical observation. The rest is what you decide a clock is for.
