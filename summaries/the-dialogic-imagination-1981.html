Four essays, arranged for an English reader

The Dialogic Imagination is an English book made in 1981 from work Mikhail Bakhtin had written much earlier. Michael Holquist edited it. Caryl Emerson and Holquist translated it. The essays come from a Russian collection published in 1975, and Bakhtin had drafted them in the 1930s and early 1940s. The editors set them in an order of growing difficulty: Epic and Novel, then From the Prehistory of Novelistic Discourse, then Forms of Time and of the Chronotope in the Novel, then Discourse in the Novel. A reader who starts at the first page is therefore following a classroom sequence, a path into the vocabulary, as much as a chronology of Bakhtin's life. The introduction and the glossary are part of that path. Words such as heteroglossia arrive with a definition because Bakhtin's Russian terms do not sit quietly inside ordinary English.

The subject that ties the four essays together is the novel's relation to language. Bakhtin treats the novel as the genre that can take in many ways of speaking and keep them audible. Epic, lyric, and official speech each have their dignity in his account. The novel's particular power is its hospitality to conflict among those speeches. Folio's card on polyphony meets Dostoevsky in the other book. Here the same thinker asks what any novel is doing when it lets a street dialect, a professional jargon, a sermon, and a private confession share one prose.

Epic and novel

Epic and Novel begins with a contrast of worlds. The epic, as Bakhtin describes it, looks back to a finished national past. Its heroes are already complete. The distance between the singer and the hero is a distance of reverence, and the language that crosses that distance is a high, unified language. The novel lives in a different time. It can touch the present, the unfinished, the low, and the laughable. Its hero is someone whose identity is still being argued, often by the hero himself. Because the present is unfinished, the novel can absorb letters, diaries, speeches, and the styles of yesterday's newspapers without first translating them into a single epic diction.

Bakhtin is fond of laughter as a historical force in this essay. Laughter brings what is high and distant down to a level where it can be examined, turned over, and retold in another voice. Once that has happened, a genre can represent a world in which more than one evaluation is alive. He thinks this is why the novel feels younger than it is, and why it keeps invading other genres. A poem or a play written after the novel has become dominant often has to answer the novel, even when it refuses the novel's habits. The essay is a theory of literary history told as a theory of how close a genre stands to the present tense.

Before the novel had a name

From the Prehistory of Novelistic Discourse looks backward, past the eighteenth century, for the energies that would later look like novels. Bakhtin is interested in periods when more than one language shared a culture. He uses polyglossia for that historical condition: two or more national languages in contact, each able to hear the other as a language and as a view of the world. In such conditions, he argues, no single language can pretend to be the transparent voice of truth. Parody becomes possible at a deep level, because one language can represent another and, in representing it, show its limits.

The essay spends time with ancient and medieval materials, including traditions of comic and parodic retelling, in which solemn narratives are rewritten from below. Bakhtin is assembling a prehistory of what he sometimes calls novelness, a pressure inside culture that may appear before anyone has a settled name for the novel as a modern genre. A reader looking for plot summaries of Tom Jones will be in the wrong chapter. The reward is a sense of why prose fiction, once it gets going, is so good at hosting imitation, satire, and the sly quotation of someone else's style.

The chronotope

The third essay introduces the chronotope, Bakhtin's name for the way a narrative fuses time with space. A chronotope is a typical setting in which certain kinds of event are possible. On the road, strangers meet and fates cross. In a castle, the past presses on the present as Gothic terror. In a provincial town, biographies unfold at the pace of rumour and the daily round. In a threshold, a crisis can happen in a moment that feels outside ordinary duration. Bakhtin reads ancient adventure novels, chivalric stories, idylls, and later realistic fiction as a history of these fusions. The point is that time in a novel is never a neutral clock. The clock is built into the rooms, roads, and parlours where people are allowed to change.

This essay is the one to read slowly if the earlier pair felt like manifestos. It is full of examples, and the examples do the arguing. When he comes to the modern novel, the chronotope of the threshold and the chronotope of the parlour help explain why some fiction lives on sudden encounters and why some lives on the slow exposure of a household. Dostoevsky's staircases and scandal scenes belong here, though this essay is doing a wider historical job. The practical lesson for a reader is simple to carry and hard to exhaust. Ask where the novel thinks time can happen. The answer will tell you what kinds of persons and changes the book is able to take seriously.

Heteroglossia in the sentence

Discourse in the Novel is the essay Folio's card points to when it sends a reader towards heteroglossia. Heteroglossia is the coexistence, inside one language, of many social languages: the speech of professions, generations, regions, classes, newspapers, churches, and families. Bakhtin thinks every national language is already this kind of crowd. Centripetal forces try to pull it towards a norm, a standard, a schoolbook correctness. Centrifugal forces spin it outward into variety. The novel is the art that can represent the crowd as a crowd.

He shows this with the idea of hybrid speech. A single sentence of novelistic prose can contain the narrator's accent and a character's accent at once. Free indirect style is one famous way of doing it, though Bakhtin ranges more widely than any one technique. He is interested in the way a word arrives already used, already answering other uses. Dialogue, in this essay, is a property of language before it is a string of indented speeches. An utterance is a link in a chain. It responds to what has been said, and it is shaped by the answer it expects. The novel's distinction is that it can stage that chain openly, letting a solemn word and a mocking word occupy neighbouring clauses, sometimes the same clause.

Readers often come to this essay after the Dostoevsky book and expect another study of independent heroes. They find something adjacent. Polyphony is a special achievement inside the novel, a case in which whole consciousnesses remain unmerged. Heteroglossia is the linguistic weather in which novels, polyphonic and otherwise, have to live. Dickens can be richly heteroglot, full of professional and class dialects bending the narrator's voice, and still organise those dialects towards a moral centre in a way Bakhtin thinks Dostoevsky refuses. The Dialogic Imagination gives you the weather. Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics gives you one extraordinary climate inside it.

Why it matters here

The card on polyphony describes dialogism and heteroglossia as the wider ideas that make Dostoevsky's many voices intelligible, and it names this English collection as the way in. The four essays earn that recommendation because they move in order from a picture of the novel against the epic, through a prehistory of parody and linguistic contact, into the concrete settings where novelistic time happens, and only then into the theory of social languages. A reader who wants a usable test can take it from the last essay. Mark a paragraph in any novel and ask how many ways of speaking are active in it, and whose answer each way expects. That question is Bakhtin's, and this book is where he builds it for the novel as a whole.
