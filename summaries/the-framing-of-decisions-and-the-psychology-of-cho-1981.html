Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman asked students at Stanford and the University of British Columbia to choose a program against a disease expected to kill 600 people. One wording said that if program A were adopted, 200 people would be saved, and that program B gave a one-third chance that all 600 would be saved. Of 152 students, 72 percent chose the sure saving. A second group got the same numbers as deaths. Program C: 400 people would die. Program D: a one-third chance that nobody would die, and a two-thirds chance that 600 would. Of 155 students, 22 percent chose the sure 400 deaths and 78 percent took the gamble. The two pairs of programs are the same pair. "Saved" produced caution. "Die" produced a bet.

A second pair of questions, about a $10 play, shows the same habit with money. Imagine you have decided to see the play and, walking in, find you have lost a $10 bill. Would you still buy a ticket? Of 183 people, 88 percent said yes. Now imagine you have already bought the ticket and lost that. The seat was not marked, so the ticket is gone. Would you pay $10 for another? Of 200 people, 46 percent said yes. The wallet is $10 lighter either way. In the second telling, the evening now costs $20, because the lost ticket and the new one land in the same mental account. The lost bill does not.

A consent form, a discount, and a job offer all have a frame like these. Survival rates and death rates are the same facts, and patients and doctors move when the label changes. A fee called a surcharge is harder to swallow than a cash price called a discount, even when the money is identical. And a small extra cost feels large when it is the whole purchase, as with a second ticket, and small when it is just a bill you happened to lose. The authors' advice is the unglamorous one: write the same choice the other way, in gains and in losses, or in this account and in the wider one, and see whether you still want it.

The disease result has been repeated, at a smaller size than the original classroom split. In the 2014 Many Labs project, 36 samples and 6,271 people still showed the gain-versus-loss shift, with a weighted effect of d = 0.60, and 86 percent of the samples found it. The direction held. The 72 percent versus 22 percent was the dramatic end, not the typical one.
