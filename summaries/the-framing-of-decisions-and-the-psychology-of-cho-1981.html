Background

Prospect theory had said, in 1979, that people evaluate changes from a reference point, and that losses loom larger than gains. The value function is concave over gains, which yields a preference for a sure gain over a risky one, and convex over losses, which yields a preference for a gamble over a sure loss. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's 1981 paper in Science, The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice, is the demonstration that this psychology can be switched on by a description. The same outcomes, the same probabilities, and a different wording are enough to move the reference point, and with it the choice. Folio's framing card is built on that demonstration, and on the problem that carried it into classrooms.

The problem is the Asian disease problem, and it belongs to this paper. An outbreak is expected to kill 600 people. One programme will save 200 lives for certain. The other gives a one-third chance of saving all 600 and a two-thirds chance of saving nobody. Most people choose the sure saving. Describe the identical programmes as deaths, and the majority moves. The paper's claim is that the move is not a slip of arithmetic. It is the way a frame sets the origin from which gains and losses are felt.

The question

If two descriptions are mathematically the same, must a rational preference treat them as the same? The principle Tversky and Kahneman put under pressure is invariance. A preference between options should depend on the outcomes and the probabilities, not on how a speaker happens to draw the zero. Everyday decision theory assumed invariance the way it assumed that a person knows what she wants. The question of the paper is what people do when invariance is easy to violate and hard to notice. Do they recode every frame back into a canonical form, or do they accept the frame they were given and choose inside it?

How the studies were set up

The Asian disease problem was given in two versions, to different people, so that no one would see the trick by comparison. The cover story said that the United States was preparing for an outbreak of an unusual disease, expected to kill 600 people if nothing were done. In the gain frame, programme A saves 200 people for certain. Programme B gives a one-third probability of saving all 600, and a two-thirds probability of saving nobody. In the loss frame the same arithmetic is told in deaths. Programme C means that 400 people will die for certain. Programme D gives a one-third probability that nobody will die, and a two-thirds probability that all 600 will die. Saving 200 is the same state of the world as losing 400, once 600 are expected to die. A one-third chance of saving everyone is the same gamble as a one-third chance that no one dies. The pairs are not approximately alike. They are the same pair.

The paper does not stop at the disease. Other problems present money, or other outcomes, relative to different reference points, so that an identical final wealth can be felt as a gain in one wording and as a loss in another. Some problems frame the contingency rather than the outcome, describing a protective action by the lives it secures or by the deaths it fails to prevent. The method throughout is the method of the disease problem. Hold the consequences fixed. Change the origin the description invites. Ask for a choice, not for a judgement about whether the two wordings match.

What they found

In the gain frame, most people chose the sure saving of 200. In the loss frame, most people chose the gamble. The majority that had been risk-averse when the story was about lives saved became risk-seeking when the story was about deaths. Nothing in the medical facts had changed. The reference point had. Against a background of 600 expected deaths, 200 survivors are a gain, and a concave value function prefers that gain for certain. Against the same background, 400 deaths are a loss, and a convex value function prefers a chance of losing nothing to a certain loss. The reflection effect from 1979 is here produced by a sentence.

The other problems in the paper make the same point without an epidemic. When a description moves the reference point, a person who had preferred the sure thing can prefer the risk, and can do so while remaining sure that she is answering the question that was asked. People do not spontaneously translate a loss frame into a gain frame, or a gain frame into a loss frame, before they choose. They choose inside the frame. Invariance is a normative requirement. It is not a description of the unprompted choice.

How the authors read the results

Tversky and Kahneman read the shift as prospect theory, not as a loose suggestion that wording matters. The frame supplies the reference point. Outcomes are then coded as gains or as losses relative to that point, and the shape of the value function does the rest. A frame that highlights gains encourages risk aversion. A frame that highlights losses encourages risk seeking. The Asian disease problem is the clean case because the arithmetic identity is obvious once both versions are on the table, and invisible when only one version is. The bias is not a failure to multiply one-third by 600. It is a failure to notice that a different multiplication, from a different zero, is the same plan.

They draw a wider moral about the nature of preference. If a choice can be reversed by a redescription that preserves every consequence, then the preference was not retrieved from a stable list of wants. It was constructed at the moment of choice, inside a frame the decision maker did not choose. That is the sentence Folio's card treats as the lesson. Preferences are often built when the question arrives, out of whatever zero the question suggests. A person can be perfectly serious, and still have no frame-free preference waiting underneath the wording.

Limits

The disease problem is a vignette. Nobody in the study was a health minister, and nobody's choice saved or lost a life. The paper shows that a frame can reverse a majority on a hypothetical outbreak. It does not show that every real committee, staring at the same spreadsheet for a week, will split in the same way, or that expertise is a perfect solvent. The medical sequel on this card, the 1982 study of surgery and radiation, is the paper that asks the question of physicians and patients with a real disease. This one earns the right to that sequel. It does not replace it.

There is a limit of size as well. Most people shifted. The paper's famous result is a reversal of the modal choice, not a claim that every reader is helpless against every adjective. Later work, including Kühberger's meta-analysis, finds the effect reliable and also sensitive to the details of the task. A frame that barely changes the reference point, or a pair of options that are not truly identical, will not reproduce the disease problem's clean flip. The 1981 paper is the existence proof and the theory. It is not a catalogue of every wording that works.

Replications and critiques

The Asian disease problem has been repeated often, and Folio's card records that it replicated in the Many Labs projects, in contrast to many more fragile effects of the same era. Replication here means that the gain frame still favours the sure thing and the loss frame still favours the gamble, across laboratories. It does not mean the shift is the same size in every population, or that a clumsy paraphrase will do. Kühberger's review is the place on this card for the dependence on detail. The 1981 paper remains the source of the problem those later tests are tests of.

A different criticism accepts the finding and disputes the moral. If people construct preferences on the spot, then asking them what they want is already an intervention, and whoever writes the question is part of the choice. Tversky and Kahneman state that implication. They do not, in this paper, propose a politics of who should hold the pen. The framing result is a constraint on anyone who thought a neutral description was the default. It is not yet a theory of which frame a clinic or a parliament ought to use.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Framing: same facts, different choices, is a reading of this paper. The 600 expected deaths, the sure saving of 200, the one-third chance of saving all 600, and the matching description in deaths are the 1981 problem, and they should not be borrowed to decorate a different article. The link to prospect theory is the point of the card. Gains invite the sure thing. Losses invite the gamble. The same facts, under two zeros, produce both invitations. McNeil and colleagues take the mechanism into the clinic. Kühberger asks how often, and under what wording, the mechanism fires. Read the 1981 paper for the problem itself, and for the claim that a preference can be a product of its frame.
