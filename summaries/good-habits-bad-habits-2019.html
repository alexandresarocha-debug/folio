Wendy Wood's 2019 book Good Habits, Bad Habits is built on field experiments about popcorn. David Neal, Wood, Mengju Wu, and David Kurlander handed people a box that was either freshly popped or a week old and stale, and weighed what was left. At a campus cinema, 98 people watched movie trailers with a box that started at about 62 grams. People with a strong habit of eating popcorn at the movies ate about the same amount either way. In that theater they finished about 63% of the box regardless of how it tasted. They could tell the difference. Fresh popcorn was rated more likable than stale. The rating did not steer the hand. People with a weak habit ate according to taste.

The same boxes did not do the same thing in a meeting room. Sixty people watched music videos there. A strong cinema habit predicted eating the stale popcorn in the theater, the place where the habit had been performed before. It did not predict that eating in a room with no such history. A second cinema study, with 89 people, changed the hand instead of the room. A box with a handle forced some people to eat with the nondominant hand. When the familiar hand was free, strong-habit eaters again ate fresh and stale alike. When the hand was the unfamiliar one, how the popcorn tasted got its vote back.

The book uses those results to move the advice off motivation. Hunger, liking, and a plan to eat less were weak levers inside the cue that usually starts the habit. Changing the room, or making the movement slightly awkward, interrupted the sequence. The same pattern shows up with a phone at dinner, a cigarette with coffee, or a snack on the commute. While the context is intact, deciding you no longer want the thing arrives late. Move the cue, or make the old movement hard to run, and the habit has less to grab.

Source. Wood, W. (2019). Good Habits, Bad Habits: The Science of Making Positive Changes That Stick. New York: Farrar, Straus and Giroux. https://archive.org/details/goodhabitsbadhab0000wend_n9p2
