The research programme, said for a general reader

Wendy Wood's Good Habits, Bad Habits, published in 2019, is the book-length version of a case she had been making in journals for years. Habits, on that case, are learned associations between a context and a response. A place, a time, a preceding action, or a set of people comes to trigger a behaviour while the person is often thinking about something else. The book gathers the evidence for a reader who will never open Psychological Review, and it turns the evidence into a practical account of why good intentions fail and what to do instead. Folio's card names the book as the summary of the research for a general audience. A walkthrough should keep the book's role clear. It is a synthesis and a guide. The measurements it relies on were made in the papers it draws from.

The card's own summary is the set of claims the book exists to teach. Diary studies found that roughly 43 per cent of everyday behaviours were performed almost daily, in the same context, usually while people were thinking about something else. Intentions predict behaviour poorly once a habit is strong. Changing context, a move of house or a change of job, opens a window in which old cues are gone and new responses can be attached. Formation is slow and variable. Lally and colleagues, tracking ninety-six volunteers, found a median of sixty-six days to a plateau of automaticity, with a range from eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four days. The popular claim that a habit takes twenty-one days is unsupported. Advice follows: add friction to unwanted acts, remove friction from wanted ones, and tie new acts to stable cues. Wood's book is where those sentences become a continuous argument rather than a list on a card.

Context as the unit of the habit

The theoretical chapter of the argument is the one Wood and David Neal set out in 2007. A habit is formed while a person is pursuing a goal and repeating a response in a stable setting, typically with some reward. Once formed, the setting can start the response without consulting the goal. That dissociation is what makes habits both powerful and awkward. They continue when the desire that built them has faded, and they continue when a person has sincerely decided to stop. The book spends its early pages making this feel like a description of ordinary days rather than like a claim about rats and levers. The kitchen, the commute, the desk at the start of work, the sofa at a certain hour: these are the contexts in which a reader can recognise an act that happens before a decision is quite made.

The diary proportion is the book's way of saying that this is a large slice of life. Roughly 43 per cent is large enough that a psychology built only on intentions will miss nearly half of what people do. Wood uses the figure to shift the reader's attention from dramatic choices to repeated ones. The dramatic choices are real, and the book does not deny deliberation, values, or plans. It locates them as the right tools for acts that are new, rare, or genuinely contested, and as the wrong tools, used alone, for acts that already have a cue. A resolution that changes a mind and leaves the cue in place is, in the book's narrative, the standard way a new year fails. The cue fires. The old response appears. The person concludes that the failure was a shortage of character, which is the conclusion the book is written to retire.

Friction, cues, and the disruption of a move

The practical chapters follow the mechanism rather than a catalogue of hacks. If context starts the response, then editing the context is the intervention that matches the cause. Friction is the homely version of that edit. An extra step between the person and an unwanted act, a phone in another room, a food that has to be fetched, makes the habitual response less smooth. A removed step in front of a wanted act, the exercise clothes already out, the fruit already on the counter, makes the new response the smoother one. The book treats these edits as changes to the association, not as tests of virtue. A person who needs the edits is a person who has understood the theory. The edits work, when they work, because they change what the room invites, including on the days when intention is tired.

Stable cues are the other half. A new behaviour needs a reliable trigger if it is going to become the sort of act that no longer needs a decision. Wood's advice is to attach the new act to something that already happens, and to keep that pairing steady enough for the association to form. Lally's curve is the reason the advice includes patience. Early repetitions do the most to build a sense of automaticity, later ones consolidate, and the time to a plateau varies so widely that a single deadline will mislead. The book can tell a reader to expect a process measured in weeks and often in months. It cannot honestly tell a reader the morning on which the process will feel finished. Sixty-six days is a median from one study of simple daily acts. Used as a landmark and as a warning against the twenty-one-day folklore, the median earns its place. Treated as a promise to every reader, it does not.

Disruption is the third practical idea, and it is the one most easily missed by readers hunting for a trick they can do at the desk they already have. When the context itself changes, a house, a job, a route, the old associations lose their triggers. Intentions, which are weak against an intact habit, become more effective in the gap, because there is no cue competing with them. Wood presents these windows as opportunities the theory predicts. A person who arrives in a new city with a plan for the first weeks is using a moment the theory says is rare. A person who arrives and lets the new rooms fill up at random is training whatever the random fill happens to be. The book is an argument for treating moves and new jobs as habit-formation projects, which is a less glamorous use of a life change than the culture usually proposes, and a more realistic one.

Willpower, relocated

Galla and Duckworth's finding belongs in the book's portrait of self-control, and the card places it there. People high in trait self-control report fewer temptations, and beneficial habits mediate the relationship between that trait and positive life outcomes. The person who seems to have unusual willpower may be the person who least often needs it. Wood's book is the narrative version of that finding. It asks the reader to admire the arranged life rather than the clenched will, and to notice that arrangement is a skill with materials: cues, friction, repetition, and the luck or the planning of a stable context. Admiration shifts, and with it the blame. A failed diet that left the kitchen unchanged is a failed design. The blame belongs with the unchanged room.

The relocation has a limit the book has to keep in view if it is not to become a new moralism. Contexts are unequally available. A shift worker, a crowded flat, a street without a safe walk, and a job that changes hour by hour are all situations in which stable cues are expensive. Advice about fruit on the counter assumes a counter, and time, and fruit. Wood's research programme is a psychology of the interface between habit and goal. The distribution of helpful interfaces is a social fact sitting outside any one reader's kitchen. The book can still be useful as an account of mechanism, and a reader should hear the advice at the volume the mechanism earns: strong where the setting can be edited, quieter where it cannot. Folio's card, aimed at a general reader of the idea, is in the same position. Friction and cues are real levers. They are levers some days and some lives can reach more easily than others.

Ego depletion makes a brief contrasting appearance in any honest map of this shelf, and it should stay brief. The claim that willpower is a tank, illustrated by people who resisted cookies, ate radishes, and then persisted less on an unsolvable puzzle, did not reappear in Hagger's large preregistered multilab test in 2016. Wood's book does not need that tank. Its account of self-control is about how often the tank is called on. A science that finds the tank effect fragile and the habit effect more durable is a science that has moved its practical advice from rest and glucose towards the design of the room. The book is a popular statement of that move.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Habits live in context, not in willpower, lists Good Habits, Bad Habits as the place a general reader can meet the whole argument. The 2007 paper with Neal is the theory of the interface. The diary proportion, roughly 43 per cent of everyday acts repeated in context while attention is elsewhere, is the scale. Lally's curve, a median of sixty-six days and a range from eighteen to two hundred and fifty-four, is the formation clock, and the retirement of the twenty-one-day claim. Galla and Duckworth are the evidence that beneficial habits carry much of what trait self-control seems to do. Read Wood's book for the continuous version of that case, including the advice about friction, stable cues, and the windows opened by a change of context. Read the papers when you want the claim at the size the studies actually support. The book is the guide. The card is the reminder that the guide is standing on those studies, and that willpower was the wrong place to look for the engine.
