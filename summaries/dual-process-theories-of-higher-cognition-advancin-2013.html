A defence that begins by narrowing the claim

Jonathan St. B. T. Evans and Keith Stanovich’s article “Dual-Process Theories of Higher Cognition: Advancing the Debate”, published in Perspectives on Psychological Science in 2013, is a review with a programme. Dual-process theories, the family of ideas that distinguishes a fast, automatic kind of thinking from a slow, deliberative kind, had become popular enough to attract a serious backlash. Critics said the theories were vague, that they re-described findings without predicting them, that the two “systems” were characters in search of a mechanism, and that a single process with varying effort could explain the same data. Evans and Stanovich had both helped to build the family of theories. Their article does not answer the critics by repeating the popular version. It answers by making the popular version smaller and sharper, and by stating what a dual-process theory is committed to and what it is not.

The popular version, the one Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow made common knowledge, lines up a set of opposites. One process is fast, unconscious, automatic, emotional, and evolutionarily old. The other is slow, conscious, controlled, rational, and new. Evans and Stanovich’s first move is to say that most of this list consists of typical correlates, not of defining features. A process can be fast because it is practised, emotional because of its content, or evolutionarily old without that fact doing any explanatory work. If the theory is identified with the whole list, it is easy to refute, because there are automatic processes that are not ancient and deliberative episodes that are not especially rational. Their proposal is to define the types by one property each, and then to let the correlates be correlates, usual but not necessary.

The defining features

Type 1 processing, in their definition, is autonomous. It does not require working memory, and it runs when its triggering conditions are met, whether or not the person intended it. Skilled perception, the retrieval of a stored response, and the fluent wrong answer to a trick question are all autonomous in this sense. Autonomy is what makes Type 1 both powerful and dangerous. It delivers an answer without asking permission, and the answer can be expert or absurd depending on what has been stored. Type 2 processing is defined by the engagement of working memory and by cognitive decoupling: the capacity to suppose, to simulate a possibility that is not the one in front of you, and to hold a representation apart from the world so that it can be manipulated. Decoupling is their candidate for the heart of higher cognition. It is what lets a person entertain a hypothesis, follow a rule that conflicts with appearance, or check an intuitive answer against a calculation. It is slow and limited because working memory is slow and limited, not because slowness was written into a job description.

This redefinition changes several arguments at once. Slowness is no longer the essence of Type 2, so a critic who finds a fast process that is in some other way “controlled” has not automatically collapsed the distinction. Rationality is no longer the essence either. A Type 2 episode can apply a foolish rule carefully, and a Type 1 response can be the correct one, as it is in a well-trained expert who simply sees the diagnosis. The theory’s claim is architectural. There are processes that run by themselves, and there is a capacity, dependent on working memory, that can intervene on their outputs. The claim is not that the second capacity is the good one. Evans and Stanovich are explicit that the field did itself harm by talking as if System 2 were the adult and System 1 the child. Intervention can fail to happen, can happen and still err, or can be unnecessary because the autonomous response was already right.

Default and intervention

The architecture they defend is default-interventionist. Type 1 processes generate a default response. Type 2 processes may intervene to override it. Intervention is not guaranteed by the presence of a conflict. It depends on the person detecting that something is wrong, on having the working-memory capacity to construct an alternative, and on being disposed to spend the effort. This is where individual differences enter, and where the article connects with the Cognitive Reflection Test without being a paper about that test. People differ in cognitive ability, which Stanovich has long separated into the efficiency of the machinery and the knowledge, the mindware, the machinery can apply. They also differ in thinking dispositions, the willingness to engage the machinery rather than to accept the default. A person can have the ability and lack the disposition, or have the disposition and lack a crucial piece of knowledge, and in both cases the intuitive answer stands. Frederick’s test, on this reading, mixes ability and disposition in a way that is empirically convenient and theoretically impure. Evans and Stanovich want the impurity noticed, not scorned.

They contrast the default-interventionist picture with a parallel picture in which two processes always run together and race. The default-interventionist view fits the evidence that people often give the intuitive answer without ever constructing the alternative, and it fits the finding that load on working memory hurts the override more than it hurts the intuition. It also explains why a warning, a training, or a monetary incentive sometimes does little. If the conflict is not detected, there is nothing for the incentive to act on. The article is careful not to pretend that one diagram has won. It argues that the diagram which says “an autonomous default, then an optional, expensive intervention” organises more of the findings than the diagram which says “two systems with a list of opposite adjectives”.

Answering the critics

To the charge of vagueness they offer the defining features. A theory that predicts an effect of working-memory load on one kind of response and not on another is a theory a critic can try to break. To the charge of the homunculus, the little person inside System 2 who does the thinking, they answer that Type 2 is not a person. It is the set of operations that working memory makes possible, serial and capacity-limited, and there is no requirement that it contain another copy of the mind. To the single-process critics, among them advocates of a unimodel in which rule use varies only in accessibility and motivation, they grant that many findings can be redescribed in single-process language. They do not grant that the redescription is an explanation of cognitive decoupling, or of why hypothetical thinking has the limits it has. A continuum of effort, they argue, can be what a two-type architecture feels like from inside, without the architecture being a continuum all the way down. The qualitative distinction they will not give up is the distinction between processing that needs working memory and processing that does not.

They also tidy the evolutionary story. Popular dual-process talk often says that System 1 is the old animal and System 2 the new human achievement. Evans and Stanovich treat this as an optional and risky overlay. Some autonomous processes are recent acquisitions, the result of practice. Some distinctively human cognition may itself have become partly autonomous. The theory does not need a morality play about evolution in order to function in the laboratory. Dropping the play removes a line of criticism that was always aimed at the rhetoric rather than at the experiments, and it stops the theory from predicting, falsely, that whatever is fast is primitive.

What the review does not settle

The article is a conceptual review, not a meta-analysis, and it does not produce a new estimate of how large any particular bias is. Its evidence is the existing literature, organised. A reader who wants a number will be in the wrong paper. A reader who wants to know whether “dual process” still names a claim worth making, after a decade of complaints, is in the right one. The remaining disagreements are real. Some researchers still prefer a family of specialised processes to two types. Some think the intervention stage has been described more clearly than it has been observed. Some, working in the tradition of Kahneman’s heuristics, care more about the list of biases than about the architecture, and will reasonably say that a bias can be robust whether or not one has the right diagram of the mind that produces it. Evans and Stanovich would agree with the last point. Their claim is only that if one is going to use the diagram, one should use the version that can survive contact with the critics.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on two systems presents Kahneman’s characters and then notes that researchers continue to debate whether two distinct systems exist or whether processing is better seen as a continuum. This article is that debate, conducted by two of the people most entitled to run it, and it is the card’s cited review. Their answer is neither the popular slogan nor a flat rejection. Two types, defined by autonomy and by working memory, with intervention optional and fallible, and with most of the famous opposites demoted to correlates. The card also points to Frederick’s test, which this framework interprets as a measure of whether the optional intervention happened, and to the 1974 heuristics paper, whose biases are what the default response looks like when the intervention does not. Read Evans and Stanovich when the card’s two systems start to sound like two people. The article’s whole effort is to keep the distinction and fire the homunculi.
