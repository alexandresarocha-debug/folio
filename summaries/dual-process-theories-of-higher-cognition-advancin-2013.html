Jonathan Evans and Keith Stanovich use a syllogism to show the two answers that can be in play at once. People were asked whether a conclusion had to follow, not whether it was true. One valid argument said that no police dogs are vicious, some highly trained dogs are vicious, and therefore some highly trained dogs are not police dogs. Across three experiments by Evans, Barston, and Pollard, 89 percent accepted it. A valid argument with an unbelievable conclusion, that some vitamin tablets are not nutritional, was accepted by 56 percent. An invalid argument with a believable conclusion, that some addictive things are not cigarettes, was accepted by 71 percent. An invalid and unbelievable conclusion, that some millionaires are not rich, was accepted by 10 percent. Logic moved the answers. Belief moved them more, and it moved them most when the logic was bad.

A second line of evidence is what happens when the slow check is blocked. Evans and Stanovich point to studies in which people judged the same kind of syllogism under a time limit or while holding other material in mind. Accuracy fell and the pull of belief rose. If the time pressure had only made people sloppy, the errors would have been scattered. They were not. People slid toward the conclusion that sounded true.

That is the version of the idea worth keeping at a desk. A sentence that fits what you already think can be accepted before you test whether it follows. A sentence that clashes with what you think gets the test, and the test needs spare attention. A deadline, a second task, or a long brief makes the believable answer win. The practical move is to separate "does this follow?" from "do I like where it lands?" and to do the first one when you are not already full.

Evans and Stanovich also cut back the popular picture of two little minds, one fast and biased, one slow and wise. They argue that the only firm line is this: some answers arrive on their own and do not need working memory, and some answers require you to hold a possibility in mind that is not the world in front of you. Speed, consciousness, and being right are common companions of that line, not the line itself. A fast answer can be correct. A slow one can be a careful defense of the first hunch.
