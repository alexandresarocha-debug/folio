A replication designed to study variation, not a single failure

In 2014 a large group of researchers, with Richard Klein among the coordinating authors, published “Investigating Variation in Replicability: A ‘Many Labs’ Replication Project” in Social Psychology. The paper belongs to the period in which psychology was confronting how many of its familiar findings could be reproduced, and it was built to answer a more specific question than “does it replicate”. If a finding fails in a new laboratory, the failure is ambiguous. It may mean the finding was never real, or it may mean that some unnoticed feature of the new setting, the country, the kind of participant, the difference between a laboratory and an online sample, changed the effect. Many Labs 1 was designed to remove that ambiguity for a set of findings by running the same protocols in many places at once. Thirty-six independent samples took part, thirty-six collections of data in a common design, amounting to 6,344 participants. Twenty-seven of the collections were in a laboratory and nine were online. Twenty-five were in the United States and eleven were elsewhere. Each team followed the same instructions, met a minimum sample size, and documented its procedure. Variation, if it appeared, could then be compared with variation in setting.

The project chose thirteen effects that were classic or contemporary and that could be administered in a standard session. The set was not a random sample of psychology, and the paper says so. It was a set that could be run cleanly, that included both effects the authors expected to be sturdy and effects that were more fashionable and more fragile, and that covered topics a large international group could agree to reproduce exactly. The point of including the fragile ones was the point of the project. A replication effort that only ever tests effects everyone already believes will teach nothing about variation, and an effort that only tests curiosities will teach nothing about the centre of the field.

What was tested, and what anchoring was

The thirteen effects included anchoring, gain-versus-loss framing, the sunk-cost fallacy, the retrospective gambler’s fallacy, a question-wording effect in which “allowed” and “forbidden” draw different responses, a scale effect in which the labels on a rating scale change the answers, a norm-of-reciprocity effect, imagined contact as a way of reducing prejudice, a flag-priming effect on political attitudes, a currency-priming effect on endorsement of the prevailing system, and measures of implicit attitudes towards mathematics and of the sex difference in those attitudes. Anchoring was not the 1974 wheel-of-fortune study. It was a later, well-specified version, from Jacowitz and Kahneman, in which people judge a quantity after being asked to consider a number that is clearly too high or too low. The replication used four of the original topics: the distance from San Francisco to New York, the population of Chicago, the height of Mount Everest, and the number of babies born per day in the United States, with metric conversions where the sample required them. Each person estimated after a high anchor or after a low one. This is the two-step, explicitly considered form of anchoring, the form that the broader literature regards as the robust case, rather than the subtler form in which a number merely happens to be in the room.

Framing was included in a form descended from the Asian disease problem, adapted so that the country named in the scenario was the country of the participants. Its presence in the same session as anchoring is useful for Folio’s purposes, because it puts two of the field’s famous judgement effects through the same multi-laboratory sieve. The priming effects were included as representatives of a different, newer literature, in which a symbol or an image was reported to shift attitudes. Everyone in the project answered a common set. Order and materials were controlled so that a difference between laboratories would not be a difference of wording.

What replicated, and where anchoring sat

In the aggregate, ten of the thirteen effects replicated in the sense the paper uses: the overall result was in the predicted direction and distinguishable from zero, with the size varying from effect to effect and not always matching the original report. Anchoring was among the successes, and among the larger and more consistent ones. It appeared across the samples rather than in a few lucky laboratories. Framing also belonged to the set that reproduced. The paper’s figure of the individual samples is the right way to see the result. A replicated effect is not a guarantee that every single site found a significant result on its own. It is a demonstration that the average effect is there and that the sites are scattered around it rather than around zero. Anchoring’s scatter was tight enough, and far enough from zero, that the project treated it as a model of a reliable finding.

One effect, imagined contact reducing prejudice, received only weak support. The aggregate leaned slightly in the predicted direction, and most individual samples did not show the effect clearly. Two effects did not replicate. Flag priming, the reported shift in conservative attitudes after exposure to a national flag, and currency priming, the reported shift in system-justifying attitudes after exposure to money, produced aggregate effects indistinguishable from zero. Sites did not quietly find a smaller version of the original result. They found nothing, with the occasional significant result no more common than chance and, in one case, in the wrong direction. The contrast inside a single project is the contrast the replication crisis needed. It was no longer possible to say that psychology’s methods could not reproduce anything, or that they could reproduce everything. In the same laboratories, with the same participants, some effects were boringly reliable and some evaporated.

Did the setting matter?

The second question of the paper was the question about variation itself. Did laboratory samples differ from online samples in the size of effects? Did American samples differ from samples elsewhere? The answer, for this set of effects, was that by and large they did not. An effect that was large in one kind of site was large in the others. An effect that was absent was absent across the board. The paper is cautious about the reach of that conclusion. Thirty-six samples is a large replication and a small sample of the world’s settings, and eleven non-American samples are not a world survey. What the comparison undermines is a particular excuse. It is harder, after Many Labs 1, to explain a failed replication of one of these effects by saying that the new participants were students, or were online, or were not American, at least within the range of samples the project actually ran. The effect itself, rather than the laboratory’s local atmosphere, was the main determinant of whether the result appeared.

That finding has a constructive implication the authors emphasise. If variation across reasonable settings is modest for a given effect, then a sufficiently large, well-designed replication in one or a few sites tells you most of what a many-labs project would tell you. The many-labs design is expensive. It is the right instrument when the claim under test is precisely a claim about variation, or when a finding is so important, and so disputed, that only a coordinated estimate will settle it. It is not the only instrument. Many Labs 1 was as much a demonstration of method as a set of thirteen results, and later many-labs projects have reused the method on other effects, including further work on framing.

What this does and does not certify

The limitations are built into the design and should be kept attached to the headline. The thirteen effects were chosen partly for administrative convenience, so the success rate is not an estimate of the replicability of psychology, or even of social psychology. Effects that require elaborate staging, deception over long periods, or special populations were outside the project’s reach, and those are among the effects the replication crisis has treated most harshly. Within anchoring, what was replicated is a particular, explicit, two-step paradigm on factual questions. The result does not by itself certify every commercial claim about incidental numbers, and it does not re-run the original wheel-of-fortune study with its particular cover story. What it does is place one standard anchoring result among the findings that survive a change of laboratory, of country, and of recruitment method. Flag priming and currency priming, run in the same sessions, show that the project was capable of reporting a failure. Anchoring’s success is more credible because of those failures.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on anchoring cites Many Labs 1 as the evidence that anchoring is one of the more robust effects in the judgement literature, and it pairs that citation with the caution that incidental anchors, the ones people barely notice, have produced smaller and less consistent effects. The paper supports both halves. The anchoring it replicated was the explicit kind, and it replicated across thirty-six samples and thousands of participants, while two priming effects in the same design did not replicate at all. The card’s other references, Furnham and Boo’s review and the Englich sentencing studies, sit at different levels. The review maps the literature. The sentencing studies ask whether experts in a consequential task are moved by an anchor they know is random. Many Labs asks whether a basic version of the phenomenon is real outside the original laboratory. Read it for the design as much as for the result: the same protocol, many sites, a pre-committed set of effects, and a willingness to report which of them the sites could not find. Anchoring was one they found.
