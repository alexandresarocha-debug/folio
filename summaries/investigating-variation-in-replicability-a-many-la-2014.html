In 2014 Richard Klein and a large group of coauthors reported the first Many Labs project. Thirty-six separate samples, 6,344 people in all, each tried the same set of 13 published effects. Twenty-seven samples were in a lab and nine were online. Twenty-five were in the United States and eleven were elsewhere. Each sample had to collect at least 80 people and follow a shared script.

The largest effects were four anchoring questions taken from Jacowitz and Kahneman. People saw a number that was plainly too high or too low, then estimated a quantity. One question was the height of Mount Everest. All 36 samples found the same pull. Combined and weighted by sample size, that result was a Cohen's d of 2.23 on 5,627 people, a very large effect. A question about how many babies are born in the United States in a day was larger still, d = 2.42 on 5,609 people, and again every sample found it. The distance from San Francisco to New York was the smallest of the four and still large, d = 1.17 on 5,362 people, significant in every sample.

Two other effects in the same package did not survive. A faint picture of the American flag, from a study by Carter, Ferguson, and Hassin, did not make people more conservative. A background of dollar bills, from a study by Caruso, Vohs, Baxter, and Waytz, did not make people more accepting of the way society is arranged. For each of those, the combined effect sat near zero. The one flag result that reached significance pointed the wrong way. A third effect, merely imagining contact with an outsider group, was weak: it came out in the predicted direction in only 4 of the 36 samples.

For anyone using these findings outside a lab, the split is the useful part. A number you are asked to judge against still moves an estimate, in students and in online samples, in the United States and outside it. A picture slipped into the background of a page is a different kind of claim, and in this project that kind failed. Ten of the 13 effects did replicate, and which one it was mattered far more than whether the test was run in a classroom or on a website.

Source. Klein, R. A., et al. (2014). Investigating Variation in Replicability: A "Many Labs" Replication Project. Social Psychology, 45(3), 142–152. https://doi.org/10.1027/1864-9335/a000178
