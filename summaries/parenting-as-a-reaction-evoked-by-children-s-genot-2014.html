Most research on parenting asks how parents shape their children. Reut Avinun and Ariel Knafo at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem asked the reverse: how much do children shape their parents?

Twins make this testable. Parents raising twins are the same people, in the same home, at the same time. If they treat identical twins more alike than non-identical twins, something about the children's genes is drawing out the parenting. Studies built this way are called children-as-twins designs.

Avinun and Knafo pooled 32 such studies of parenting. Across them, about 23% of the differences in how parents treated their children was linked to the children's genes. This is the evocative kind of gene-environment correlation: a child's inherited traits, such as temperament, call out a certain style of parenting.

The rest was environment. About 43% was shared, meaning parents tended to treat both twins in the same way. The other 34% was not shared, meaning parents also treated each twin differently for reasons unrelated to genes.

The figures were not fixed. Results depended on how parenting was measured, on the children's age, and on which aspect of parenting was studied. Each of these made a significant difference.

What this means in real life: when a warm parent has a cheerful child, it is tempting to credit the parent. Some of the warmth may be the child's doing. The authors argue that studies treating parenting as the only cause need to be read again with this in mind.

Caution: these studies use the same twin assumptions as all twin research, including that identical twins are not treated more alike for reasons other than their genes. That assumption is exactly what is being measured here, so the logic needs care. Results also changed with the way parenting was measured, so 23% is an average across very different methods.

Source. Avinun, R., and Knafo, A. (2014). Parenting as a reaction evoked by children's genotype: a meta-analysis of children-as-twins studies. Personality and Social Psychology Review, 18(1), 87-102. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868313498308
