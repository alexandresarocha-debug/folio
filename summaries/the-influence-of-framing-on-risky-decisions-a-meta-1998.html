The result Anton Kühberger set out to measure was already famous. A disease is expected to kill 600 people. Told that a program will save 200 for sure, most people take it over a one-third chance of saving everyone. Told that 400 will die for sure, most people gamble. In the original classroom test, 72 percent of 152 students chose the sure saving, and 78 percent of 155 students chose the gamble when the same programs were worded as deaths. Kühberger asked how large that kind of shift is once you stop looking at the single dramatic demonstration.

He pooled 136 papers, nearly 30,000 participants, and calculated 230 effect sizes. The weighted average difference between the gain wording and the loss wording was a Cohen's d of 0.31, small to moderate. The shift was real, and it was not one study's accident. It was also not uniform. It was larger when the wording moved the reference point, as the disease problem does, by making the same outcomes look like gains in one form and losses in the other. It was smaller when researchers only made one feature of an outcome more salient. It was larger when people had to choose than when they rated or judged. It did not matter much whether the participants were students or the people who actually face that kind of decision, or whether one person answered or a group did.

For a consent form, an investment option, or a public-health message, the practical reading is narrower than the classroom percentages. Wording a risky option as lives saved rather than deaths does move people toward the sure thing, and the reverse wording moves them toward the gamble. The typical move is a modest one, not a swing from 72 percent to 22 percent. A message that merely stresses the upside, without changing what counts as the starting point, should be expected to do less. And seeing both wordings, which a within-subjects design approximates, shrinks the gap, which is a reason to write the choice both ways before treating either answer as the patient's preference.

Later counts put the average higher, not lower. Steiger and Kühberger, reanalyzing the literature in 2018 and correcting for publication bias, got a d of 0.52. The 2014 Many Labs project, which could not bury a null result, found a weighted d of 0.60 for the disease problem on 6,271 people. The 1998 average was the cautious figure. The effect did not go away.
