Background

After 1981, framing was easy to demonstrate and easy to doubt. A lecturer could reverse a room with the Asian disease problem. A sceptic could answer that one vignette, however neat, is a thin basis for a law of choice. Through the 1980s and 1990s, laboratories produced a crowd of variants: lives and money, sure things and gambles, wordings that matched Tversky and Kahneman's originals and wordings that only resembled them. Some variants moved choices a great deal. Some barely moved them. Anton Kühberger's 1998 paper, The Influence of Framing on Risky Decisions: A Meta-analysis, is the attempt to say what that crowd adds up to. Folio's framing card relies on his conclusion. Framing effects are reliable, and they are sensitive to the details of the task.

A meta-analysis is not another disease problem. It is a method for putting many problems on one scale, so that the field can see a typical effect and the conditions that enlarge or shrink it. The paper matters because it converts a famous demonstration into a qualified generalisation. Reliable is the part that answers the sceptic who thought framing was a parlour trick. Sensitive is the part that answers the enthusiast who thought any adjective would do.

The question

Across the published experiments on risky choice, does a gain frame systematically favour the sure option and a loss frame the risky one, and if so, on which kinds of problem is that tilt large? Kühberger's question is about the literature as a whole. A single laboratory can always be unusual. A review that merely lists papers can always be led by the list's rhetoric. He asks for a quantitative summary, and then for a breakdown. Does it matter whether the two options are strictly identical under both descriptions, as they are in the Asian disease problem? Does it matter whether the frame is a frame of lives, of money, or of some other stake? Does it matter whether both options are reframed, or only the sure thing changes its clothing? Those are questions about boundary conditions, which is what a mature effect needs once its existence is no longer the news.

How the review was built

The paper gathers experiments in which people chose between a risky option and a sure or safer option, and in which that choice was presented under a positive frame and under a negative frame. Studies had to be framing studies in that definite sense, rather than any research in which someone mentioned the word frame. For each comparison that qualified, the review records the direction and the strength of the difference between the two wordings, and it records features of the task that might explain why one comparison is large and another is small. The method then averages in a way that gives more influence to more precise studies, and it asks whether the average depends on those recorded features.

This is a different labour from replication. A replication runs one protocol in a new sample. A meta-analysis takes the protocols researchers actually ran, with all their differences of wording, of participants and of stakes, and asks what is common. Its picture of framing is therefore a picture of a research practice as well as of a mind. If experimenters mostly copied the Asian disease problem, the average will look like that problem. If they wandered, the average will show the wandering. Kühberger's breakdowns are there to keep those two possibilities apart.

What the review concludes

The central conclusion is the one the card states. Framing effects on risky decisions are real. A gain frame pushes towards the sure option. A loss frame pushes towards the risk. The tilt is not an artefact of one famous text, because it appears across a body of studies. It is also not a constant of nature. Its size depends on how the task is built. Problems that preserve a strict equivalence between the two frames, so that only the reference point has changed, show the effect more cleanly than problems in which the wording alters the options themselves. The classic disease problem is a member of the clean class, which is why it became the textbook case and why a sloppy imitation should not be expected to match it.

Other details of the task move the result as well. The domain of the outcome, the way the risk is numerically described, and the extent to which both options rather than one of them are rewritten all change how much the choice shifts. Kühberger's service to the reader is to make that sensitivity a finding rather than a complaint. A literature in which every variant worked equally would be a literature that had not yet noticed its own mechanism. A literature in which the effect appears when the reference point genuinely moves, and fades when the manipulation is diluted, is a literature behaving as prospect theory said it should.

How to read a qualified effect

Reliable but sensitive is a harder slogan than framing always works, and it is the accurate one. Reliability means that a reader who meets a well-built framing experiment should expect the gain frame and the loss frame to pull in opposite directions, more often than chance would pull them. Sensitivity means that the reader should ask, before being impressed by a null or by a huge shift, whether the two descriptions were truly the same prospect. A null on a botched pair of wordings is not a refutation of Tversky and Kahneman. A huge shift on a pair that also changed the incentives is not a pure framing effect. The meta-analysis is a machine for enforcing that distinction across papers that enforced it unevenly.

Read this way, the review also explains why framing survived the later replication crisis better than many social-psychological effects of similar age. The card notes that the Asian disease effect replicated in the Many Labs projects. Kühberger, writing earlier, had already described an effect that was dispersed across many laboratories and many wordings, and that depended on a structural feature, the reference point, rather than on a delicate piece of staging. Effects with that profile were the ones the crisis tended to leave standing. His paper is not a replication project. It is one reason the card can call the effect reliable without calling it unbounded.

Limits

A meta-analysis can only summarise the studies it can find, and the studies researchers publish are not a random sample of the studies they run. If framing experiments that worked were more likely to be written up than framing experiments that failed, the published average is too kind. Kühberger's era had fewer registered protocols than the decade that followed, so that kindness cannot be ruled out by the method. The right use of his conclusion is comparative. Within the published record, the effect is consistent enough to take seriously, and variable enough that task details are part of the result. It is not a measurement of the effect that would appear if every framing study ever begun were in the file.

There is a limit of scope in the title itself. The review is about risky decisions, the choice between a gamble and a safer alternative. Framing in the wider sense, a slogan, a photograph, a default on a form, is a larger country. This paper does not license a claim about all of it. It licenses a claim about the risky-choice paradigm that began with the disease problem and spread through money, medicine and the rest of the experimental menu.

What later work added

Later large-scale replications asked a sharper version of Kühberger's question: take the classic problem, run it the same way in many places, and see if the tilt is still there. The card's answer, via the Many Labs projects, is that the Asian disease effect is among the findings that held. That answer sits comfortably beside a meta-analysis that had already called the effect real and fussy. Fussiness is not fragility. Fragility is an effect that dies when a new laboratory follows the recipe. Fussiness is an effect that dies when the recipe is changed. Kühberger's contribution is the map of which changes matter.

The review also leaves a theoretical residue. Prospect theory predicts the direction of the tilt and the importance of the reference point. It does not, by itself, rank every moderator a meta-analysis can code. Some of the sensitivity Kühberger documents is a direct fingerprint of the theory. Some of it is a fingerprint of experimental habit, of the way problems are photocopied and lightly edited. Telling those fingerprints apart is the work his breakdowns begin, and it is not finished by a single paper in 1998.

Why it matters here

The framing card would be incomplete if it stopped at the demonstration. Tversky and Kahneman showed, in the 1981 paper, that one carefully built pair of wordings can reverse a majority. McNeil and colleagues showed that physicians are moved by a survival frame and a mortality frame in a real disease. Kühberger shows what the wider literature does with that discovery. The effect is reliable enough to teach, and sensitive enough that the teaching has to include the task, not only the slogan. Read the 1981 paper for the mechanism in its pure form. Read this review for the scope, and for the reasons a framing result can be both famous and uneven.
