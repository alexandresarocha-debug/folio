Stanley Milgram’s 1974 book, Obedience to Authority, is the full series, not the single famous percentage. In the basic setup a man from the New Haven area was told to shock a learner for each mistake on a memory task. The generator ran from 15 to 450 volts. Forty men were tested in each condition. In the remote condition, learner in another room and no voice, 65 percent went to 450 volts. With the learner’s voice coming through the wall, 62.5 percent did. When the learner sat a few feet away in the same room, 40 percent did. When, from 150 volts on, the teacher had to force the learner’s hand down onto a shock plate, 30 percent did. Seeing and touching the person lowered the rate. It did not bring it near zero. In each of those conditions a large minority still went to the top of the board.

Two other changes cut deeper. When the experimenter gave the first instructions and then left, continuing by telephone, 9 men stayed obedient to the end, against 26 when he sat a few feet away. Some of the men on the phone quietly gave lower shocks than they had been told to and assured the experimenter they were following the procedure. In another condition the real participant watched two peers refuse. Those confederates went along until the learner’s protest at 150 volts, then one walked away from the generator, and the second quit at 210 volts. After that, 36 of the 40 real participants defied the experimenter. Milgram wrote that none of his variations undercut the experimenter’s authority as effectively as those two refusals. Moving the same procedure to a plain office in Bridgeport, under a made-up firm called Research Associates of Bridgeport, left 48 percent fully obedient, against 65 percent in the matching condition at Yale.

The practical reading is that obedience in this laboratory was a property of the arrangement. Distance from the person being hurt, distance from the person giving the order, and the sight of someone else refusing each changed the count. A policy, a hospital order, or a military instruction that can only be questioned in private, with the authority still in the room and no colleague willing to speak first, matches the conditions where Milgram’s rate stayed high. A visible refusal by a peer matches the condition where it collapsed.

Milgram estimated that in each condition two to four men did not believe they were giving painful shocks, and he left them in the results. In the voice-feedback condition, among the men who accepted the cover story, about 60 percent were still fully obedient. Belief in the shocks does not erase the finding. It does cap how many of the obedient men can be described as sure they were torturing someone.
