The argument in outline

Obedience to Authority, published in 1974, is Stanley Milgram's full account of the research that the 1963 paper had only opened. The book is an experimental report stretched into an argument. It describes the method, the baseline result, and then the variations that show the result is not a single number. It offers a theory, the agentic state, for why a person who is distressed and free to leave nevertheless stays and harms. It answers the charge that the laboratory has nothing to say about history. Folio's card says the book is more nuanced than the textbook summary, and that a key detail in it sits awkwardly with the idea of blind orders. The walkthrough follows the book's own order: the situation, the variations, the theory, and the historical claim.

The situation he built

Milgram begins with the moral puzzle that sent him into the laboratory. How do large harms get done by people who are not monsters and who, in other parts of life, keep ordinary scruples? His answer starts by building a situation in which the scruple and the instruction conflict, and in which the harm is visible to the person doing it. A volunteer comes to a scientific study of memory. A rigged draw makes him the teacher. A confederate is the learner, strapped into a chair. The teacher is to punish mistakes with shocks from a board that runs from 15 volts to 450, the upper end marked as dangerous. The learner's protests are a script. The experimenter, in a lab coat, uses a fixed series of prods if the teacher falters, from a mild request to continue to a final statement that he has no choice and must go on.

In the condition that became famous, the learner is in another room and can be heard. Of the forty men, twenty-six went to the maximum. Milgram spends pages on how they looked while they did it. They sweated, laughed strangely, protested, and continued. He wants the reader to understand the behaviour as obedience against conviction, not as hidden cruelty uncovered. He also reports how badly experts predicted the result. People who heard the procedure described expected almost everyone to stop. The book's first move is to make that expectation the thing that needs explaining. If we cannot imagine the obedience, we will keep looking for monsters after each new atrocity, and we will keep being surprised.

The variations

The centre of the book is the set of changes to the situation. Milgram treats them as the real experiment. The baseline says that obedience can be high. The variations say what it depends on. When the learner was brought closer, so that the teacher could see him, and closer still, so that the teacher had to press the learner's hand onto a shock plate, obedience fell. Proximity made the victim harder to put out of mind. When the experimenter was physically removed and gave his instructions by telephone, obedience fell, and some participants quietly delivered lower shocks than they reported. Distance weakened the authority more than it weakened the task. When the study was moved out of Yale to a modest office in a nearby city, obedience was somewhat lower and still substantial. Prestige mattered, and it was not the whole story.

The group variations cut deeper. When the participant was one of several teachers and the others, confederates, refused to continue, most real participants refused too. A peer's defiance gave a model and made defiance thinkable. When two experimenters disagreed with each other about whether to go on, obedience collapsed. An authority contradicted by an equal authority is no longer a clear definition of what the situation requires. When the participant was free to choose the shock level, the shocks stayed low. The men were not looking for an excuse to harm. Given a way to choose, they chose mildly. Women, in the condition Milgram ran with them, obeyed at rates similar to men. The book uses that result to block the easy comfort that the baseline was something about the particular men of 1961. Across the variations, the same person-shaped volunteer behaves very differently as the structure changes. That is the empirical heart of the argument, and it is the part a summary that quotes only 65 per cent has dropped.

The agentic state

Milgram's theory is an attempt to say what the obedient participant has become. He distinguishes an autonomous mode, in which a person experiences himself as responsible for his own acts, from an agentic mode, in which he experiences himself as an instrument of another person's will. The shift, as Milgram describes it, is a normal human capacity. Hierarchies require it. An employee, a soldier, a student, all spend parts of the day carrying out purposes that are not privately theirs, and social life would be impossible if they did not. The laboratory, on this account, recruits that capacity. The participant enters as an autonomous volunteer, accepts a role inside a legitimate enterprise, and then finds that leaving the role feels like a breach while staying in it feels like doing his job. Milgram calls the forces that make entry easy and exit hard the binding factors: politeness, the agreement already given, the awkwardness of calling a scientist a torturer, the tiny size of each next step.

Strain is the other half of the theory. The agentic state does not abolish the participant's values. It sets them against the role, and the result is the sweating and the protests. Milgram describes the ways people manage the strain without breaking out. They dispute while complying. They narrow their attention to the mechanics of the word pairs. They shove responsibility up, to the experimenter, or out, onto the learner who is making mistakes. Some refuse, and the refusal is the strain winning. The theory's prediction is that strain rises when the victim is close and when the authority is weak or divided, which is the pattern the variations show. A reader can find the phrase "agentic state" too grand for the evidence and still use the machinery underneath it. Binding factors plus mounting strain plus a legitimate frame is a good description of the sessions, whether or not one wants a special mental state to house it.

History, and what he claimed to explain

The book's outer chapters face the question Milgram was always asked. What does a paid hour in New Haven have to do with a bureaucracy of murder? His answer is deliberately limited and still ambitious. He does not say that his participants would have staffed a camp, or that Americans and Germans are the same, or that orders are the only thing an atrocity needs. He says that a recognisable process, the surrender of personal responsibility to a legitimate authority inside a structured task, is available in ordinary people, and that this process is one ingredient of organised harm. He engages Hannah Arendt's report on Eichmann and the phrase "the banality of evil" as a neighbouring claim, while insisting that his own contribution is experimental rather than reportorial. The laboratory shows the ingredient in a pure and small form. History shows what the ingredient can be combined with: ideology, distance, career, propaganda, a victim already defined as outside the moral circle.

He also insists that obedience is often a virtue. The same handover of initiative that produces a dangerous teacher produces an ambulance crew and a fire drill. The moral question is not whether people can take a role. They can, and must. The question is what the role is for, who is watching, and whether the binding factors have been arranged so that exit is impossible just when exit is the right act. The book ends as a warning about the design of institutions, not as a sneer at human character. Milgram's participants did what the design made easy. A different design, the variations have already shown, makes a different act easy.

Why it matters here

Folio's card says the book is more nuanced than the textbook summary, and the variations are the nuance. Obedience was high when the authority was present, the victim was distant, and the participant was alone. It fell when the victim was close, when the authority gave orders down a phone line, when the setting was less august, and when peers refused. It collapsed when authorities disagreed. It was not what people chose when the shock level was theirs to pick. The 65 per cent is one cell of that table. Quoting it without the table turns a study of conditions into a claim about a national character.

The card adds a detail that later readers, Haslam and Reicher among them, have made central. The prod that sounds most like a direct order, the one that tells the participant he has no other choice and must go on, was typically the prod that failed. People who had continued through appeals to the needs of the experiment often stopped when the appeal became a naked command. That pattern fits the book's own emphasis on legitimacy and on a shared scientific task more tightly than it fits a picture of automatic submission to orders. Haslam and Reicher call the better-fitting picture engaged followership: the participant stays in order to help a cause he has accepted. The agentic-state chapters can be read as struggling towards that picture and also as over-stating the switch in the person. Read the variations either way. They are the part of Milgram the card most wants in hand, because they show a behaviour that is powerful, conditional, and sensitive to the presence of a single ally.
