Norbert Schwarz, Herbert Bless, Fritz Strack, and three colleagues asked students to list examples of their own behavior and then rate how assertive they were. The twist was the length of the list. Forty women at a German university were asked for either 6 examples or 12. A pretest had shown that 6 felt easy and 12 felt hard. They also rated how hard the list had been: 5.1 for six examples and 7.2 for twelve, on a scale from 1 to 10.

The self-ratings went with the feeling, not with the number of examples. Women who listed assertive acts rated themselves 6.3 after six examples and 5.2 after twelve, on a combined scale from 1 to 10. Women who listed unassertive acts showed the mirror image: 5.2 after six examples and 6.2 after twelve. The group that had produced the most evidence of assertiveness came out feeling less assertive, because the long list had been a struggle. With 9 or 10 women in each cell, that crossover was only marginally significant. A second study, with 158 students at a teachers' college, found the same pattern more clearly, on a scale from 1 to 9: 5.2 versus 4.7 for assertive lists of 6 and 12, and 4.7 versus 5.3 for unassertive lists.

A third study, with 78 women, showed when the feeling stops counting as news about the self. They listed the examples while listening to meditation music. Some were told the music would make assertive memories easy to recall, and some were told it would make unassertive memories easy. When the music was a plausible cause of the ease or the difficulty, the pattern reversed: a long list of assertive acts produced a higher self-rating than a short one. When the music did not explain the feeling away, six assertive examples still scored 5.6 and twelve scored 4.5, on a scale from 1 to 9.

The practical version is familiar. Asked for reasons to take a job, leave a city, or trust a doctor, a short list that comes quickly feels like a strong case. A long list that stalls feels like a weak one, even though it contains more reasons. If you already know why the list is hard, a bad week, a distracting room, a form that asks for too many lines, the struggle is worse evidence. The content still matters. It matters most when producing it felt easy.
