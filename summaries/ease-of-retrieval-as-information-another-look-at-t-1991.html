Background

By 1991 the availability heuristic was nearly twenty years old. Tversky and Kahneman had argued that people judge frequency and probability by the ease with which examples come to mind. Textbooks treated the idea as a bias about content. If you can recall many plane crashes, plane crashes feel common. The content of memory was doing the work, and ease was simply how that content announced itself. Norbert Schwarz, Herbert Bless, Fritz Strack and their colleagues thought this reading was incomplete. The experience of ease or difficulty is itself a piece of information, and it can contradict the examples just retrieved.

The distinction matters because the two cues usually point the same way. A large class is easy to sample, so content and ease agree, and nobody can tell which one was used. To separate them, a study has to make content and ease point in opposite directions. That is the design of this paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, and it is why Folio's availability card treats the work as a refinement rather than as a rival theory.

The question

When people recall examples of their own behaviour and then judge themselves, do they consult the examples, or do they consult how hard the recall felt? If content is all that matters, listing more examples of assertiveness should make a person feel more assertive. If ease matters, a request that makes recall feel difficult should push the self-judgement the other way, even though more examples have been brought to mind. A further question follows. If the feeling of difficulty can be explained away, as the side effect of something irrelevant, do people abandon it and fall back on the content?

How the studies were set up

The basic experiment asked students at a German university to recall examples of their own behaviour. Some recalled assertive acts, times they had spoken up or taken a stand. Others recalled unassertive acts. Crossed with that, some were asked for six examples and some for twelve. Six examples of one's own life are usually easy to find. Twelve are a struggle. After the recall, people rated their own assertiveness. The prediction from a content-only view is a simple one: more assertive examples, a higher self-rating; more unassertive examples, a lower one. The prediction from an ease view is a crossover. Twelve assertive examples, being difficult, should make the person feel less assertive than six. Twelve unassertive examples, also difficult, should make the person feel less unassertive, which is to say more assertive, than six.

A later experiment added a misattribution. People did the same recall while a piece of meditation music played. Some were told that the music would make it easier to remember the kind of episode they had been asked for. Others were told that the music would make that kind of episode harder to remember. The music is irrelevant to how assertive anyone actually is. Its only job is to offer an explanation for the feeling of ease or difficulty. Where the feeling matches what the music was said to do, the feeling is no longer diagnostic of the self, and people should turn to the content of what they recalled. Where the feeling contradicts the cover story, the feeling should seem especially informative, and the original crossover should remain.

What they found

The crossover appeared. People asked for six assertive examples rated themselves more assertive than people asked for twelve. People asked for six unassertive examples rated themselves less assertive than people asked for twelve. The group that had produced the longer list of assertive acts, which is the group with the most confirming content in hand, came out feeling comparatively unassertive. Difficulty had outweighed the examples. The mirror image held for unassertive acts. Struggling to find many instances of weakness made people feel less defined by weakness.

The music study showed that the feeling is used only when it seems to be about the self. When the experienced ease or difficulty matched the alleged effect of the music, and could therefore be set aside as a side effect, self-ratings followed the content instead. More assertive examples then meant a higher rating, which is what a content theory had predicted all along. When the feeling ran against the cover story, people treated it as news about themselves, and the ease pattern returned. The subjective experience was not a passive glow accompanying memory. It was a cue, and like other cues it could be discounted when a plausible external cause was supplied.

How the authors read the results

Schwarz and his colleagues argued that Tversky and Kahneman had been right about the importance of accessibility, and wrong if accessibility is read only as the content that arrives. People also notice the experience of bringing that content to mind. When the experience and the content agree, as they do in ordinary life, the distinction is invisible. When a request makes a large sample feel difficult, the experience reverses the implication of the sample. Availability, on this reading, is ease of retrieval used as information, not the mere presence of retrieved items.

They placed the result in a wider family of feelings-as-information. Mood, the feeling of familiarity, and the feeling of effort can all be consulted as if they were evidence, and all of them can be discounted if an irrelevant cause is made obvious. The practical sting is that asking someone for many reasons, many risks, or many examples of a virtue can backfire. Past a point, the struggle becomes the message. A person who has just failed to list twelve reasons for a belief may hold the belief less confidently than a person who listed three and stopped while it still felt easy.

Limits

The studies use a particular judgement, a rating of one's own assertiveness, made immediately after a recall task that the experimenter designed to be easy or hard. That is a fair test of the cue. It is not a census of every judgement availability is supposed to explain. The samples were students, the behaviour was self-reported rather than observed, and the music cover story is the sort of instruction that works in a laboratory and is hard to reproduce in a newspaper or a doctor's surgery. The paper shows that ease can dominate content under these conditions. It does not show that content never dominates, or that people always notice how hard a memory felt.

There is also a boundary the design itself reveals. When ease was explained away, content came back. The heuristic is not a compulsion. It is a default use of a feeling, abandoned when the feeling is no longer trusted. Any strong claim that people are prisoners of retrieval fluency has to reckon with that flexibility. The same person, minutes later, can be using the opposite cue, because the experimenter has changed what the feeling seems to mean.

Replications and critiques

The ease-of-retrieval pattern has been repeated across a range of judgements, including attitudes, health beliefs and assessments of other people, and the misattribution logic has travelled with it. That is the result's strength. It is also the source of a fair criticism. Once any feeling can be cast as information, the account becomes easy to apply after the fact. A finding that follows content can be called a case where ease was discounted. A finding that contradicts content can be called a case where ease was used. The original paper is protected against that circularity by its experimental control: ease was manipulated by the number of examples requested, and the diagnosticity of the feeling was manipulated by the cover story. Later applications are only as good as their ability to say, in advance, which cue should win.

A second caution is about size and context. The classic crossover is a laboratory effect with a task that makes difficulty unmistakable. In the wild, people rarely try to list twelve episodes on command, and a vivid single example can still outweigh a laborious search. Ease of retrieval qualifies availability. It does not retire the older point that the content of what comes to mind, especially a single dramatic case, moves judgement. The right summary is that both cues are real, that they can be set against each other, and that people have some ability to decide which one they are using when a reason to doubt the feeling is put in front of them.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on availability says that what comes to mind feels more common, and then adds the twist that makes the idea precise. People asked for twelve examples of their own assertive behaviour rated themselves as less assertive than people asked for six, because twelve felt hard. This paper is that twist. It keeps Tversky and Kahneman's insight that memory is used as a frequency meter, and it changes what is being read on the meter. The card's practical advice, to ask for a base rate rather than for a story, is stronger once you see that asking for more stories can make the class feel smaller. Read the 1973 paper for the heuristic. Read this one for the demonstration that effort, and not only content, is what the heuristic consults.
