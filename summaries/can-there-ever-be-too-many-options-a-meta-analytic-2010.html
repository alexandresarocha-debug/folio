By 2010, the idea that more choice can overwhelm people was everywhere, but some studies found no such effect, and some even found that people liked having more options. Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd decided to add everything up. Their meta-analysis appeared in the Journal of Consumer Research.

They collected 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments, with 5,036 people in total. For each experiment they compared a small and a large set of options on the outcome the researchers had measured, such as satisfaction, willingness to buy or confidence in the choice. Then they averaged across studies.

The average effect was virtually zero. That does not mean nothing happens in any experiment. It means that some studies found that large sets hurt, some found that they helped, and these cancelled. There was considerable variance between studies, so something differed from one study to the next. The authors looked for factors that explained the pattern. They found several that seemed to be preconditions for choice overload, but no condition that was sufficient to produce it every time. They also pointed out that individual studies had suggested special conditions, which might explain when and why overload occurs reliably, and they reviewed these as ideas for future research.

What this means in real life: the claim that fewer options is always better is not supported. If you design a menu, a shop or a form, the slogan is a weak guide. The evidence says to look at your own situation: what people are choosing, how well they know what they want, and how easy the options are to compare.

Criticism: this review was itself challenged. The 2015 meta-analysis by Chernev and colleagues used 99 observations and argued that once the right moderators are taken into account the effect is real.

So the fair summary is mixed: the headline effect is not a law, and the average of the published and unpublished studies together is close to nothing.

Source. Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., and Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409-425. https://doi.org/10.1086/651235
