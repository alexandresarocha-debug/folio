In 1703, Louis-Armand de Lom d'Arce, Baron de Lahontan, published dialogues between a character with his own name and a man called Adario. A note in the book identifies Adario with Kandiaronk, a Wendat statesman who lived from about 1649 to 1701. Across five conversations, on religion, law, happiness, medicine, and marriage, Adario mocks European punishment, private property, and the habit of obeying people who do not work. David Graeber and David Wengrow argue that this is not a French aristocrat putting clever lines in a "savage's" mouth. They argue that the basic case was Kandiaronk's, that he had seen France, and that this Indigenous critique of European inequality fed arguments the Enlightenment later treated as its own.

They make a similar claim with ruins. At Teotihuacan, in central Mexico, they describe a huge city whose usual signs of a Mesoamerican divine king are hard to find, and where a great share of the people lived in apartment compounds rather than under a palace household. They read that absence as collective government, even a republican tradition, and they set it against the assumption that a city of that size must have had a king. The larger argument is that there was no one-way ladder from foraging to farming to hierarchy. People tried out different political arrangements, kept some, and dropped others. A big settlement was not a sentence to live under a permanent boss.

That changes what a person is entitled to assume about the past, and about the present. If inequality and the state were the automatic price of farming, then asking for a different arrangement looks naive. If cities and farms have been run in more than one way, the narrow choice between a violent free-for-all and a single sovereign is a story later writers told, not a report of what people actually did. The useful question, when someone says a hierarchy is natural, is which hierarchy, chosen by whom, and what the neighbors who refused it were eating that year.

The Kandiaronk claim has been challenged on the documents. Robert Twiss, writing in 2024, went back through the evidence that Kandiaronk visited France and concluded that the visit is unlikely. Without that visit, Lahontan's dialogues are much harder to treat as a near-transcript of one man's views. They can still be part of a wider record in which Indigenous people criticized European society. They are not, on Twiss's reading, the word-for-word source Graeber and Wengrow want. On Teotihuacan, the archaeologist Michael E. Smith noted in 2022 that the collective-government reading is shared by many specialists, and that the book simply leaves out archaeologists, Saburo Sugiyama among them, who argue the city was ruled more autocratically.
