Against the ladder

David Graeber and David Wengrow's The Dawn of Everything, published in 2021, is a long attack on what they take to be the standard story of human political origins. The story, as they reconstruct it, runs from small egalitarian bands of foragers, through the invention of farming, to the rise of hierarchy, cities, and the state, with the whole sequence treated as both inevitable and, past a certain point, irreversible. Hobbes supplies the version in which the state saves us from a brutal freedom. Rousseau supplies the version in which the state, and farming before it, costs us an original freedom. Later writers, Jared Diamond among them, supply a scientific-sounding version in which geography and agriculture push societies up the same ladder. Graeber and Wengrow's opening claim is that the ladder is false, and that it is false in a way that makes us stupider about freedom. The archaeological and ethnographic record, they argue, shows people experimenting with political forms for thousands of years. Large settlements without kings, societies that changed their social order with the season, and cities that do not fit the model of an oriental despotism are not colourful exceptions. They are the evidence that a single track never existed.

Schismogenesis, seasons, and the three freedoms

One of their organising ideas is schismogenesis, borrowed from the anthropologist Gregory Bateson. Neighbouring peoples define themselves against each other. A society that sees a neighbour becoming hierarchical may deliberately cultivate an ethic of refusal, and the neighbour may answer by becoming more hierarchical still. Difference is not only adaptation to an environment. It is a choice made in awareness of an alternative next door. Graeber and Wengrow use this to break the assumption that each society's form is the form its ecology required. People have known about other ways of living for a very long time, and they have sometimes organised their institutions as a rejection of those ways.

The seasonal argument is their most concrete ethnographic exhibit. Drawing on accounts of peoples of the Americas and elsewhere, they argue that the same group could live part of the year in a strict, even authoritarian order, gathered for a hunt or a ceremony, and part of the year in a loose and egalitarian one. Hierarchy was not a door that, once opened, locked. It was a mode that could be entered and left. If that is true of recent societies with seasonal rounds, the authors invite the reader to take seriously the possibility that prehistoric societies did it too. The archaeological signature of a rich burial or a monument would then be the signature of a season, a phase, or a faction, not proof that a permanent state had been founded. This is a powerful interpretive suggestion. It is also, as they acknowledge by the way they argue, an inference from later ethnography back into a past that did not leave minutes of its meetings.

They name three freedoms which they think were widely understood and variously protected. The freedom to move away from a political arrangement one dislikes. The freedom to disobey a command. And the freedom to imagine, and to enact, a different social order. A society that keeps these freedoms open can be large and complex without being a state in the later sense. A society that closes them, by tying people to land, monopolising violence, and treating the order as cosmic, is the kind of society the ladder calls a success and which they regard as one option among others, often a grim one. The three freedoms are the book's moral vocabulary. They are the standard by which the case studies are judged.

Cities without the inevitable king

The central archaeological chapters apply that standard to early large settlements. Graeber and Wengrow gather cases which, on their reading, show scale without the kind of hereditary domination the standard story requires. They discuss the monumental enclosures of the early Near East, including Göbekli Tepe, as evidence that large coordinated projects and rich ritual life preceded farming, which reverses the usual order in which agriculture creates the surplus that creates the monument. They discuss the huge settlements of the Ukrainian mega-sites, associated with the Trypillia tradition, as places where tens of thousands of people may have lived in a planned layout without the palaces and royal tombs that would announce a king. They discuss Teotihuacan in Mexico as a city that may, in one long phase, have been governed without a singular ruler, and they discuss North American centres such as Poverty Point and the traditions around Cahokia as evidence of political experimentation rather than of a stalled march towards the state. Mesopotamia itself, the textbook birthplace of the state, is reread so that early cities are not automatically palaces with countryside attached.

The method in these chapters is to take the absence of royal iconography, the presence of collective infrastructure, and the variety of house plans as positive evidence for political forms that later categories miss. James Scott's work on early states, which treats grain agriculture as the basis of taxation and coercion, is a neighbour to this argument and not the same argument. Graeber and Wengrow share Scott's refusal of a happy ladder from farm to civilisation. They are less willing to treat the cereal state as the main line and everyone else as people who escaped it. They want the early city itself to be politically various, a place where different constitutions were tried, including constitutions a modern reader might recognise as civic rather than as despotic.

The indigenous critique

The chapters on the Enlightenment are the book's most distinctive historical claim. Graeber and Wengrow argue that indigenous Americans, in conversation with French missionaries and officials, developed a criticism of European society that Europeans then had to answer. The figure they put at the centre is Kandiaronk, a Wendat statesman whose views are known through the dialogues published by the Baron de Lahontan at the start of the eighteenth century. In those dialogues a Native speaker dissects European monarchy, money, and the obedience of the poor with a clarity that the authors regard as too pointed to be a simple piece of European ventriloquism. They argue that ideas of freedom and equality entered European argument partly through this criticism, and that the later habit of treating Rousseau's state of nature as a European fantasy erases the non-European interlocutors. The standard story, on this account, was invented in part to shut a door that indigenous political thought had opened.

The conclusion they draw for the present is that the question of political origins has been used to shrink the imagination. If hierarchy is the price of complexity, then any large society must accept domination as the rent it pays. If complexity has often existed without that rent, the rent is a political choice and can be refused. The Dawn of Everything is a prehistory written so that a different politics looks less like a utopia and more like a recovery of experiments people have already run.

What specialists have pushed back

The pushback has been as broad as the book. Archaeologists of the particular sites have argued that the political readings are more definite than the remains allow. Absence of a palace is not presence of an egalitarian constitution. A mega-site can be seasonal, sparsely occupied, or organised by authorities who did not bury themselves like Egyptian kings. Near Eastern specialists dispute the claim that monumental life before farming overturns the connection between surplus and hierarchy, rather than complicating its timing. Anthropologists have argued that the seasonal pattern, real in some cases, cannot be projected onto every prehistoric settlement that looks puzzling. The ladder the authors attack is also, some critics say, a ladder the profession had already abandoned, so that the book is aimed at a popular simplification and at a few famous popularisers rather than at current research.

The Kandiaronk chapters have drawn a separate and serious objection. Lahontan's dialogues are a literary work. How much of the voice is a Wendat statesman and how much is a French baron putting his own critique in a more persuasive mouth is a question about sources, and several historians of the Enlightenment think Graeber and Wengrow answer it too confidently. If the indigenous critique is over-read, a beautiful chapter of the book becomes a weaker foundation for the claim that Europe learned freedom from America. None of these objections returns the reader to a simple Hobbesian or Rousseauian track. They limit the licence with which any one site, or any one dialogue, can be made to carry a theory of human political nature. The book's durable contribution is the demand that deep history admit variety and conscious choice. Its vulnerable habit is to treat a suggestive site as a recovered constitution.

Why it matters here

The card diamond-geography presents Guns, Germs, and Steel as the geographic account of the human career, and it lists The Dawn of Everything among the challenges to that kind of single-track story. Graeber and Wengrow reject the ladder from geography and agriculture to hierarchy on which Diamond's chain depends, and they reject it with a file of societies that do not sit on the rungs. The card scott-against-grain presents James Scott's argument that the first states were fragile grain-and-tax machines rather than the natural end of farming, and it names this book as the extension of that argument towards a wider range of political experiments. The extension is real and it is not an echo. Scott's early state is a coercive exception. Graeber and Wengrow's deep past is a field of constitutions, some of them coercive and some of them not. Folio uses the book to keep both cards from hardening into a new single story, whether the single story is geographic destiny or the cereal state. The dawn they describe is untidy on purpose.
