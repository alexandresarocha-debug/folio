The '3.5% rule' is the claim that no government has withstood a challenge from 3.5% of its population mobilised against it at a peak event. In this discussion paper for Harvard's Carr Center for Human Rights Policy, Erica Chenoweth explains where the figure came from and what it does and does not mean.

The idea began with a question. At a workshop in 2013, activists asked her how many people it takes for a movement to win. Political scientist Mark Lichbach had speculated in his 1995 book The Rebel's Dilemma that no government could withstand a challenge from 5% of the population. Chenoweth turned to the NAVCO 1.1 data, dividing the number of participants at each campaign's largest event by the country's population in the campaign's final year. All campaigns that reached 3.5% had succeeded, and all of them were primarily nonviolent. She presented the finding in a TEDxBoulder talk that year.

She then adds cautions. The figure is a descriptive statistic based on past movements, not a recipe; trying to hit the threshold without building a broad constituency guarantees nothing. Such large movements are rare: in the newer NAVCO 1.2 data, only 17 of 169 nonviolent campaigns between 1945 and 2013 passed the threshold. Among nonviolent campaigns that succeeded, 83% did so without reaching 3.5%.

There are exceptions. The uprising against King Hamad in Bahrain from 2011 to 2014 reportedly drew 100,000 people to a major demonstration, over 6% of the population, and still appears to have failed. The rule, she concludes, is a tendency rather than a law, and other factors such as momentum, organisation and strategic leadership are likely as important as size.

What this means in real life: large participation is associated with success, but movements need strategy and organisation, not just a numerical target.

Caution: this is a short discussion paper rather than a peer-reviewed study, the participation counts are estimates, and the data cover campaigns seeking to overthrow governments or win independence, not other kinds of protest.
