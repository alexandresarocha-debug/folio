Lucy Aplin and colleagues at Oxford turned the milk-bottle story into a field experiment in Wytham Woods, where many great tits carry tiny transponder tags. They built an automated puzzle box baited with live mealworms. A sliding door could be pushed either way: the blue side to the right, or the red side to the left. Both worked equally well.

Two resident males were caught from each of eight sub-populations. In five, both were trained in captivity on one solution, two sub-populations on the blue side and three on the red; in three control sub-populations, neither was trained. The birds were released, and three puzzle boxes offering both options were set up in each area.

In the five seeded sub-populations the skill spread fast, reaching an average of 75% of local birds within 20 days of exposure; across the study, 508 birds performed 58,975 solutions. Far fewer birds learned in the control areas. Social network data from feeders showed that the skill travelled along social ties: a bird's learning rate rose about twelvefold per unit of association with birds that already knew the trick. Each area stuck heavily to the technique it had been seeded with.

The birds were conformists. Most that used both options kept to the local favourite, even after finding the other just as rewarding. Of 14 birds that moved between areas with different traditions, 10 switched to the new local technique. The following winter, when only about 40% of the birds were the same individuals, the boxes went back up in some areas and the local traditions reappeared.

What this means in real life: arbitrary local traditions, the bird equivalent of a regional custom, can arise and persist in wild animals, which helps explain how habits such as milk-bottle opening can sweep through a population.

Caution: great tits were drawn to an artificial, highly rewarding food source, and only one replicate of each condition was retested the next year. Conformity was inferred statistically from choices, and the experiment does not show how far such traditions persist without people providing the puzzle.
