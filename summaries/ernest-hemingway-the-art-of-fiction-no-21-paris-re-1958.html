An interview as a piece of prose

In the spring of 1958 The Paris Review published George Plimpton's conversation with Ernest Hemingway as The Art of Fiction No. 21. Hemingway was famous, Nobel already behind him, living at the Finca Vigía outside Havana, and long past the young man of the early stories. The interview belongs to a series in which writers talk about method. Like other interviews in that series, the published text is a shaped document. Questions and answers have been arranged into a readable sequence. What the reader gets is Hemingway willing, up to a point, to describe a working day, a standard of truth, and a principle of omission he had already sketched in Death in the Afternoon a quarter of a century earlier.

The voice is recognisable and also performed. He is concrete about tools and hours. He is evasive when a question asks him to become his own critic in public. He corrects anecdotes that have begun to circulate about his apprenticeship, including the idea that he had been a baseball writer for the Kansas City Star. The correction matters. A theory of writing built on a legend of the newsroom is, by his own account here, already a little false. He would rather talk about what he does in the morning when the writing is actually happening.

The morning, and the decision to stop

Asked about schedule, he describes beginning as soon after first light as he can, reading over what he has written, and going on from the place where he stopped. The stop is the part worth lingering on. He says he stops when he still knows what will happen next, while there is still juice in the work, and then he has to live through the wait until the next morning. The advice is practical and a little severe. Stopping in the middle of a known stretch means the next day begins with momentum. Stopping because you are empty means the next day begins with a search. He treats the search as the dangerous condition, the one in which a writer starts to decorate, explain, or fake.

He talks about rewriting in the same spirit. The last page of A Farewell to Arms, he tells Plimpton, was rewritten many times, thirty-nine times by his count, before he was satisfied. Pressed for the technical problem, he reduces it to getting the words right. The exchange is a small demonstration of the standard he is preaching. A reader hoping for a trick of plot is given a standard of the sentence instead. Elsewhere he is frank that financial security and ordinary health can help a writer, because worry destroys the reserves the work draws on. The interview is less a myth of inspired poverty than a description of protecting a morning.

The iceberg, stated again from the desk

Plimpton brings him to omission, and Hemingway restates the principle with the familiar image: seven eighths of the iceberg underwater for every part that shows. Anything you know, he says, you can eliminate, and the elimination can strengthen what remains. If you omit something because you do not know it, the ignorance shows as a hole. This is the same condition he had set in Death in the Afternoon. In the interview he ties it to a book of his own, The Old Man and the Sea. That book, he says, could have been enormous. It could have included the whole life of the village, everyone born and educated and set to work. He treats that kind of completeness as a task other writers have already done well. His own luck, as he puts it, was a good man and a good boy and the sea. He then lists knowledge he left out: the mating of marlin, a school of sperm whales in the same water, a whale he harpooned and lost, the stories he knows from the fishing village. The underwater part, in this telling, is a pile of things seen and then refused by the page.

He also tells the origin of Hills Like White Elephants as a story about looking. He had eaten oysters at Prunier, met a girl, understood that she had had an abortion, talked with her about other things, and then gone home and written the story that afternoon, missing lunch to do it. The interview presents the episode as evidence that observation continues when you are away from the desk, and that the thing you understand need not be the thing you discuss at the table. In the story, the operation is never named. The interview is more explicit than the story. Read together, they show the principle in both its phases: the knowledge first, the named fact withdrawn once the conversation on the page can carry it.

Observation, invention, and the detector

A thread about painting and about close looking runs through the answers. Hemingway describes training himself on details that carry emotion before anyone has explained them: the way an outfielder throws a glove, the squeak of resin, the colour of a man's skin and the old cuts on his face. These are sketches, he suggests, of the kind a painter makes, stored for later use. When Plimpton asks about personal knowledge, Hemingway pushes back against the idea that a writer only copies what he has done. A writer invents from what he knows personally and impersonally, and sometimes from knowledge he cannot fully account for. The interview keeps invention and experience in the same hand. Omission is a choice among real stores of material. It is also a making.

Near the end he names the gift he thinks a writer cannot do without: an internal detector for what is false, shockproof and always on. The phrase is rougher in his mouth than a critic's paraphrase, and the roughness is part of the pose, but the function he assigns it fits the rest of the interview. The detector is what tells you that a sentence is showing off, that a detail was put in because you wanted to be admired for knowing it, that an ending has gone soft. Journalism, he grants, can teach declarative sentences, and that teaching helps. It does not replace the detector. Nor does talent, if talent means a facility for writing sentences that sound like writing.

What the conversation withholds

The interview is rich about craft and guarded about pain. He acknowledges nightmares and sees no obligation to write them all down. Anything you can omit, if you know it, remains in the work as quality. That sentence is as close as he comes to a confession, and it is still a technical sentence. Readers who know the later biographical record sometimes want the 1958 conversation to say more about injury, depression, and the cost of the persona. The document they actually have is a craftsman's interview. Its silences are themselves examples of the method, chosen or habitual, and a careful reader will notice which questions produce a story about marlin and which produce a short answer.

Plimpton's questions are good because they stay with procedure. When do you work. How much do you rewrite. What do you leave out. Hemingway's answers have been quoted ever since as a programme for minimalism. The programme is real, and it is narrower than the quotation-culture around it. He is describing a desk, a standard, and a particular book from which whole regions of knowledge were cut because he had them. He is describing the hole that opens when the cut is a bluff.

Why it matters here

Folio's iceberg card uses this interview as the later statement of a principle first set down in Death in the Afternoon. The interview earns its place because it attaches the familiar image to a named book and to a list of omitted knowledge, and because it pairs the image with a working day: stop while you still know the next thing, rewrite until the words are right, keep a detector for falsity. Big Two-Hearted River, the fishing story from which he elsewhere said the war had been left out, is part of the card's larger account of omission. In this 1958 text the clearest demonstration is The Old Man and the Sea, together with the unnamed operation in the story he wrote after lunch at Prunier. Read the interview beside the stories, and the stories will show you the eighth that was allowed to show.
