In the spring of 1958, George Plimpton interviewed Ernest Hemingway at the Finca Vigía, outside Havana, for The Paris Review. The piece ran as The Art of Fiction No. 21. Asked how much rewriting he did, Hemingway said he had rewritten the last page of A Farewell to Arms thirty-nine times before he was satisfied. Plimpton asked what the trouble had been. The answer was, "Getting the words right."

The same conversation is where he states the iceberg in his own words. "There is seven-eighths of it underwater for every part that shows. Anything you know you can eliminate and it only strengthens your iceberg." Leave something out because you do not know it, he said, and "there is a hole in the story." The Old Man and the Sea could have run past a thousand pages and taken in everyone in the village, how they made a living, how they were born and educated and raised children. He had seen marlin mate, and left that out. He had seen a pod of more than fifty sperm whales in the same water, and once harpooned one nearly sixty feet long and lost it, and left that out too. The things he knew and refused were the part under the water.

That is a rule you can use at a desk. A story, a letter, or a report hits harder when you have the fact and then decline to explain it. The reader feels the weight of what was withheld, the way a talk can turn on something nobody names. The rule collapses when the gap is a bluff. If the village, the whale, or the next sentence is missing because you never had it, the page shows a hole.

The snap of the famous exchange was tightened for print. In the Paris Review archive at the Morgan Library, the typescript has Hemingway saying he rewrote that last page "thirty-nine times before I got it right." Plimpton changed "got it right" to "was satisfied," so the next sentence, "Getting the words right," would not repeat the line it answered. The thirty-nine drafts are Hemingway's. The timing of the reply is also the interviewer's.
