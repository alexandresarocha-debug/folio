David Kaniewski and his colleagues drove a sediment core, labeled B22, into the Larnaca Salt Lake beside Hala Sultan Tekke, a Late Bronze Age harbor on the southeast coast of Cyprus. From 84 pollen samples they tracked the vegetation moving from Mediterranean woodland toward dry steppe. In their reconstruction the driest stretch runs from about 1200 to 850 BC. They write that rainfall and groundwater around the site were then probably no longer enough to keep up the farming the town had depended on. The core shows the dry phase ending in the ninth century BC. Archaeology shows state-level life returning to Cyprus in the eighth century, and dense occupation returning to the Syrian coast at the end of the ninth or in the eighth, after the pollen turns wetter.

They set the Cypriot record beside an earlier core from Gibala, the site of Tell Tweini, a port of the kingdom of Ugarit on the Syrian coast. The two moisture series move together. The strongest match is at no time lag, with a correlation of 0.626. A destruction layer at Tell Tweini has a pooled radiocarbon age of 2962 ± 14 years before present, which calibrates, at one standard deviation, to 1215–1190 BC. That window matches the end of the Late Cypriot IIC period on the island. The authors treat the drought, the failed harvests, the burned port, and the sea raids as one crisis. Their last claim is direct: a drought of about 300 years set in 3,200 years ago, brought crop failure, scarcity, and famine, and pushed people to move.

A farming town can survive a dry year. It is in a different kind of trouble when the dry spell lasts for centuries and the trading partner across the water is drying out on the same calendar. The surplus that fed soldiers, smiths, and sailors thins out. People leave. Some of them show up somewhere else as migrants, and some as raiders. Egyptian inscriptions name the raiders, because royal texts name enemies. A pollen core cannot name a fleet. It can show whether the countryside was getting drier while the inscriptions were being carved.

A. Bernard Knapp and Sturt W. Manning, in the American Journal of Archaeology in 2016, did not deny a shift toward drier conditions. They argued that this research group moved too quickly from a pollen pattern to a cause, and that the dates are not sharp enough to make a raid, a famine, and a 300-year drought "the same event." Drier land belongs in any honest account of these coasts. A core from one lagoon does not count the missing grain, and it does not by itself explain the palaces of Greece or the Hittite heartland.
