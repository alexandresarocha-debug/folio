Background

The end of the Late Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean has never lacked for narratives. Egyptian inscriptions blame invaders they call the Sea Peoples. Archaeologists record burned cities, the end of the Hittite empire, the destruction of trading towns on the Syrian coast, and the fall of the Mycenaean palaces, clustered in the decades around 1200 BC. For a long time the argument was about which human enemy, or which internal revolt, deserved the leading role. A separate literature, using pollen, lake levels, and the chemistry of cave deposits, has been building the case that the same centuries were unusually dry across parts of the eastern Mediterranean. David Kaniewski and his colleagues wrote this 2013 paper in PLoS ONE to put a well-dated climatic and ecological sequence from Cyprus next to the archaeological crisis, and to say, more bluntly than a cautious review would, that the environment belongs at the root of the story rather than in a footnote.

The question

The question is whether the Late Bronze Age crisis on Cyprus and in coastal Syria coincides with a drought severe enough to have broken agriculture, and whether that drought can be tied, through the dating, to the famines, the movements of people, and the collapses that texts and excavations already document. They are not asking whether the climate of the whole hemisphere changed in identical step. They are asking whether, in this part of the sea where the powers actually met, the physical environment turned against the farming systems those powers rested on, and whether the turn can be shown in a core rather than inferred from a royal complaint about hunger.

Design

The centre of the study is a sediment core from the area of Hala Sultan Tekke, a major Late Bronze Age centre on the coast of Cyprus. From the core they read pollen, which records the vegetation around the site and therefore the balance between woodland, cultivation, and drier plant communities, together with other sedimentary indicators of the coastal environment. Radiocarbon dates anchor the sequence so that a change in the pollen can be placed on a calendar that archaeologists also use. They then compare the Cypriot record with their own earlier work at Gibala, the site of Tell Tweini on the Syrian coast, where a destruction layer and a parallel environmental sequence had already been studied. The comparison matters because one core is an anecdote and two regions moving together are a climate.

The analysis is statistical as well as visual. They reduce the many pollen and sedimentary variables to principal components so that the dominant pattern, wetter and more cultivated against drier and more stressed, can be tracked through time. They test whether the Cypriot series and the Syrian series are correlated, and at what lag, which is a way of asking whether the island and the mainland experienced the same anomaly or merely a vague family resemblance. They also set their local results beside published indicators from elsewhere in the eastern Mediterranean, including isotopic records and evidence for the Nile and for the great rivers of Mesopotamia, not as their own new measurements but as a check that the dry phase is not an accident of one lagoon.

Results they report

The vegetation record shows a shift towards a drier landscape, with the most marked stress falling in the centuries at the end of the Late Bronze Age and the start of the Iron Age. They identify a hydrological anomaly spanning roughly 1200 to 850 BC in calibrated radiocarbon years, during which precipitation and groundwater at Hala Sultan Tekke were, on their reconstruction, no longer enough to support the kind of agriculture the site had depended on. The Syrian sequence shows a comparable dry interval. The two series are correlated, in their test, without a long lag, which they read as evidence that Cyprus and the mainland were responding to the same regional drying rather than to unrelated local accidents. A destruction layer at Tell Tweini is dated to the years around the end of the thirteenth century BC, inside the onset of that interval.

They also argue for an end to the episode. The core indicates that the drought eased during the ninth century BC, and they note that dense occupation and state-level life reappear in Cyprus and on the Syrian coast in the ninth and eighth centuries, later than the first crisis and after the ecological pressure had relaxed. The recovery is part of the result. A climate story that only explains collapse and cannot say anything about the return of settlement is weaker than one that tracks both. Kaniewski and colleagues present the drying, the agricultural failure, the archaeological destruction, and the textual picture of famine and sea-borne attack as a single connected crisis, with climate as the underlying driver and the Sea Peoples as one of its historical faces rather than as an unrelated cause.

Interpretation

Their interpretation is stronger than a mere coincidence of dates. They think a prolonged drought undermined the agrarian base of Late Bronze Age societies in this corridor, produced famine, pushed people to move, and thereby fed the raids and the wars that the Egyptian texts record. The title's word, roots, is deliberate. Proximate causes, a fleet, a burned palace, a lost battle, sit on top of an ultimate environmental cause. The correlation between the Cypriot and Syrian records is the statistical support they offer for calling the drought regional. The match in time with the destruction horizon is the archaeological support they offer for calling it causal. In their reading, the Late Bronze Age did not end only because a new set of enemies appeared. It ended in a landscape that had become harder to farm, in places where the political system had been built on agricultural surplus and on sea trade that a hungry hinterland could no longer sustain.

The paper is also an argument about method. Literary texts from Egypt will always be tempted to name a human enemy, because that is what royal inscriptions are for. A pollen core cannot name an enemy. It can show whether the countryside was drying out while the inscriptions were being carved. Kaniewski and colleagues want the core to have the last word on the underlying conditions, and the texts to be reread as descriptions of what people did under those conditions. Migration and piracy, on this view, are responses to an environmental crisis, and can still be violent and historically important without being the root.

Limits and later criticism

A core from a coastal lagoon records the surroundings of that lagoon, filtered through pollen that travels and sediments that rework. It is a strong local record and a debatable regional one. The step from Hala Sultan Tekke and Tell Tweini to the whole eastern Mediterranean crisis, including the Aegean palaces and the Hittite heartland, is an extrapolation. Those regions have their own evidence, some of it consistent with drying and some of it still argued, and a Cypriot core does not settle them. Radiocarbon chronologies have ranges. A correlation of environmental series shows that two places dried together. It does not, by itself, measure how much food was lost or how many people moved. The causal chain from drought to Sea Peoples is the most interpretive sentence in the paper. People raid for reasons other than rainfall, and states fall for reasons other than grass.

Later syntheses, Eric Cline's among them, have been glad of the climatic evidence and unwilling to let it become the new single cause. Cline's perfect storm keeps drought and adds earthquakes, internal revolt, and the breakdown of trade, on the ground that the Late Bronze Age powers had survived dry years before. That criticism does not erase a multi-century dry interval if the interval is real. It refuses the word roots if roots means that the rest of the story is secondary. The right use of the paper is as a dated environmental constraint. Around the end of the thirteenth century and for a long time afterward, Cyprus and coastal Syria show a drier, agriculturally stressed landscape. Any historical narrative of the crisis in that sea has to be compatible with that landscape. It does not have to agree that the landscape is the whole explanation.

Why it matters here

The card bronze-age-collapse cites this paper as the palaeoclimate study, using sediment cores from Cyprus and Syria, that pulled a multi-century drought into the account of the Late Bronze Age collapse. Kaniewski and colleagues are the source of that citation. They supply the environmental series, the rough span from about 1200 to about 850 BC, and the claim that the island and the mainland dried together. The card's larger narrative, drawn from Cline, treats drought as one element in a systems failure rather than as the single root the paper's title announces. Folio can hold both. The cores make it irresponsible to tell 1177 as a story about invaders alone. The limits of two coastal sequences make it equally irresponsible to tell it as a story about rainfall alone. The paper earns the climate its place in the card. It does not earn it a monopoly.
