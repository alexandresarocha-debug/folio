Opening the essay as a single sitting

The full text of Politics and the English Language, as the Orwell Foundation prints it, is the Horizon essay of 1946 from start to finish. It is short enough to read in one sitting and dense enough that a second sitting pays. A reader who knows the six rules by heart and has never watched Orwell arrive at them is missing the essay's actual movement. He does not begin with advice. He begins with a quarrel about causes, then he pastes other people's prose on the page, then he names the habits those passages share, then he turns the habits into a political danger, and only after that does he trust himself, and you, with a list. The list is the souvenir. The essay is the order.

The opening paragraph is worth reading slowly because it is already an example of the two-way claim. He grants that the language is in a bad way. He grants that politics and economics are causes. He refuses the fatalism that usually follows the grant. If thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought, and a person can fight the second corruption in his own sentences this week. Everything later in the essay is an expansion of that refusal. When you read the full text, mark the moment he says the process is reversible. That sentence is the licence for the rules. Advice offered without a belief in reversibility would be etiquette. He thinks he is offering a small act of resistance.

How to read the five specimens

After the opening, Orwell quotes five passages. The full text is where you should actually look at them, because summaries of the essay tend to replace the evidence with the verdict. Read each passage once for sense, and once for the features he is about to name. Ask, before you take his word, whether you can see a picture. Ask who is doing the verb. Ask whether any phrase has arrived in a block you have met before. The passages are uneven, and one or two may strike you as ordinary academic prose of their decade, guilty of a period style more than of a personal collapse. That reaction is allowed. The essay is stronger if you notice where his contempt outruns the crime, because you will then trust him more where the crime is obvious.

He follows the quotations with a catalogue: dying metaphors, operators or verbal false limbs, pretentious diction, meaningless words. In the full text the catalogue is a guided rereading of the quotations, not a separate theory. He gives lists of his own, phrases that have lost their image, verbs that have been swollen into constructions, Latinate and fashionable words, political terms that signal approval or disgust without naming a fact. Read the lists aloud. The comedy is part of the criticism. A dead metaphor is often funny the moment you force the picture back into it, and the laugh is his test. If the picture is absurd and the writer did not intend absurdity, the writer was not seeing.

The political turn, in his own examples

The essay changes temperature when he stops mocking style and starts naming acts. This is the stretch to read without skimming, because it is the reason a style sheet became a political essay. He takes concrete horrors and sets beside them the phrases that were available for hiding them. Villages bombed and emptied can be called pacification. Populations pushed off their land can be called a transfer, or a rectification of a frontier. Imprisonment without trial, and killing, can be called the elimination of unreliable elements. In the full text you can watch him insist that the problem is mental pictures. A fresh, concrete style makes the act appear. A prefabricated style lets a speaker skip the appearance and keep the conclusion. He is writing in 1946, with recent European history and contemporary imperial language both in range. The examples are the essay's moral centre. The rules later on are a way of making such skips harder for the writer who is willing to be made uncomfortable.

He is also explicit, in a passage summaries sometimes soften, that the drift to ready-made prose is a convenience for anyone who has to defend the indefensible. The full text keeps the political range wide enough to include the defence of Soviet policy and the defence of British imperial action in the same breath. Whatever you think of his balance, the mechanism he describes is the same mechanism. Find the phrase that lets a room full of people nod without seeing. Then refuse it. Reading the essay in full is how you watch him try to refuse it in his own voice, including in sentences that are cleaner than his specimens and in sentences that are not.

The questions, then the rules

Before the famous list he offers a quieter one, and in the full text the quieter one is better. A scrupulous writer, he says, asks what he is trying to say, which words will express it, what image or idiom will make it clearer, and whether the image is fresh enough to work. He asks whether he can cut a word, and whether he has said anything avoidably ugly. These questions are a revision practice. They assume a draft already exists and a writer still present to interrogate it. They are also a test of knowledge. If you cannot answer the first question, no rule about short words will save the paragraph. Readers who come to the essay for permission to delete adjectives can find that permission. Readers who come for a way to discover that they do not yet know their point will find that too, higher up the page.

Then the six rules. In the full text they are printed as a consequence, and the sixth is visibly a correction of the first five: break any of these sooner than say something barbarous. Read number six twice. It is the rule that tells you the essay knows rules become jargon. It is also the rule most easily dropped when the essay is turned into a poster. After the list, Orwell returns to the claim that private effort matters, that bad habits can be mocked out of use, and that you do not have to wait for a reform of society in order to reform a page. The closing is hopeful in a grim register. He does not think the language is doomed. He thinks doom is the story people tell so they can keep the convenient phrases.

Reading him by his own lights

A full reading includes turning the questions back on the essay. He uses metaphors of his own, some of them vivid because they are newly seen, some of them already on the way to becoming the sort of phrase he dislikes. He uses the passive. He uses long words when he needs the name of an idea. The Foundation text lets you catch him at it, which is the right use of a free complete copy. The catches do not cancel the specimens or the euphemisms. They locate the essay as a piece of advice written by a working journalist under the pressure he describes. Nineteen Eighty-Four, three years later, builds a world in which the advice has been defeated on purpose, the dictionary cut down so that the pictures cannot form. If you read the essay in full first, the novel's invented language has a non-fiction ancestor you have actually watched at work.

The practical way to finish is to take one paragraph of your own and run his questions at it, with the sixth rule in force. Where the paragraph goes barbarous in the name of a short word, keep the longer one. Where a phrase is there because you have seen it in print, make yourself see the picture, and then choose. That exercise is the essay's own ending, implied by the order of the full text. Specimens, habits, horrors, questions, rules, and a warning against the rules. Skip a stage and you have a slogan. Read the stages in order and you have the argument.

Why it matters here

Folio's card sends you to the full text because the card can only keep the argument's outline: decline is reversible, the habits have names, euphemism hides pictures, six rules end in a permission to break them. The Foundation copy is where the outline gets its evidence back. The five passages, the lists of dead phrases, the political examples in his wording, and the catechism that comes before the rules are all doing work that a summary interrupts. Read them in one sitting when you want the essay's sequence, and keep the sequence when you go on to Newspeak. The novel is the thought experiment. The full essay is the observation the experiment starts from, still intact, still quotable, and still willing to be tested on its own sentences.
