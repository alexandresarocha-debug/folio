If animals copy the majority, could they get stuck with a habit that no longer pays? Lucy Aplin, Ben Sheldon and Richard McElreath returned to the great tits of Wytham Woods, where earlier experiments had seeded stable local traditions for opening a two-way puzzle box.

This time they changed the rewards. In sub-populations with an established tradition, the familiar technique now gave a less preferred food, sunflower seeds, while the alternative technique gave a better one, live mealworms. If conformity were all that mattered, the birds should have stuck with the local habit.

They did not. After only 14 days, 49% of birds had switched to the higher-payoff technique, and younger birds changed faster than older ones. The authors then fitted a learning model to each bird's sequence of choices to see how individual decisions produced the change.

The result surprised them. The shift happened without significant random exploration and without any evidence that birds were choosing to copy the more successful individuals. Instead, the birds combined two simple rules: copy the common technique, and repeat what has paid off for you personally. Together these let both individuals and the population follow a changing environment.

What this means in real life: conformity need not trap a group in outdated habits. When personal experience is rewarded, a tradition can bend and change within weeks, which is worth knowing for anyone hoping to shift habits in animals or people.

Caution: the work involves one species, one woodland and an artificial feeder, and the learning model, while powerful, is one way of interpreting choices that were not directly observed as copying. Different payoffs, or behaviours that are harder to try out alone, might give different results.
