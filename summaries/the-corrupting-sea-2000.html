Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell published The Corrupting Sea in 2000 as an argument against one Mediterranean. Fernand Braudel's great book of 1949 had made the sea a single world, with one geographical time underneath the reign of Philip II. Horden and Purcell's unit is smaller. A microecology is a pocket of coast, hillside, or wetland with its own soil, rain, and crop, often unlike the pocket a few miles away. A household lives with that risk by scattering its plots, by keeping more than one way to eat, and by exchanging whatever a bad year removes. The fragmentation is not a failure of the region to become uniform. It is, in their account, the normal condition of Mediterranean life from antiquity through the middle ages, and it does not stop being normal when an empire draws a line around it.

The sea enters as the thing that connects those pockets, and the connection they emphasize is the short hop. A state grain fleet supplying a capital is the voyage a chronicle notices. The ordinary link is cabotage: a small boat putting in at the next harbor, exchanging oil, grain, timber, or people, and going on. That traffic is easy to miss in a history written from capitals, and easy to underestimate, because no single voyage looks important. It is also why a "decline" dated from the capital can be invisible in a small port that is still trading with the harbor along the coast. Towns, on this account, are nodes in the web. They are not the definition of civilization, and the sea is not a blank between them. Horden and Purcell's title takes the old suspicion that the sea unsettles settled life and treats the unsettling as the region's ordinary economy. Connectivity keeps rearranging what a microecology can risk.

The practical test, when someone says the Mediterranean fell after Rome, or that it has one fate because it has one climate, is the scale of the evidence. An imperial average can fall while a coast of small harbors stays busy. A bad harvest in one pocket is normal, and the region absorbs it by the short crossing, not by a uniform recovery planned from the center. The history of the sea, in this book, is the history of those crossings added up, over a very long time, rather than the history of a few famous fleets.

The model is broad enough that critics have asked what is left of a specifically Mediterranean story. Fragmented landscapes tied by coastal trade can be found on other seas. David Abulafia's The Great Sea (2011) put named sailors, ports, and particular decades back in the foreground, on the view that a history of microecologies leaves out too much of what Mediterranean people actually did when a king, a war, or a religion reorganized the same shore. Horden and Purcell's answer is already in the method. The reorganization is real. It is visible, they argue, only if the small ecology underneath it is visible first.
