A Mediterranean that will not sit still for one portrait

Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell’s The Corrupting Sea, published in 2000, is a history of the Mediterranean over a very long period, from antiquity towards the early modern centuries, written in continuous argument with Fernand Braudel. They admire the scale of his question and reject the shape of his answer. Braudel’s sea, especially in the first part of The Mediterranean, can look like a single geographical personality: mountains, plains, and a unifying water, framing the age of Philip II as one world. Horden and Purcell think the Mediterranean is the opposite of a personality. It is a mosaic of very small regions, microecologies, whose defining feature is not a common climate or a common civilisation but an intense, irregular connectivity across the water. The sea corrupts, in the classical moral sense they borrow for their title, because it mixes. It carries people, goods, cults, and microbes between places that remain, in their soils and their risks, stubbornly different. Unity is something the Mediterranean is always producing and always failing to sustain.

The book is not a narrative of any one century, and a reader looking for Philip II, or for a sequence of empires, will not find the familiar spine. The evidence is archaeological, ethnographic, and textual, and it is deployed topically: production, navigation, the consequences of being an island or a coast, the meaning of a town, the recurrence of catastrophe and recovery. The style is dense by design. They are trying to replace a picture, the romantic Mediterranean of olive, vine, and grain, with a model, and the model has to be stated generally enough to cover two millennia of fragments. The reward is a set of concepts that, once absorbed, changes what a particular harbour history is an example of.

Microecologies and the risk of the harvest

A microecology, in their usage, is a small region defined by what it can produce and by what it cannot reliably produce. The Mediterranean’s physical structure, a coast of pockets, a handful of soils, a rainfall that fails often enough to matter, makes these pockets extreme. A valley grows a surplus of one thing and lacks another. A hill behind it grows something else and starves in a different year. No pocket, on their argument, is self-sufficient over time, and the attempt to behave as if it were is one of the region’s recurring disasters. The logical response to unreliable production is redistribution: storage, exchange, dependency on a neighbour, and the cultivation of crops that can be sold rather than eaten. Connectivity is not a luxury added to subsistence. It is how subsistence is managed in an environment of high local risk. The sea is the great enabler of that management, because water transport, even in small boats, moves bulk more cheaply than land ever could in this terrain.

This is the point at which they break most cleanly with a Mediterranean of timeless peasant wisdom. The picturesque terraces and the famous triad of crops are, for them, strategies within a system of risk, not expressions of a regional soul, and they come and go. Intensification, the piling of labour onto a piece of land to make it yield more, and abatement, the letting go of land when the labour, the market, or the rain fails, are the two movements they see everywhere. A landscape that looks ancient may be a recent intensification. A landscape that looks abandoned may be an abatement, a rational response to risk, rather than a decline. The vocabulary is their way of talking about change without the moral architecture of rise and fall. Mediterranean history, in this book, is a history of continual small adjustments, most of them invisible to any archive that records only kings.

Connectivity, cabotage, and towns

Connectivity is the term that has travelled furthest beyond the book. Horden and Purcell mean by it the density of links among microecologies, and they stress that the important links are often short. Cabotage, the tramping of small vessels from bay to bay, matters more in their Mediterranean than the grand convoys of state navies, because cabotage is how ordinary redistribution actually happened. A cargo of grain in a famous famine is the spectacular case of a relationship that, in calmer years, is a thousand unrecorded bargains. They are interested in the conditions that make links easy: a coast with many landfalls, a sea small enough that a crossing is imaginable and large enough to connect different climates, a population accustomed to leaving. They are equally interested in the conditions that snap links. Piracy, a bad decade of storms, a political rupture, or a shift in demand can turn a connected coast into an isolated one within a generation, and can reverse the change in the generation after that. Connectivity is a variable, not a permanent blessing of the inland sea.

Towns, in this model, are not the opposite of the countryside and not the heroes of a modernising story. A town is a place where the redistribution becomes permanent, where people who do not grow the food can live on the food that risk-management moves. It can be tiny. Their Mediterranean is full of small centres whose significance is positional, a roadstead, a shrine, a market at the junction of two pockets, rather than monumental. This lets them look at antiquity and the Middle Ages with the same apparatus. The Roman city’s grandeur does not remove it from the ecology of risk and redistribution. It shows what that ecology looks like when an empire forces the intensification and skims the result. When the empire recedes, the underlying pattern, pockets, links, abatement, is still there to be read in the archaeology of the ordinary.

Against unity, decline, and the picturesque

Three polemics run through the book and explain its tone. The first is against unity. They do not believe there is a Mediterranean culture, a Mediterranean personality, or a single Mediterranean environment that explains the societies on its shore. The olive and the vine are not a civilisation. They are crops with a range, grown or not grown according to the microecology and the market. The second polemic is against decline-and-fall as the master plot. A period that produces fewer inscriptions, or smaller houses, or less imported marble, is a period of abatement or of a different connectivity, and it may be a period in which more people ate better because fewer people were extracting the surplus. They are not cheerful by rule. Catastrophe, plague, and violent rupture are in the book. They are not willing to call every reduction in monumentality a catastrophe. The third polemic is against the picturesque Mediterranean of modern northern writing, the sea as a holiday from history, all light and unchanging villages. Their sea is laborious, risky, and economically neurotic. It has to be, because the rainfall is unreliable and the neighbour is a boat ride away.

Braudel is the opponent they most respect, and the contrast can be put cleanly. He offers three speeds of time and a unified geographical stage, and he sets a political narrative on that stage in the age of a single monarch. They offer one very long duration, inside which the significant variation is spatial and local, and they have little use for the monarch. Where Braudel’s longue durée can feel like destiny written in relief maps, theirs feels like a restless equilibrium, always being renegotiated between a field and a harbour. Readers who want the majesty of the first part of Braudel will find Horden and Purcell austere. Readers who suspected that the majesty was smoothing over too many coasts will find the austerity a relief.

What the model cannot see

The objections the book has drawn are mostly about the price of its flexibility. A model in which intensification and abatement, connection and isolation, can describe almost any archaeological pattern is a model that risks explaining everything and therefore nothing. Critics have asked for harder tests: predictions about a particular valley that could have been false. Others have asked for more politics. A redistribution economy is also a power relation, and the book’s ecological language can understate coercion, slavery, and the simple fact that some pockets controlled the boats. Environmental historians have wanted more climate history inside the long period, more attention to the ways in which the supposedly stable backdrop of risk was itself moving. These are requests for a sequel the authors themselves have treated as unfinished business, and they do not erase the concepts. Microecology and connectivity are now part of the equipment of Mediterranean history, used by people who do not accept the whole programme.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on Braudel’s three speeds presents The Corrupting Sea as the modern successor to his Mediterranean, the book that keeps the long duration and replaces his unity with fragmented micro-regions and their connectivity. That is the right relationship, and it is a relationship of correction as much as of descent. Braudel supplies the demand that history be written at more than one speed, and the demonstration, in Philip II’s sea, of what a slow history looks like. Horden and Purcell supply the suspicion that the slow history is not one history. The Mediterranean is many microecologies, coupled by a sea that makes coupling cheap and breakage frequent. Read them when Braudel’s mountains have started to feel too majestic, too much like a single explanation. Their sea is more chaotic, more local, and, in its way, more historical, because the connections that matter can be turned on and off inside a human lifetime, even when the rocks last forever.
