Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell begin The Corrupting Sea (2000) by refusing the Mediterranean as a single stage. Fernand Braudel had written it as a sea of mountains, winds, and routes, moving so slowly that Philip II's battles sat on it like foam. They keep the long duration and change the unit. The useful piece, they argue, is the microecology: one valley, one pocket of plain, one island harbor, with its own mix of grain, olives, vines, pasture, and a bad year that arrives on its own schedule. No such district feeds itself reliably. The neighbor's harvest fails on a different calendar. What joins them is not a climate and not the famous grain fleets of Venice or Rome. It is cabotage, the short hop along the coast, a boat trading the surplus of one creek against the shortage of the next. They compare that constant small motion to Brownian motion. High commerce sits on top of it. The ordinary year is made by the small boats.

The title takes an old suspicion and turns it over. Classical writers treated the sea as morally dangerous, a solvent of settled life, because trade and sailors corrupted the farmer and the citizen. Horden and Purcell's evidence runs the other way. The settled places lasted because of the traffic. A district that tried for real self-sufficiency was the fragile one. They use this against two familiar breaks in the history of the region. Moses Finley had described the ancient economy as largely local and largely for use, with trade as a side effect of what the rich wanted. Archaeology of ordinary goods, in their reading, shows exchange that was wider and more routine than that. The other break is the early medieval "dark age," often drawn as the end of Mediterranean trade. They argue that the low-level coastal exchange went on through it. Empires and luxury cargoes come and go. The short trip is the structure that stays.

The practical consequence is a change in what you treat as the cause. A famine, a city, or an imperial grain office is real, and it is sitting on a web of districts that were already depending on each other. If a history of the region follows only the famous fleets and the famous battles, it will miss the boats that made an ordinary year possible, and it will mistake a political border for an economic one. Connectivity is their word for that web. It is not a mood of openness. It is the dull fact that the next valley is a different risk, and that someone is usually sailing between them.

Later climate work puts a limit on how far a continuous web can be asked to explain. In 2012 a study in the Journal of Interdisciplinary History, led by Michael McCormick, set tree rings, ice cores, and lake beds next to the written record and argued that the centuries of the Roman Empire were a warm, relatively stable climate phase, and that late antiquity was not. Shifts of that size are hard to file as background noise. The short coastal trip remains the way the districts lived. It is not the whole reason one century differed from the next.
