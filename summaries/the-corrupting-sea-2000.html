Melos, in the Aegean, is one of the four places Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell use to say what the Mediterranean actually is. The island is eroded, short of water, and marginal as farmland. What it had, and traded for centuries, was obsidian, and what kept it going was the density of its links to other ports. A valley on the mainland makes the same point at a larger scale. The Biqa, in Lebanon, is not one ecology. It holds several smaller ones, wetter and drier, and the traffic among them changes from century to century. South Etruria and Cyrenaica are the other two. None of the four fed itself reliably, year after year, out of its own soil. The classical ideal of autarky, living on what you grow, is in their account a wish repeated in texts, not a condition anyone sustained.

The ordinary link was small. Cabotage, boats working from bay to bay, moved grain and other bulk more cheaply than carts could move it over this coast, and it did so in bargains that almost never entered an archive. A famous cargo in a famine year is the visible case of a trade that, in ordinary years, was a thousand short hops. They treat the link as a variable. Piracy, a run of bad winters, or a political break could leave a coast isolated within a generation, and the generation after could open it again. A town, on this view, is a place where that redistribution becomes permanent enough for people who do not grow the food to live on it. It can be a roadstead or a shrine. Roman grandeur is what the pattern looks like when an empire forces the intensification and takes a share. When the empire recedes, the pockets and the boats are still there to read in the archaeology.

The use of the book, when someone is deciding what "the Mediterranean" explains, is to refuse the single portrait. Olive, vine, and grain are crops with a range, grown or dropped as the market and the rain require. A landscape that looks ancient may be a recent piling of labor onto a slope. A landscape that looks abandoned may be a letting-go, after the rain or the buyers failed. Horden and Purcell are arguing with Fernand Braudel's unified sea, and with the habit of calling every loss of marble a decline. The rainfall is unreliable. The neighbor is a boat ride away. That is the history.
