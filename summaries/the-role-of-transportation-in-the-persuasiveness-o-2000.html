In 2000 Melanie C. Green and Timothy C. Brock had 97 undergraduates read a story they called "Murder at the Mall," adapted from Sherwin B. Nuland's How We Die. A college student, Joan, loses her little sister Katie, who is stabbed to death by a psychiatric patient while they are at a shopping mall. Readers then rated how transported they had felt: how much they pictured the scene, felt with the people, and lost track of the room they were sitting in. Split at the median score of 72, the more transported readers rated violence as more likely, 11.35 against 10.26, and rated Katie more warmly, 47.96 against 43.04.

A second group of 69 students was asked to circle anything in a story that rang false. The authors called the circles Pinocchios. Highly transported readers circled fewer of them, 4.13 circles against 10.52, and marked fewer lines, 6.71 against 17.22. In a later experiment with 258 people, instructions that lowered transportation also lowered beliefs that matched the story. Calling the story fact rather than fiction generally left both transportation and those beliefs about where they were.

The beliefs that moved were implications, never stated as a moral. A murder in a mall suggests that malls are unsafe. A killer who is a psychiatric patient suggests that such patients should not be left unsupervised. A child who has done nothing wrong suggests that the world is unjust. A third experiment, with 274 readers, found the same pattern: higher transportation went with more of those story-consistent beliefs. Experiment 3's violence scores were 11.56 for the more transported readers and 10.78 for the less transported.

This is why a feature, a fundraising letter, or an episode about a crime can change what a public place feels like. The lever is whether you were inside the scene with Katie. The label on the title page, fact or fiction, did little of that work. Green and Brock's scale has been reused for ads, health messages, and news. Their own numbers say the absorbed reader accepts more of the story and comes out a bit more persuaded. A reader who stays outside, circling what does not fit, moves less.
