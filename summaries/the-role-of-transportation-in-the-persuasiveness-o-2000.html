Absorption as a measurable state

In 2000 Melanie C. Green and Timothy C. Brock published a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology that gave the feeling of being lost in a story a scale and a set of experiments. They call the feeling transportation. Drawing on Richard Gerrig's image of the reader as a traveller, they define transportation as absorption in a narrative: imagery, emotion, and focused attention, with the surroundings dropping away. Their proposal is that this state is a route by which stories change beliefs. A reader who is transported is a reader who is simulating the events, caring about the people, and doing less of the sceptical work that an explicit argument would provoke. The paper sets out to measure that state and then to see whether higher transportation goes with more story-consistent belief.

The narrative that carries the first studies is called Murder at the Mall. It was adapted from a passage in Sherwin Nuland's How We Die. In the version participants read, a young girl, Katie, is stabbed to death by a psychiatric patient while at a shopping mall with an older sibling. The events are concrete and grim, written with enough sensory detail that a reader can picture the place, and plausible enough that the same text can be dressed as a short story or as a newspaper report. That double plausibility is essential to the design. Green and Brock wanted a story whose world a reader might enter, and a story that could be labelled fiction or fact without becoming absurd under either label.

The scale, and the first findings

They built a transportation scale from the parts of the definition. Some items ask whether you could picture events, whether you wanted to know how the story ended, whether you felt emotionally involved, whether you were mentally elsewhere while you read. A further set asks about images specific to the story in front of you, so that transportation is partly a general tendency to be absorbed and partly a response to this telling. The scale is the instrument later researchers picked up. In the 2000 paper it is also the predictor. People who score higher are, on the authors' account, deeper inside the narrative world.

The experiments then look at beliefs and at judgements of characters. In the first experiment, with 97 participants, greater transportation went with stronger story-consistent beliefs and with more favourable evaluations of the protagonists. A second experiment, with 69 participants, asked readers to mark false notes, places where the story rang wrong. Highly transported readers marked fewer of them. Two further experiments, one with 274 participants and one with 258, again found transportation associated with beliefs and evaluations. In the last of these, the researchers tried to move transportation directly, by the instructions they gave for reading. When the instructions reduced transportation, story-consistent beliefs and favourable evaluations fell with it. Across the series, labelling the story as fact or as fiction generally left both transportation and the corresponding beliefs in place. The frame that says this is made up did not, by itself, switch off the effect in their data.

A route unlike argument

The theoretical claim is that narrative persuasion uses a different route from a list of reasons. An argument invites counter-argument. A story invites a run of experience. If you are busy picturing Katie and dreading the next sentence, you are less occupied with generating objections, and the beliefs implied by the sequence, about the world of the mall, about violence, about the people in charge of a patient, can settle in as if they had been witnessed. Green and Brock also stress connections with characters. Transportation is partly caring how things go for someone. Care is a biased state, and the bias is the method. The paper's darker implication is built into the method. A well-made story can move beliefs about real categories of people while wearing a label that says the events are invented. The label, in their results, was a weak protection.

The story they chose makes the implication hard to miss. Murder at the Mall is about a killing by a psychiatric patient, which means story-consistent beliefs are beliefs about mental illness and public danger, a region already thick with fear. Transportation research can look like a neutral mechanics of engagement. The stimulus in the original paper is a reminder that engagement has a subject, and that the subject can be a stigma. Later work has used narrative to encourage healthier behaviour and to open sympathy, and the same mechanics apply. The 2000 paper is honest about the double use because its own narrative is not a fairy tale. It is a public story about harm, the kind of story news and fiction both know how to tell.

What the experiments do and do not show

These are laboratory studies. People read an assigned text under assigned instructions, then fill in scales. The belief items are the researchers' operational versions of being influenced, answers on a questionnaire at the end of a session. That is a real psychological effect, and it is a long way from a vote, a clinical attitude, or a lasting change of mind a month later. The instruction result is the closest the paper comes to a lever: lower the absorption and the beliefs move. Even there, the lever is a set of laboratory instructions, easier to use when you want to disrupt reading than when you want to guarantee a deep one. Green and Brock are more confident that transportation, when it occurs, predicts agreement, than they are that a writer can switch it on at will.

The paper also leaves individual differences in plain sight. Some people are more transportable than others, and some texts invite imagery more than others. The scale measures a state in a sitting. It does not, by itself, explain why one person enters a novel and another stays outside it, or why the same person enters one chapter and resists the next. Gerrig's book had treated those questions as part of a general psychology of narrative worlds. Green and Brock take his traveller and ask a persuasion question. The narrowing is the achievement. It is also why the paper has to be read beside later reviews, which ask how stable the association is across many stories, many products, and many laboratories, and beside the simple caution that a questionnaire at the end of a grim story is the beginning of a political worry, not the end of one.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on narrative transportation starts with this paper: the name, the scale, the finding that absorbed readers take on story-consistent beliefs and find fewer faults, and the awkward fact that calling the story fiction did not reliably stop the process. Murder at the Mall is the concrete case to remember, because it keeps the mechanism attached to a real kind of public narrative, a killing, a patient, a place. Van Laer's later meta-analysis asks whether the association holds across a wide consumer literature. Gerrig's earlier book is where the traveller metaphor comes from. Green and Brock are the hinge, the place where a literary feeling became an instrument, and where the instrument showed that stories persuade by absorption. The practical lesson on the card follows directly. A good story is a strong way to teach, and the same strength is available to anyone who would rather you did not stop to argue.
