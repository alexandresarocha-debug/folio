From a paradox to a shape

James C. Davies’s essay “Toward a Theory of Revolution”, published in the American Sociological Review in 1962, is a short attempt to turn a historian’s paradox into a general hypothesis with a picture. The paradox is Tocqueville’s. Revolutions, Davies argues, are not the children of endless misery. They are most likely when a long period of rising expectations, fed by real economic and social improvement, is followed by a short, sharp reversal. During the long rise, people come to expect that needs which are beginning to be met will go on being met, and will be met more fully. During the reversal, actual satisfaction falls while expected satisfaction continues, for a time, to climb. The gap between the two lines opens quickly. That gap, and not the absolute level of suffering, is what Davies thinks produces a revolutionary mood. Draw the actual line and it resembles a J tipped on its side, rising and then hooking down. Draw the expected line and it keeps rising. The intolerable space between them is the occasion for revolt.

He is explicit about the theories he is trying to displace. One is the simple misery thesis, associated with a vulgar reading of Marx, in which progressive degradation of the poor makes revolution more and more likely until it is certain. Davies thinks the historical pattern is closer to the opposite: prolonged degradation produces resignation, and improvement produces the standards against which a setback is measured. Another is any theory that reads revolution directly off a level of income. Poor societies are not uniformly revolutionary, and rising societies are not uniformly contented. The relevant quantity is the relation between what people have come to expect and what they are getting. He calls the essay a theory in the modest sense of the title. It is a hypothesis offered for the organisation of cases, not a completed model and not a statistical demonstration across a sample of countries.

The cases he uses

The method is historical illustration, and a reader should see exactly how thin and how suggestive that is. Davies takes situations in which a revolt or a revolution occurred, reconstructs a period of improvement followed by a setback, and asks whether the mood of the rebels fits the gap between expectation and satisfaction. He leans on Tocqueville for the French Revolution, treating the prosperity and the reforms of the decades before 1789, and the frustrations of the late 1780s, as the pattern in its classic form. He takes the Russian Revolution as a modern case in which years of industrial growth and of rising, if uneven, expectations were followed by the disasters of war, military defeat, and the collapse of ordinary provisioning. The long rise is the stem of the J. The war is the hook.

He also uses smaller and less mythologised cases, because a theory that only fits the canonical revolutions is indistinguishable from a story about those revolutions. Dorr’s Rebellion in Rhode Island in the 1840s, a fight over a suffrage that had not widened with the state’s economic life, is one of them. The Egyptian revolution of 1952 is another, read as the product of a period in which nationalist and developmental hopes had been raised and then blocked by a regime that could neither meet them nor suppress the comparison. The point of the minor cases is portability. If the J-curve is real, it should appear in a state legislature’s suffrage crisis and in a military coup against a discredited monarchy, not only in 1789 and 1917. Davies treats the fit as supportive and, in his own word, tentative. He is showing that the shape can be found, not that he has searched for it where it might have been absent.

What the theory commits him to

Several commitments follow from the shape, and they are the part of the essay worth arguing with. The first is psychological. Davies is not, in the end, offering an account of states, armies, or organisations. He is offering an account of mood. A population that fears the loss of ground recently gained becomes available for revolution. Whether anyone organises that availability, whether the state splits, whether the army fires, is outside the theory’s main claim. The second commitment is about time. The rise has to be long enough to reset expectations. The reversal has to be short and visible enough that expectations do not quietly fall with it. A slow stagnation may impoverish people without revolutionising them, because expectations sag at roughly the rate of conditions. A sudden humiliation after a generation of gains does not give expectations time to sag. The third commitment is comparative in principle and difficult in practice. To test the theory one would need measures of expected satisfaction, which is a state of mind, and of actual satisfaction, which is a bundle of income, status, and security. Davies uses the indicators he can find, production, reform, war, prices, and the testimony of observers. He does not pretend they are the thing itself.

He also commits himself to a prediction that later writers found useful and dangerous. If the gap is what matters, then a government that has been successfully reformist is in more danger from a stumble than a government that has never allowed anyone to hope. This is Tocqueville’s dangerous moment, generalised beyond France and beyond the particular politics of feudal remnant. It is also a warning against a certain kind of modernisation optimism. Development, on the J-curve, does not purchase stability in any simple way. It purchases a higher standard of expectation, which is an asset if improvement continues and a liability if it breaks. The essay thus sits, somewhat awkwardly, between the hopeful social science of the early 1960s and an older tragic sense that reform is a risk.

Why political scientists moved on

The criticisms that stuck are almost all about what the picture cannot identify. Expectations are easy to infer after a revolution and hard to measure before one. Any rising society that later has a revolution can be described as a J if one is allowed to choose the starting date and the definition of reversal. Societies that display the same economic shape and do not revolt are the decisive test, and Davies’s design, which starts from revolts and looks backward, cannot see them. Ted Robert Gurr’s later work on relative deprivation took the psychological insight and tried to give it a broader empirical base, asking whether the gap between what people think they deserve and what they get predicts political violence more generally. The results of that wider programme have been mixed. Grievance of a relative kind is rarely irrelevant. It is also rarely sufficient, and it travels poorly as a sole predictor once organisation, repression, and opportunity are in the equation.

Theda Skocpol’s criticism, in States and Social Revolutions, is the sharpest structural version. Peasant misery and frustrated expectation are, in her view, too common to explain the rare event of a social revolution. What needs explaining is the breakdown of states under international military pressure, and the capacity of certain rural communities to revolt when the state’s grip fails. A theory of mood does not say why the French state, rather than another reforming monarchy, reached a fiscal dead end, or why the Russian army’s collapse mattered more than a curve of real wages. Skocpol is not denying that the people who rebelled were angry, or that some of them were angry about a loss of ground. She is denying that the anger is the analytical place to start. Davies’s essay is most vulnerable exactly where it is most ambitious: the jump from a recognisable shape in a few narratives to a theory of revolution as such.

What is still worth keeping

What survives the criticism is the negative claim, which was always the more solid half. Long, unbroken misery is a poor predictor of revolution, and improvement is not a guarantee of quiet. Any theory that plots unrest as a simple rising function of poverty will misread both the resigned poor and the furious beneficiaries of recent growth. The J-curve is one way of writing that negative claim so that it can be remembered in a crisis. It tells an observer to look at the recent path, not only at the present level, and to take seriously the possibility that a setback is being measured against a standard the setback itself has not yet revised. That is good practical advice for reading a society, and it is weaker as a law. It does not specify the organisations that turn a mood into a seizure of power. It does not specify the international conditions under which a state can ride out the mood. It does not, because Davies was honest about the evidence he had, rest on anything more than a set of reconstructions.

The honest way to use the essay is as a constraint on stories. When someone explains a revolution by saying that people were badly off, the J-curve asks: badly off compared with what recent past, and were they worse off a generation earlier without revolting? When someone explains stability by saying that things are improving, it asks: improving relative to expectations that improvement itself has raised, and what happens in the first bad year? These questions are Tocqueville’s questions, made portable. Davies’s contribution was the portability, and the picture. His overreach was to call the picture a theory of why revolutions occur, when it is a theory of when a population becomes more combustible, which is a different and still useful thing.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on the Tocqueville paradox presents Davies as the social scientist who formalised the paradox: the J-curve, a long rise in expectations and gratifications followed by a sharp reversal. That is an accurate account of what the 1962 essay is for, and the card is right to put it between Tocqueville and Skocpol. Tocqueville saw the pattern in one country’s provinces and in one government’s reforming decade.
