James C. Davies's finding, stated in the first sentence of his 1962 article, is that revolutions are most likely when a long period of real economic and social improvement is followed by a short, sharp reversal. People then fear that ground gained with great effort will be lost. The case he counts most carefully is Dorr's Rebellion in Rhode Island. In 1828 the state had about 94,000 people and 3,600 votes were cast, 4 percent of the population, against 11 percent of the national population excluding slaves. In 1840, with about 109,000 people, 8,300 votes were cast, 8 percent, against 17 percent nationally. Suffrage demands had been refused since the 1790s. In December 1841 the dissidents submitted a People's Constitution, adopted in an election open to adult men, and in 1842 Thomas Dorr's side tried to install a government the charter authorities would not recognize. One person was killed. More than 500 men were arrested, mostly textile workers, mechanics, and laborers. The legislature then called a convention chosen by something close to universal manhood suffrage, and a new constitution took effect in January 1843.

He reads 1917 the same way, and he gives the numbers he is using. He dates the long rise from the emancipation of the serfs in 1861 to the revolution of 1905. One estimate he cites puts the net yearly income of a peasant family of five in the black-earth region, late in the century, at 82 rubles, against about 168 rubles for a good male factory wage. Urban population almost doubled between 1878 and 1897. On 22 January 1905 troops in St. Petersburg killed hundreds of petitioners marching on the palace. The share of workers who struck that year, on the series he uses, was 163.8 percent, which means the workforce struck, on average, more than once. The war that began in 1914, the shortages of 1916, and the break in February 1917 are the reversal after that rise.

The shape is a warning about the recent path. A population that has just spent a generation getting somewhere will measure a bad year against that gain. A population that has been poor for a long time may endure a worse year and not move. Davies was plain that he had started from rebellions and looked backward. He named disturbances that did not become revolutions, among them the Sepoy Rebellion of 1857, the Pullman Strike of 1894, and the Boxer Rebellion of 1900, and he did not claim the shape would have predicted them.

Theda Skocpol's objection, in States and Social Revolutions (1979), is that frustrated hope is too common to pick out the rare cases. France, Russia, and China, on her evidence, turned when the state broke under military pressure and peasant communities were able to act together. A mood, by itself, does not say which of those things will happen.
