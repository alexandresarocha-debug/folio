In the election of 1828, Rhode Island cast about 3,600 votes. The state had about 94,000 people, so the vote was about 4 percent of the population. In the United States as a whole, excluding enslaved people, votes that year came to about 11 percent of the population. By 1840 Rhode Island had about 109,000 people and cast 8,300 votes, about 8 percent, against about 17 percent nationally. A colonial charter still tied the franchise to property. Textile mills, established in the state from 1790, had pulled whole families into the towns, and in good years an industrial family earned two or three times what the farm had paid. The depression that hit the mills in the middle of the 1830s, worsened by the Panic of 1837, cut that gain. In 1841 suffrage associations called a convention the government did not want. Adult men voted, and in December they approved a People's Constitution. On 3 May 1842 they inaugurated Thomas Dorr. The legislature had made it a crime, punishable by life in prison, to hold office under that constitution. One man was killed. More than 500 were arrested, mostly textile workers, mechanics, and laborers. A constitution the legal government did accept, written under a wider suffrage, was in force in January 1843.

James C. Davies, in "Toward a Theory of Revolution" (American Sociological Review, February 1962), reads Dorr's Rebellion as a shape, not as a freak of Rhode Island. Revolutions are most likely, he writes, when a long period of real economic and social development is followed by a short, sharp reversal. People come to fear that ground gained with great effort will be lost. He draws the satisfied line rising and then hooking down, like a J on its side, and the expected line still rising. The gap is the revolutionary mood. His Russian illustration uses the same gap in money. One estimate he cites puts the net yearly income of a peasant family of five, in the rich black-earth country late in the nineteenth century, at 82 rubles. A good wage for a male factory worker was about 168 rubles. That difference, he argues, nearly doubled the urban population between 1878 and 1897. The city was the rise. War, defeat, and the collapse of ordinary supplies were the hook, and 1917 was the break.

The use of the curve is as a check on two easy stories. Long misery, by itself, is a poor predictor: people who have never been given a reason to expect more often endure. Steady improvement is also a poor guarantee of quiet, because the improvement resets what counts as an injury. Davies already records a large reversal that did not become a revolution. In 1932, private production income in the United States was back where it had been in 1916, and at least 29 people died of starvation in New York City. He credits the absence of a revolution to apathy among the worst hit, to a constitutional habit shared across classes, and to a national government that, from 1933, attacked the slump hard enough that there was no class left to hate as indifferent.

Theda Skocpol, in States and Social Revolutions (1979), argued that the curve still cannot pick out the rare cases. Frustrated expectation is common. A social revolution, on her comparison of France, Russia, and China, also requires a state breaking under war or debt, and rural communities in a position to revolt when it does. Davies's own design starts from rebellions and looks backward, so it is built to find the J where a revolt has already happened.
