In 1828, Rhode Island cast about 3,600 votes. The state had about 94,000 people, so the ballots came to roughly 4 percent of the population. In 1840, with about 109,000 people, it cast 8,300 votes, about 8 percent. Leave out the enslaved population and the United States as a whole was nearer 17 percent. Rhode Island was still living under its colonial charter of 1663, and a man generally needed property to vote. James C. Davies uses those figures in "Toward a Theory of Revolution" (1962) for a revolt that does not fit the picture of the hungriest people rising first.

Textile mills had been in the state since 1790. In Massachusetts the early mills hired farmers' daughters, who could go home. In Rhode Island whole families moved to town and lived on wages. Their working lives had changed. The electorate had not. Suffrage demands came in waves, in 1790, 1811, and again after Connecticut and Massachusetts widened the vote. In 1841 the reformers held their own elections, open to adult men, wrote the People's Constitution, and in December had it approved by that same wide electorate. Thomas Wilson Dorr led the resulting People's government. In 1842 the clash with the charter government came to a head at an arsenal. The rebels' cannon misfired. Davies's tally of this "little revolution" is one person killed and more than 500 arrested, mostly textile workers, mechanics, and laborers. The revolt failed. The old legislature then called a new convention, and a wider constitution took effect in January 1843.

Davies's shape for this, and for larger revolutions, is a J. A long period in which real conditions and people's expectations both rise is followed by a short, sharp drop. The dangerous gap is not between the poor and a distant rich. It is between what people have come to count on and what they now fear they are about to lose. His second case is Russia. He dates the long rise from the emancipation of the serfs in 1861. One estimate he cites puts the net yearly income of a peasant family of five, in the rich black-earth region late in the century, at 82 rubles, against about 168 rubles for a good male factory wage. The urban population nearly doubled between 1878 and 1897. Then the line bent. On 22 January 1905 troops in St. Petersburg shot demonstrators. In 1905 the proportion of workers who struck reached 163.8 percent, meaning the average worker struck more than once. The 1914 war brought the shortages and the military collapse that brought the regime down in February 1917. Steady misery, in his account, does not do this. The loss of ground people thought they had gained does.

The pattern is a way to read a disturbance without treating poverty itself as the cause. A group can be better off than its parents and still be the group that moves, if a promised or accustomed gain is shut. Rhode Island's argument was the vote, not bread.

David Snyder and Charles Tilly tested that family of claims on France from 1830 to 1960, in the American Sociological Review in 1972. Year to year, measures of short-term hardship did not track collective violence. Conflict organized around who held power tracked it better. A curve fitted after a revolution is easier to draw than a curve that predicts the next one.
