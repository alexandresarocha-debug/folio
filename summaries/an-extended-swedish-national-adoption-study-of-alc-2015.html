Adoption is a natural experiment. An adopted child gets genes from one set of parents and a home from another. If the child's risk follows the biological parents, genes are at work. If it follows the adoptive parents, the home is at work.

Kenneth Kendler and colleagues used Sweden's national registers, which link medical, legal and pharmacy records for the whole country. They found 18,115 adoptees born between 1950 and 1993. Alcohol use disorder was counted when it appeared in those records. Among the adoptees, 9.4% had it.

Both sides of the family mattered. An adoptee whose biological parent had alcohol use disorder had about 1.5 times the odds of developing it (odds ratio 1.46). A biological brother or sister with the disorder raised the odds to almost double (1.94). An adoptive parent with the disorder, who shared no genes with the child, also raised the odds, by 1.40. The genetic and home risks simply added up.

The team then checked the result in two other kinds of family. They looked at 171,989 children of parents they never lived with, 94% of them fathers. These parents passed on genes but not a home. They also looked at 107,696 children raised by a stepparent, who passed on a home but not genes. Both groups confirmed the adoption results.

The genetic risk was broad. Children were more likely to develop alcohol use disorder if the biological parent they never lived with had alcohol problems, but also drug abuse, psychiatric illness or a criminal record. On the home side, a stepparent's alcohol problems, drug abuse, crime or early death all raised the risk.

What this means in real life: alcoholism runs in families by two routes at once, through genes and through the home. Removing one route does not remove the other.

Caution: registers miss people who never came to official notice, so the cases found were probably more severe on average. Adopted children are not placed at random: biological and adoptive parents were slightly alike in education, though the authors judged this too small to change the results. And the findings may not apply outside Sweden.
