In 376 the emperor Valens allowed a large body of Goths, led by Fritigern and Alavivus, to cross the Danube into the Roman empire. They were fleeing Hunnic attacks and had asked to come in. The crossing was a Roman decision, and it was badly managed. Ammianus Marcellinus, who wrote soon afterward, says that the Roman commanders Lupicinus and Maximus exploited the hungry refugees, selling them dog meat and taking Gothic children as the price. The Goths revolted. On 9 August 378, at Adrianople, they destroyed the eastern field army that Valens brought against them, and Valens was killed. Guy Halsall's Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West, 376–568 (Cambridge University Press, 2007) starts from that crisis and refuses to treat it as the arrival of a nation on the march.

His narrative runs through Alaric's sack of Rome in 410, the Vandal capture of Carthage in 439, Attila's campaigns, and the removal of the last western emperor in 476, and it stops at the Lombard entry into Italy in 568. The reading he gives these events is political. The bands that ended up ruling pieces of the west were shaped inside Roman civil wars, Roman recruiting, and Roman attempts to settle armed men in return for military service. In 476 Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus and sent the western imperial regalia to the emperor Zeno in Constantinople. Halsall treats that as a change in how Italy was governed, after decades in which generals and kings already held the real power, rather than as the night a foreign people abolished Roman life.

The date a person chooses is the argument. If 376 is the start of an invasion by intact peoples, then Adrianople is the first crack in a falling building. If 376 is a refugee admission that Roman officers turned into a war, and 476 is a coup by a general the empire had itself armed, then the story is about a state that broke itself and bargained with the soldiers it could still hire. Halsall wants the second story. Names such as Goth and Frank, in his account, are political labels that changed as groups formed and split, not passports carried intact from Scandinavia.

Peter Heather's The Fall of the Roman Empire (2005) uses the same years and puts the weight on the outside. The Huns, in that reading, forced large armed groups across the frontier, and the loss of African taxes after 439 finished the west. The two books agree on the battles and disagree on whether those battles were the cause or the symptom.
