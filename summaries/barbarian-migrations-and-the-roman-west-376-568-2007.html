In 376 the emperor Valens allowed a large body of Goths, recently beaten by the Huns, to cross the Danube into the empire. Roman officers then starved them and sold them bad food, and the Goths revolted. On 9 August 378, at Adrianople, they destroyed the eastern field army and killed Valens. Guy Halsall's Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West, 376–568 (Cambridge University Press, 2007) starts from that disaster and treats it as a Roman failure. The Goths were armed refugees whom a Roman government had admitted and then mishandled. The defeat happened inside a Roman province, against a Roman emperor who had chosen to fight.

He uses the same test on the next crisis. In 406, on the usual dating, Vandals, Alans, and Sueves crossed the Rhine. The western government was already fighting itself. Usurpers raised in Britain and Gaul were at war with the court in Italy, and the field armies were occupied with that war. Halsall's conclusion reverses the familiar order of events. The collapse of the western Roman state produced the migrations. Groups that Roman politics had armed, paid, and settled became the kingdoms of the fifth and sixth centuries because the center that used to manage them had broken. The book closes in 568, when the Lombards entered an Italy that Justinian's long war against the Goths, from 535 to 554, had already ruined. The movement again followed the political damage.

The order changes the evidence you collect. A story of outside peoples destroying a healthy empire sends you to population totals and invasion routes. A story of a state failing, and of armed groups long known to Rome taking the pieces, sends you to civil war, army pay, and the deals generals made. Peter Heather's The Fall of the Roman Empire (2005) reads the same opening years the other way. Hunnic pressure from 376, and the Vandal capture of Carthage on 19 October 439, cut the west off from its richest tax province and, on his account, broke an empire that was still able to function. The two books share the dates. They disagree about which event caused which.
