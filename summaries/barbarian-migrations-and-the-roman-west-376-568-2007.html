A narrative that starts by questioning its own title

Guy Halsall's Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West, 376-568, published in 2007, is a detailed political and archaeological history of the two centuries in which the western empire gave way to kingdoms ruled by military elites that Roman writers called barbarians. The dates are deliberate. He begins with the Gothic crossing of the Danube in 376, the event that leads to Adrianople, and he ends in 568, with the Lombards entering Italy after the wars of Justinian. Between those years he tells a continuous story. He also spends the opening of the book taking apart the assumptions that usually arrive with the phrase barbarian migrations. A migration, in the old textbook sense, is a people on the march, already formed, carrying a single identity from a distant homeland into a Roman province. Halsall does not think that is what the evidence shows.

His first substantive argument is about words and identities. Barbarian is a Roman category. It gathers very different groups by saying what they are not. Goths, Vandals, Franks, Alamanni, Burgundians, Sueves, Alans, and Lombards did not share one language, one origin, or one political habit. Even a single name, Goth or Frank, covers people whose cohesion was made and remade by war, royal families, and service under Rome. Halsall is sceptical of the idea that each of these names hides a stable ethnic core that travelled intact for centuries. He is equally sceptical of any romantic picture in which free Germanic nations overthrew a decadent slave society. The groups that matter in his book are political and military communities, often quite new, formed in contact with the empire and sometimes inside it.

A fourth-century west that still worked

Before the crossings, he describes a western empire that is under strain and still functioning. Taxation is heavy and resented. Civil war between claimants to the throne is a recurring damage. The frontier is a zone of relationship, not a wall with monsters beyond it. Roman policy for generations has settled friendly groups, recruited barbarian soldiers, paid subsidies, and played leaders against each other. Germanic societies near the frontier have become more hierarchical partly because of that relationship: Roman silver, Roman titles, and Roman demand for troops give chieftains the means to build followings. Halsall's fourth century is not Gibbon's exhausted civilisation waiting for a push. It is a powerful state with a dangerous political system, because any successful general can imagine himself as emperor, and the army will sometimes agree.

This is the background against which 376 has to be read. The Goths who ask to cross the Danube are fleeing Hunnic pressure, and Halsall does not deny the reality of that pressure. He denies that the Huns are a sufficient explanation of the western collapse. What happens after the crossing is, in his account, a Roman failure of management and then a Roman civil war. The people admitted in 376 are exploited and mistreated by officials. They revolt. At Adrianople in 378 they destroy an imperial army and kill the emperor Valens. The settlement that follows gives a Gothic military force a place inside the Roman system. Halsall reads the next decades as a struggle over the terms of that place, conducted with Roman titles, Roman enemies, and Roman gold, as much as a foreign invasion.

Civil war, crossing, and settlement

The early fifth century, in his narrative, is a chain of usurpations and regional crises, not a single barbarian wave. Britain produces claimants. The Rhine frontier is crossed at the end of 406 by Vandals, Alans, and Sueves, who move through Gaul into Spain. Alaric's Goths sack Rome in 410 after years of being used and discarded by court politics. Provinces are lost to imperial taxation less because a people sat down on them than because the court in Ravenna can no longer enforce its claim, and because local elites make bargains with the armed groups on the spot. Halsall keeps the violence in view. Bargains were made in the shadow of armies. He resists the further claim that each bargain was the arrival of a nation.

Settlement, as he describes it, is an arrangement about land, tax, and military service, varying by region and poorly reported by the sources. A Gothic or Burgundian king ruling from a Roman city, issuing laws, and employing Roman administrators is a fact. The exact legal form of the land settlement is something historians argue about, and Halsall is frank about the limits of the texts. What he will not do is read a cemetery full of grave goods as a map of ethnic migration. Brooches and weapons express status, fashion, and military identity. They do not, in his view, passport the wearer as a member of a migrating tribe. That methodological warning is one of the book's lasting contributions, because so much of the popular picture of the migrations comes from a too-simple reading of burials.

Africa, Attila, and the end of the western throne

The loss of North Africa to the Vandals, completed with the capture of Carthage in 439, is as serious in Halsall's political narrative as in anyone else's, because Africa was a fiscal pillar of the western state. He does not treat that loss as the result of one Hunnic domino that left Rome no choices. Roman civil wars opened the west. Armed groups already inside the provinces took opportunities. The Vandal kingdom in Africa then made recovery much harder by denying the Italian government grain and tax. Cause, in this telling, is cumulative and political. A reader looking for a single external villain will not find one.

Attila's empire of the mid-fifth century is given its weight as a predatory power on the Danube that extracted subsidies and broke armies, and then as a power that dissolved quickly after his death. Halsall uses that rise and fall to show how fast a barbarian empire could be assembled out of subject peoples and how fast it could come apart. The western imperial office itself ends in 476, when Romulus Augustulus is deposed and not replaced. Halsall refuses both of the easy readings. The year is not nothing, because a court that could still pretend to rule the west disappears, and with it a certain kind of politics. The year is not the end of the Roman world either. Latin, law, cities, and Christian institutions continue. Landowners and bishops remain the people a king has to deal with. The event matters inside a process that began long before it and was not finished after it.

Justinian, the Lombards, and the shape of the argument

The last part of the book carries the story through the eastern empire's attempt to reconquer the west. Justinian's wars, fought by generals such as Belisarius and Narses, destroy the Vandal kingdom and, after a long and ruinous struggle, the Gothic kingdom in Italy. Halsall presents those wars as a disaster for the regions they were meant to restore, whatever they achieved for the prestige of Constantinople. In 568 the Lombards enter a damaged Italy. The end date is not a second fall so much as a marker that the political map of the post-Roman west has taken the shape it will roughly keep: Frankish power in Gaul, a Lombard Italy, a surviving Roman empire in the east, and a North Africa that Justinian recovered and that would later be lost again to the Arab conquests, outside this book's frame.

The sources he trusts least are the ones that tell clean stories of tribal origin. Jordanes on the Goths is used with suspicion. Later writers who project a people's unity back into a heroic past are treated as evidence for the time in which they wrote. The sources he leans on are closer to events, Ammianus above all for the fourth century, and the archaeological record read for economy and settlement rather than for ethnicity. The argument that results is sometimes called internal, because it puts Roman politics first. It is more precise to say that Halsall makes the barbarians real as military actors and refuses to make them ancient nations. The west was not peacefully transformed by agreement, and it was not overrun by peoples who had been marching since the Baltic. It was fought over by Roman factions and by armies that Roman policy had helped to create, until the imperial centre in Italy was no longer worth rebuilding.

Why it matters here

The Folio card heather-huns presents Peter Heather's external-shock account: a functioning fourth-century empire was broken by Hunnic pressure, Gothic refugees, Adrianople, a second wave of crossings, and the loss of African revenue. The card names Halsall's book as the counterweight. This is that counterweight at full length. Halsall grants the Hunnic pressure and the seriousness of 378 and of the Vandal conquest of Africa. He denies the inference. In his pages the decisive damage is the way Roman civil war and court politics turned migration into permanent loss. The barbarians are inside the system before they are against it.

The card brown-late-antiquity is the other connection, and it is not the same one. Brown's transformation is a cultural argument about holy men, saints, and continuity of meaning. Halsall's book is a political narrative that can sit beside that argument because it, too, refuses a simple fall of civilisation to outside peoples. It does not, though, dissolve the fifth century into creative adaptation. The settlements, the fiscal crisis, and the Italian wars are destructive. A reader of the card who wants the gentle version of late antiquity will not find it here. A reader who wants Heather's single external cause will not find that either. What Halsall supplies is the internal sequence: a working western state, undone by its own wars and by armed groups it had helped to form, between 376 and 568.
