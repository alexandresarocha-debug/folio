Style as a stand you take

Clear and Simple as the Truth, by Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner, appeared in 1994. It is a short book with a large definition. A style, they argue, is a conceptual stand: a settled set of answers to questions about truth, presentation, the writer, the reader, thought, and language. Surface habits, short words, few adjectives, a certain punctuation, follow from the stand when they follow at all. Learning to write, on this account, is learning a style, which means learning the answers and meaning them. A list of tips that ignores the answers will produce a surface with nothing underneath, or a mixture of surfaces that belong to incompatible stands. The book then teaches one stand, the classic, by saying what it believes and by setting neighbouring stands beside it so the beliefs can be seen.

Classic style, in their definition, holds that truth can be known and can be shown. Prose is a window. The writer and the reader are competent equals, and the model of their relation is conversation. The motive is disinterested presentation. Language is adequate to the job. The writer has done the thinking before the sentence begins, so the sentence can present a finished perception, with the struggle kept off the page. Thomas and Turner take a line from an eighteenth-century writer on pictures as their emblem: clarity at heart, expressions simple as the truth. The title of their book is that standard. Classic prose succeeds when it feels as if the truth, once seen, could hardly have been said otherwise.

The other stands, and what each is for

The definition gets its edges from the styles they refuse to confuse with the classic. Plain style, as they describe it, speaks inside a community that already shares simple truths and reaffirms them together. Everyone is, in principle, equally in possession of the truth, and the prose has the manner of that equality. Classic style is pickier. It thinks the truth is available to anyone willing to do the work, and it thinks most people, left alone, will slide into special pleading. The classic sentence therefore carries an individual mind's responsibility for a perception, offered to one reader, with a refinement plain reaffirmation would find unnecessary.

Practical style uses writing as an instrument to get something done for a reader who has a job and a shortage of time. The manual, the memorandum, and the instructions are at home there. Classic style, by contrast, presents a subject for its own interest, as if writer and reader had time. Reflexive style keeps the writer's own process in view, the hesitation, the method, the difficulty of saying anything. Classic style treats the window as clean and declines to talk about the glass. Prophetic style speaks from an insight the reader cannot verify by ordinary looking. The prophet is above, or apart, and the reader is asked to receive. Classic style will only present what a competent reader could, in principle, see. Thomas and Turner also mark contemplative, romantic, and oratorical styles: discovery in public, the self as the subject, the crowd to be moved. Each is a coherent stand. The book's complaint is the muddle that comes from mixing them without knowing the mixture.

A museum of examples

The second half of the book is a museum. Thomas and Turner set passages side by side, some they take to be classic and some they take to be something else, and they comment. The range is part of the argument that classic style is a stand, not a period. They can find it in a letter, in a piece of philosophy, in a novelist's sentence, in prose from more than one language and century. A later edition of the book extends the project with exercises, on the view that a stand is learned by practice as well as by recognition. The 1994 book already believes that. Recognition comes first in its pedagogy because you cannot practise a stance you cannot yet see. The commentaries are there to slow the reading down until the stance becomes visible as choices: what the writer assumes you can verify, whether the writer is pointing or confessing, whether the sentence has time for its subject.

The museum method has a risk that the authors accept. Classic style is partly in the eye of the trained reader, and two competent readers can disagree about a passage. Thomas and Turner write as teachers of a taste, with examples chosen to make the taste shareable. They are at their best when a contrast makes a single feature undeniable, a sentence that shows you the thing against a sentence that shows you the writer's anxiety about the thing. They are less interested in historical fairness to the other styles. Prophetic, practical, and reflexive prose have achievements the book mentions in order to set them aside. A reader who loves those achievements can still use the contrast. Knowing which stand you are in is the lesson, even if you decline the invitation to live in the classic one.

What Pinker, and any writer, can take

Steven Pinker borrows the classic stand almost whole in The Sense of Style, and he pairs it with the curse of knowledge. The pairing is natural. A writer cursed by expertise cannot point at a shareable world, because the world in his head includes a private layer the reader cannot verify. Classic style makes that failure visible. If you are unwilling to show the thing, if you hide behind method-talk, or if you write as a member of a priesthood, you have left the stand. The cure is not a shorter word list. The cure is the decision to address an equal about something both of you could see, and then to cut every sentence that fails the decision. Thomas and Turner give Pinker, and anyone else, a way to say what clarity is for. It is for presentation of a truth the reader is trusted to recognise.

The book is itself an attempt at the style it describes, which is the right test and a fair one to apply. Where the argument is a clean pointing at a passage, the book practises what it preaches. Where it multiplies distinctions among styles, it asks a reader to hold a system, which is a slightly different pleasure, closer to a map than to a window. Both pleasures are real. A map of stands is what working writers often lack, and it explains the familiar misery of a paragraph that has changed its mind about the reader three times. Practical, reflexive, and classic aims can all be legitimate. They cannot all govern one sentence. Clear and Simple as the Truth is a training in noticing which aim has the pen.

Why it matters here

The card on the curse of knowledge names this book as Pinker's source for classic style, and it lists the neighbouring stands, plain, practical, reflexive, and prophetic, because the source defines the classic by their company. Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber show that knowledge itself makes other minds hard to simulate. Thomas and Turner show what kind of prose tries, anyway, to put a world in front of an equal. Read them for the definition and for the museum, the place where the definition has to survive contact with actual sentences. The card's practical hope, that a draft shown to someone outside the writer's private knowledge will improve, is a social version of their stand. Another person can see the glass. Classic style is the decision to write as if the glass should not be the subject.
