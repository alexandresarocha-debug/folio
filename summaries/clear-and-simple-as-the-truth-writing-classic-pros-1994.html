Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner take their definition of classic style from an eighteenth-century picture dealer. Jean-Baptiste Le Brun, writing on how to judge paintings, said that clarity was what he cared about, that his style would not be florid, and that his expressions would be "simple as the truth." They read that sentence as a stand the writer takes. The subject is already out there. The writer has looked at it. The reader is competent to see it. Prose is the window they both look through, and the window is doing its job when nobody stops to discuss the glass.

Le Brun's job makes the stand concrete. He believes a life of looking has taught him which paintings are good, and that if he places the reader at the right spot the reader will recognize the same thing. Thomas and Turner add that you do not have to share his metaphysics to use the style. For him the stand was probably a conviction. Taken as a working convention, it still produces the clarity. The model scene is a conversation between equals. In this style the writer presents something already seen, and is supposed to know the thing before the sentence starts.

They set a passage of Mark Twain beside that definition. "When I was a boy, there was but one permanent ambition among my comrades in our village on the west bank of the Mississippi River. That was, to be a steamboatman." The sentence points at a village and a wish. It treats the wish as what anyone standing there would have seen. The prose spends nothing on how hard Twain worked to remember it.

A lab report, a doctor's explanation, or a note to a customer written this way shows the object and leaves the writer's struggle off the page. The reader is treated as someone who can see. The risk is built in. A false claim, aimed through a clean window, looks as if it were simply there. Thomas and Turner are describing a manner of presentation, and a manner can be aimed at the wrong object and remain beautifully clear. That is why the style asks for a thing you have actually looked at, rather than a performance of looking.
