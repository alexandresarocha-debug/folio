A craft book for a kind of play that had a shape

Gustav Freytag's Die Technik des Dramas, published in 1863, is a manual of dramatic construction written by a working novelist and playwright. Its material is the five-act tragedy as Freytag found it in Greek drama, in Shakespeare, and in the serious German stage of his own century. He was not offering a psychology of audiences, and he was not offering a theory of all stories. He was describing, with the confidence of a craftsman who thinks the good plays already display the pattern, how a tragic action rises and then falls. The diagram that later teaching extracted from the book, Freytag's pyramid, has outlived the particular theatre he wrote for. It has also been simplified until it sometimes says less than he said. The book itself is an argument about the parts of a tragic plot and about the forces that move a hero from the opening situation to the catastrophe.

Freytag's premise is that a drama is a single movement, not a slice of life and not a chain of notable scenes. The movement has a direction. Something exciting enters a settled situation, the consequences climb, a turning point is reached, the counterforce dominates, and the action is closed by a disaster that settles the conflict. Because he is writing about tragedy, the close is not a restoration and not a wedding. It is a catastrophe. Comedies and novels can borrow pieces of the machine. The machine, as he analyses it, is built to produce a fall that feels both inevitable and earned. Folio's card places this machine at the start of a history that runs forward to computational shapes of stories. The forward glance is fair only if the machine is described as the tragic structure it is, rather than as a vague mountain of tension.

Exposition and the exciting force

The first part of the movement is the exposition, the introduction. Its job is to put the audience in possession of the present situation, the principal persons, and the mood in which the action will be intelligible, without front-loading a chronicle. Freytag is impatient with openings that explain more than the coming action needs. The exposition should already be dramatic, already a scene rather than a briefing, and it should lead quickly to what he calls the exciting force, the moment that starts the conflict. The exciting force is not yet the climax. It is the entrance of the will, the event or the decision after which the hero is in motion and cannot return to the opening balance by simply changing the subject. In a five-act tragedy this material belongs to the first act. The test of an exposition, on his account, is whether the rising action can begin at once, and whether the audience knows enough to feel the necessity of what follows and not so much that the following has been pre-empted.

This is already a stricter demand than the classroom pyramid usually conveys. Exposition in the crude diagram looks like background, the flat ground before the climb. In Freytag's account it is the first act of the construction, and it fails if it is mere background. It also fails if it is so complete that the play has no ignorance left to exploit. The audience should understand the situation and still be available for what the later acts reveal. The relation to Aristotle is obvious and incomplete. Aristotle wanted a beginning that does not itself follow from something else in the plot. Freytag wants that, and he also wants a practical division of the beginning into a present situation and a force that disturbs it, because he is telling a playwright how to spend a first act.

Rising action, climax, and the turn

The rising action is the climb. Complications accumulate, the hero's purpose meets obstacles, and the stakes become clearer. Freytag treats this as the longest and, for the playwright, the most inventive part of the serious drama. It is where the play earns the right to its turning point, by making the audience understand what the hero wants and what is gathering against it. Episodes that do not bear on that opposition are indulgences. The rising action is not a series of escalations in volume. It is a series of consequences. Each scene should leave the hero more committed, or more endangered, than the scene before, so that the apex, when it comes, is a change of direction rather than a louder repetition.

The climax, in Freytag's pyramid, sits at the apex, often in the third act of a five-act play. He does not mean by climax the moment of greatest noise, which is how the word is often used now. He means the turning point, the height at which the exciting force has done all it can and the counter-action takes the lead. Before this point, the hero's movement is ascendant. After it, the tragic force pressing down on the hero is ascendant. The climax can be a quiet scene if the quiet scene is the one in which the direction changes. Calling every peak of excitement a climax flattens the term Freytag needed, because he needed a name for the structural turn, not a name for the audience's pulse. A play that puts its loudest scene in the last act may still have had its climax, in his sense, much earlier, at the moment the fall became the true subject.

Return or fall, and the catastrophe

What follows the apex is the return or the fall, the falling action. The word return is useful because it keeps the geometry honest. The action comes back down. The forces introduced on the way up now work in the other direction, and the hero, who has been the pursuer, becomes the pursued. Freytag does not treat this descent as a brief slide between the exciting bit and the ending. It is a substantial phase, with its own scenes, and it is where many modern plays, built on a rise towards a final explosion, have the least to learn from him and the most to distort him. He also describes a moment of final suspense late in the fall, a delay in which a different outcome still seems possible. The delay is not an accident of pacing. It keeps the catastrophe from feeling mechanical. The audience should be allowed, briefly, to hope, so that the closing disaster arrives as a defeat of a real alternative and not as the next item on a list.

The catastrophe is the settling of the conflict in the hero's ruin. It answers the exciting force. What was opened in the first act is closed here, not by an explanation but by an action that makes further climbing impossible. Freytag's catastrophe is the tragic denouement, the death or the destruction or the irreversible loss towards which the fall has been moving. A happy ending is a different machine. Writers who draw his pyramid and then place a victory at the bottom right-hand corner are drawing a different structure and borrowing his picture. The picture was of a rise towards a turn and a fall towards disaster. The moral and emotional justification of the disaster, for Freytag, lies in the unity of the movement. The hero is significant enough for the fall to matter, the fall follows from the clash of purposes, and the catastrophe is the last consequence rather than a punishment added by the playwright from outside.

What the pyramid is not

The book's influence has cost it some of its specificity. Taught as a universal diagram, the pyramid suggests that all stories ought to rise and fall in this way, and that the middle is always the exciting part. Freytag's own limits are clearer. He is analysing tragedy. He assumes acts. He assumes a hero whose personal catastrophe can stand for the close of the action. Ensemble plays, tragicomedies, plots of recovery, and stories that end in knowledge rather than in ruin fit the diagram badly, and the bad fit is not a failure of those stories. It is a sign that the diagram was a technique for one dramatic tradition. Even inside that tradition, the identification of climax with the third-act turn can mislead a reader of Shakespeare, whose plays often carry their greatest pressure later than a symmetric pyramid suggests. Freytag knew his examples were various. The textbook triangle is more dogmatic than the book.

There is also a difference, which later work makes vivid, between a dramatic structure and an emotional curve. Freytag's parts are parts of an action: force, complication, turn, descent, disaster. A curve of good and bad fortune, of the kind Kurt Vonnegut liked to sketch and of the kind a sentiment analysis might recover from a novel, can rise and fall for reasons that have nothing to do with a tragic counter-action. A comedy can trace a pyramid of anxiety and relief. A novel can trace several. Using Freytag's five names for those curves is a convenience that hides his argument. His argument was that tragic action has a necessary order, and that the playwright's technique is the art of building that order so the catastrophe is the consequence of the opening force.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, From Freytag's pyramid to the six shapes of stories, begins with this book and then leaves it. The value of the model is a vocabulary for tension, turning points and release. The vocabulary is sharper if the climax is kept as a turn and the falling action is kept as a real phase, which is Freytag's use, and if the catastrophe is kept as a tragic close. Vonnegut's graphs of fortune, and the 2016 study in which Reagan and his colleagues found six dominant emotional shapes in a large body of fiction, are the card's later chapters. They inherit the hunch that stories have a small set of shapes. They do not inherit the five-act tragic machine.
