Gustav Freytag, in Die Technik des Dramas (1863), takes Romeo and Juliet apart as a pyramid. The action rises from an introduction, through the exciting force, up to a climax, and then falls to a catastrophe. Between those points sit the rise and the fall. In this play he finds four stages on the way up. First the masked ball, built from short scenes: Juliet with her mother and the nurse, Romeo and his friends, then the ball itself, with Tybalt's anger and the lovers' first talk. Second, the garden, where they decide to marry. Third, the marriage, with Friar Laurence and the nurse kept in front and the lovers partly hidden. Fourth, Tybalt's death. The climax, for Freytag, is not the tomb. It is the group of scenes that begins with Juliet's "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" and runs through Romeo's farewell. Tybalt's death is the break that separates the long rise from that turning scene. After it, the play belongs to the counter-action: the lovers are driven apart, toward the deaths in the vault.

He uses Julius Caesar to show that the same shape can be built with fewer steps. There the rise, from the moment of excitation to the climax, is a single stage, the conspiracy. The scenes grouped around the murder are the climax. Five parts, he says, can each be one scene or a run of scenes, but the climax is usually one chief scene. The picture he offers is a pyramid because the two halves of the action meet at that high point. Modern retellings of "Freytag's pyramid" often slide the climax to the end and call the last disaster the peak. In his own reading of Romeo and Juliet, the peak is the turn in the middle, when the result of the rise comes out and the fall begins. The tomb is the catastrophe, the end of the descent, not the summit.

The diagram is useful when you are lost in a draft and need to ask where the turn is, and whether the second half is really answering the first. It is a bad master if you treat every story as a five-act tragedy with a ruin at the end. Freytag was writing about serious drama on the nineteenth-century stage, and about plays he thought were well built, Shakespeare among them. A comedy that ends in a wedding, or a novel that rises and falls twice, is not a failed pyramid. It is a different shape. What still travels is the demand that the middle do work: the audience should be able to feel the moment after which the story is no longer climbing.
