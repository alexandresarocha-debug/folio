A popular idea says that heritability of height should be low where living standards are poor, and rise as nutrition and health improve and the environment becomes more even. This large study tested that idea directly, and the answer was not what many people expect.

Aline Jelenkovic and colleagues, working in a consortium of twin registries, pooled individual data from 40 twin cohorts with 143,390 complete twin pairs born between 1886 and 1994. That span covers people who grew up before and after big improvements in living standards. The cohorts came mostly from Europe, North America and Australia, and East Asia, with two from South Asia and the Middle East.

Average height rose over the birth years, and the rise from the 1940s to the 1980s cohorts was much larger in East Asia than elsewhere. The total variation in height also tended to grow, and so did the genetic variation. Yet the heritability estimates did not show a clear trend. They ranged from 0.69 to 0.84 in men and 0.53 to 0.78 in women across the birth-year groups. For example, men born in 1886 to 1909 had a heritability of 0.78, very similar to later cohorts.

Comparing regions, total height variation was greatest in North America and Australia and lowest in East Asia, but again there was no clear pattern in heritability. The authors' conclusion is blunt: their findings do not support the hypothesis that heritability of height is lower in populations with low living standards, nor that it will rise within a population as living standards improve.

How does that fit the theory? Heritability is a ratio. Better conditions can raise genetic and environmental variation together, leaving the ratio about the same. The study did find a modest but significant increase in the genetic variance with birth year.

What this means in real life: the idea that rich, uniform environments push heritability up is reasonable in principle but does not show up neatly in a century of height data from these countries. Real populations are messier than the thought experiment.

Caution: the authors note that the data are heavily weighted toward populations with Westernised lifestyles, and none of the 40 cohorts came from Africa, so the study cannot settle what happens under the poorest conditions.

Source. Jelenkovic, A., Hur, Y.-M., Sund, R., Yokoyama, Y., Siribaddana, S. H., Hotopf, M., et al. (2016). Genetic and environmental influences on adult human height across birth cohorts from 1886 to 1994. eLife, 5, e20320. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.20320
