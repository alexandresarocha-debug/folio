Most bystander research asks whether a particular individual helps. Richard Philpot and colleagues asked the question that matters to a victim: will anyone help me? To answer it, they turned to footage from city-centre surveillance cameras in Lancaster in the United Kingdom, Amsterdam in the Netherlands and Cape Town in South Africa.

Camera operators had been instructed to record any public conflict, from mild arguments to serious violence. From a raw sample of 1,225 clips the team selected 219 that showed a conflict between at least two people in an inner-city setting. Coders counted the bystanders and recorded acts of intervention, such as pacifying gestures, calming touches, blocking or pulling an aggressor away, and consoling or helping a victim.

At least one bystander intervened in 90.9% of the conflicts, and on average 3.76 people stepped in. The rates were similar across the three countries, even though they differ greatly in how safe their public spaces feel. The average incident had about 16 bystanders, and more bystanders meant more chance of help: each extra person raised the odds that someone intervened by a factor of about 1.1.

The authors argue that this does not overturn the classic bystander effect, which concerns the likelihood that each person helps. But for victims, the overall picture is reassuring: in nine of ten real conflicts, someone does something.

What this means in real life: the fear that strangers will simply watch is mostly unfounded, and training that helps people intervene well may matter more than shaming them for doing nothing.

Caution: the clips had no sound, so spoken interventions were missed, while the coding counted mild acts such as consolation. Data came from busy night-time areas with bars and clubs, not from townships or quieter places, and the study looked at public conflicts rather than medical emergencies.
