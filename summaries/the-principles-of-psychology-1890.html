In The Principles of Psychology, published in 1890, William James refuses the usual picture of thinking as a chain of separate ideas. "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits. Such words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly as it presents itself in the first instance. It is nothing jointed; it flows." A river or a stream, he says, is the metaphor that fits, and he proposes to call it the stream of thought, of consciousness, or of subjective life.

His answer to the obvious objection is a thunderclap. A silence can be broken so hard that for a moment you cannot say what happened. That confusion is itself a mental state, and it carries you from the silence to the sound. The things you notice may arrive as separate blows. The thought of them does not break. "What we hear when the thunder crashes is not thunder pure, but thunder-breaking-upon-silence-and-contrasting-with-it." Every definite image, he adds, sits in a halo of what it came from and where it is going. Traditional psychology, in his comparison, talks as if a river were nothing but the pails and pots someone had dipped into it, and ignores the free water between them.

He finds a second rhythm inside the stream. Seen from a height, consciousness alternates like a bird's life, flights and perchings. The resting places he calls the substantive parts, usually a sensation or an image you can hold still. The flights are the transitive parts, thoughts of the relations between those images. The rhythm of language follows the same alternation, he says: a thought becomes a sentence, and a sentence closes on a period, while the flight that got you there is the part traditional psychology leaves out.

This is why a transcript of your own mind, or a novel that tries to follow it, is full of leftovers. You do not hear an alarm as an alarm and nothing else. You hear it against the dream it cut off. The join is part of the experience. A list of topics misses the transition, and the transition is where the feeling lives. Virginia Woolf and James Joyce write that leftover in. James's point, for anyone paying attention on an ordinary morning, is that the mind arrives already stained by the second before.
