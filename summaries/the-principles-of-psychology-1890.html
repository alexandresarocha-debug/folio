A textbook that is also a piece of literature

The Principles of Psychology, published in 1890 in two volumes, is William James's great book on the mind, written across years of teaching at Harvard and of delay he complained about and used. It is a founding text of the discipline in America and a book people still read for the prose. James wanted psychology to be a natural science of mental life, using introspection carefully, together with experiment, nervous physiology, and comparison across animals and cultures. He also wanted the psychologist to stop committing a fallacy he names: smuggling the observer's own knowledge of a situation into the description of the mind that is in the situation. The book is huge. Habit, the stream of thought, the self, attention, memory, sensation, emotion, will, and the relations between minds fill it. Folio's card on stream of consciousness draws on one chapter. The chapter is clearer if you know what kind of book agreed to contain it.

James writes as a man who has felt the phenomena he classifies. The chapter on habit treats habit as the flywheel of society, the thing that keeps trades, morals, and manners running without a fresh decision at every step, and it advises the young to make their habits while the making is cheap. The chapter on emotion argues that the bodily commotion is not a mere consequence of a mental feeling but part of what the feeling is, the view later paired with the Danish physiologist Lange. The chapters on will include the famous domestic scene of the man who stays in a warm bed on a cold morning, knowing he must rise, until somehow, without a separate heroic fiat, he finds that he is up. These passages are arguments. They are also proof that James thought a psychology which loses the texture of experience has lost its subject.

The stream, and the five characters he gives it

The chapter called The Stream of Thought is the one that named a metaphor the novelists later borrowed. James denies that consciousness appears to itself chopped into bits. It is nothing jointed. It flows. A chain or a train, he says, are poor metaphors, because they keep the joints. A river or a stream is the metaphor that fits. Inside that claim he lists characters of thought that any later user of the phrase ought to remember, because the phrase without them becomes a licence for formless prose.

Thought, first, tends to be part of a personal consciousness. There is no experience floating free of an owner in the way he is willing to count as a fact of psychology. Second, within each personal consciousness, thought is always changing. A state that has gone does not return as the identical state, because the mind it returns to has already been changed by it. Third, within each personal consciousness, thought is sensibly continuous. Gaps in time, such as sleep, do not feel, on waking, like a change of owner. The stream goes into the bank and comes out as the same stream. Fourth, thought appears to deal with objects independent of itself. It is about something. Fifth, it is interested in some parts of its object and indifferent to others. It chooses, welcomes, rejects, all the while. The stream is personal, changing, continuous, intentional, and selective. Leave out the last and you have a weather report. James's stream is a weather report with a will in it.

Resting places, flights, and the fringe

The chapter's most original move, for a reader of fiction, is the distinction between substantive and transitive parts of the stream. Substantive parts are the resting places, the images and conclusions the mind can hold up and name. Transitive parts are the flights between them, the feelings of and, if, and but, the sense of a relation coming, the feeling of tendency that precedes the word. James insists that the flights are real mental facts. A psychology that collects only the resting places will describe thought as a sequence of beads and will miss the string, and the string is where the thinking happens. He asks the reader to notice, by a difficult kind of introspection, the moment just before a word arrives, when the emptiness is a specific emptiness, shaped for that word and wrong for another.

He calls the vague surround of a thought its fringe or halo. A word in a sentence carries a sense of how it fits the sentence's drift, a feeling of rightness or wrongness that is not itself a second, sharper word. Tip-of-the-tongue states, the feeling of knowing what you mean while the name stays out, belong to this account. So does the experience of recognising that a proposed phrase has the wrong fringe, even when you cannot yet give the rule it violates. James is defending the vague as a proper object of science. Vagueness with a definite direction is not ignorance. It is a kind of content, and any theory of meaning that waits for fully verbalised bits will be late to the mind's actual work. Novelists who later tried to put the stream on the page were, whether they had read this chapter or absorbed its climate, trying to give the transitive parts and the fringe a syntax.

From a metaphor to a narrative art

James himself is not writing a theory of the novel. The applications come in the next generation. Dorothy Richardson's long sequence, beginning in 1915, was described by May Sinclair in 1918 as stream of consciousness, the phrase crossing from psychology into criticism. Joyce, Woolf, and Faulkner then made distinct arts out of the problem James had set: how to represent a flow that is continuous from the inside and selective at every moment, owned by someone, and thick with relations that have not yet hardened into named ideas. The distinctions critics now insist on, between stream of consciousness as a subject and interior monologue as a technique, are ways of keeping James's phenomenon from being collapsed into one literary device. A novel can represent the stream by direct first-person rush, by a narrator who dips in and out, or by a style that mimics the fringe without claiming to be a transcript.

Reading the 1890 chapter beside those novels shows both the debt and the surplus. James already has the personal owner, the continuous change, the aboutness, the choosing, and the reality of the in-between. He does not have Molly Bloom's unpunctuated memory, or Clarissa Dalloway's day moving among several owners, or the idea that a whole city's styles might be the stream. Those are literary discoveries. They sit on a psychological permission he wrote down, that inner life is a flow with structure, and that the structure includes parts we are bad at naming. The Principles of Psychology gives the permission in a prose that already knows how to narrate a mind. His bed, his river, his wordless intention are demonstrations. The science and the literature are not, in his hands, different temperatures.

Why it matters here

The card on stream of consciousness begins with this book, because the phrase begins here, as a rejection of jointed metaphors and as a list of characters thought actually has. It then follows the phrase into Richardson, Joyce, Woolf, and Faulkner. The Principles of Psychology is also one of the texts the card offers to read at length, which suits a chapter that asks to be felt as well as summarised. Read The Stream of Thought for the five characters and for the flights between resting places. Then read Mrs Dalloway and Ulysses as two different arts of the same problem. James gives you the river, the owner, the choosing, and the halo around the word. The novelists give you days, cities, and sentences that try to keep the halo on the page.
