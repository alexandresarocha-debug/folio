Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn published a result that cannot be true, and then showed the machinery that made it look true. University of Pennsylvania students who listened to the Beatles song "When I'm Sixty-Four" were, by their birth dates, nearly a year and a half younger than students who listened to an instrumental called "Kalimba." Adjusted for father's age, the means were 20.1 years and 21.5 years, F(1, 17) = 4.92, p = .040. The write-up said the sample was 20 students. A song cannot change a birthday. That was the point.

The same paper then restored the cuts. There were 34 students, not 20, and a third track, "Hot Potato," by the Wiggles. The researchers had not fixed the sample size. They looked after sessions of about 10 people and stopped when the result appeared. They had collected a long list of other answers, from how old people felt, to a parent's age, to which of four Canadian quarterbacks had won an award, and father's age was the control that delivered the significant comparison. Without it the ages were 20.3 and 21.2, and the difference was not significant, p = .33. In the companion study, 30 students who heard "Hot Potato" said they felt older than students who heard "Kalimba." Adjusted for father's age, the means were 2.54 against 2.06 on a five-point scale, F(1, 27) = 5.06, p = .033. The paper then shows how ordinary flexibility, including a covariate and a choice of which condition to report, can produce a result like that.

The authors treat this as a normal risk of ordinary researcher choices, not as fraud. Decide the sample size before anyone is run, and collect at least 20 observations per group unless there is a real reason the data are expensive. List every measure you collected and every condition you ran, including the ones that did nothing. A significant p-value can be the one analysis, among many that were available after the fact, that crossed the line. When a finding is surprising enough to change a policy, a product, or a purchase, ask whether the comparison, the stopping point, and the control variable were chosen before the data were in. In this paper, the Beatles made people younger only in the analysis that was free to pick its sample, its song, and its covariate.

Source. Joseph P. Simmons, Leif D. Nelson, and Uri Simonsohn, 2011, "False-Positive Psychology: Undisclosed Flexibility in Data Collection and Analysis Allows Presenting Anything as Significant," Psychological Science, 22(11), 1359–1366. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417632
