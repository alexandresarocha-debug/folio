Background

By 2011 many psychologists already suspected that journals were full of results that would not survive a second look. What they lacked was a clean demonstration that ordinary, respectable analytic habits could manufacture a finding. Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn published that demonstration in Psychological Science. They called the habits researcher degrees of freedom: the choices, often made after the data are in, about when to stop collecting, which outcome to treat as the real one, which participants to set aside, and which extra variable to hold constant. None of these choices looks like fraud. Each can be defended in a sentence. Together they let a researcher keep fishing until a conventional significance test comes out the desired way.

The paper sits upstream of the phrase p-hacking, which the same authors helped to popularise later. In 2011 the claim was narrower and more uncomfortable. The problem was not a few bad actors. It was a literature in which flexibility was invisible, in which only the successful path through the data was written up, and in which a significant result was treated as a finding about the world rather than as one survivor of many unspoken tests. Folio's replication card treats this paper as the method behind the crisis. The walkthrough below is the method itself.

The question

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn asked two questions that are easy to blur. First, how large can the false-positive rate become if a researcher uses the flexibilities that were then common and reports only the version that worked? A false positive, here, means declaring an effect when the hypothesis is false and the only thing generating the pattern is chance plus those choices. Second, would a reader be able to tell? If the published paper looks like an ordinary experiment, with a prediction, a method and a significant test, the flexibility is not a footnote. It is invisible.

They answered with a simulation of the analytic choices, and with real experiments whose true effect was known because the claimed effect was impossible. The impossible result is the one people remember. The simulation is what shows that the result was not a conjuring trick unique to these authors. It was the arithmetic of practices already in wide use.

How the studies were set up

The simulation considered a researcher who is willing to use four freedoms, singly or together. The researcher may choose among more than one dependent variable, reporting the one that yields a difference. The researcher may collect an initial sample and, if the result is not yet significant, add further observations and test again. The researcher may include or drop a covariate. The researcher may run an extra condition and then set it aside if it spoils the story. Each choice has a benign description. Adding data looks like thoroughness. A covariate looks like statistical control. Dropping a condition looks like a failed manipulation that need not clutter the paper. The simulation asks what the nominal five-per-cent test does when these decisions are available and only the flattering combination is disclosed.

The live demonstration used a hypothesis no one could honestly believe. Participants listened to music. One track was the Beatles song When I'm Sixty-Four. The comparison was another piece of music. Afterwards the researchers had available the participants' actual ages, along with other measures, including the father's age, that could be used or ignored as covariates. Chronological age cannot be changed by a song played in a laboratory session. If a standard analysis could nevertheless report that the song made people younger, the analysis, and the selective report of it, would be the whole explanation. A second demonstration in the same paper used the same logic on a different judgement, so that the point would not rest on a single whimsical outcome. The details of the second study matter less than the shared structure: collect several measures, leave the stopping rule loose, and write up the path that crosses the significance line.

What they found

In the simulation, each freedom on its own pushed the false-positive rate above the level the test advertises. Combinations pushed it much further. A researcher who used the set of freedoms together, and who reported the analysis that worked, was in a position to declare a significant effect in a large share of studies in which nothing was there. The exact rate depends on which freedoms are combined and how far the researcher is willing to go. The qualitative result does not. The published star is not, under those conditions, evidence that the hypothesis was likely to be true. It is evidence that a flexible search was allowed to stop when it succeeded.

The song study delivered the illustration they wanted. Using the freedoms the simulation had described, including a covariate choice that would look unremarkable in a methods section, they obtained a statistically significant effect on actual age. Listening to When I'm Sixty-Four, on that reported analysis, made people younger. The result is absurd, which is why it teaches. A reader who saw only the final test would have no strong reason to doubt the procedure. The paper looked like a paper. The impossibility of the conclusion is the only alarm, and most hypotheses in social psychology are not impossible. They are merely unlikely, or small, or true only in a corner of the design. Absurdity will not be available to protect the reader.

How the authors read the results

Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn did not conclude that significance tests should be abandoned, or that every published effect is a fiction. They concluded that the test's advertised error rate is a promise about a procedure the field was not following. The procedure assumes that the comparisons, the sample size and the covariates were fixed before the researcher looked at the outcome. Once those are chosen because of the outcome, the reported probability no longer means what the paper says it means. Disclosure of the successful analysis, without disclosure of the routes not taken, does not restore the meaning. A reader cannot adjust for choices that are never described.

Their remedy was a set of requirements for authors, modest on the page and severe in practice. Decide the rule for stopping data collection before the data are collected, and report the rule. Collect enough observations per condition that a result is not a handful of people, or explain why the data were unavoidably scarce; they suggested a floor of twenty observations per cell as a minimum of decency. List every variable that was measured. Report every experimental condition, including those that failed. If any observations are excluded, also report the result with those observations left in. If a covariate is used, also report the result without it. None of these rules makes a false hypothesis true or a true one false. They make the garden of forking paths visible, and they remove the easiest ways to wander it until a star appears. The authors argued that journals could enforce the rules, and that reviewers should treat undisclosed flexibility as a reason to discount a result rather than as a private matter of analytic taste.

Limits

The paper is a proof of possibility and a portrait of incentives. It is not a count of how many published papers are false, and it does not try to be. A simulation can show what a motivated analyst can extract from noise. It cannot show how often analysts in fact used the full set of freedoms, or how often a flexible analysis happened to land on a true effect. The song study is a demonstration, built to succeed at showing a falsehood. It is not a random sample of laboratory practice. A defender of the literature can say, fairly, that many researchers felt constrained by a plan even when they did not write the plan down, and that editors sometimes demanded the extra analyses that this paper treats as temptations.

There is a second limit inside the remedy. Reporting rules help a careful reader. They do less, by themselves, about publication bias, the preference of journals for positive results, which Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn assumed as the background pressure that makes flexibility rewarding. A fully disclosed null is still a null, and in 2011 a null was still hard to publish. Later reforms, registered reports in particular, attack that incentive directly by accepting a paper before the result is known. This paper prepared that move. It did not complete it. Nor did the authors claim that every small study is worthless. Their claim is that a small, flexible, selectively reported study cannot carry the confidence that its p-value suggests.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the replication crisis says that the causes were already understood before a hundred replications made them famous. This is the paper that made the causes tangible. Optional stopping, a menu of outcomes, a dropped condition and a convenient covariate are the practices the card lists. The Beatles result is the card's example of a hypothesis made to look supported when it cannot be true. When the Open Science Collaboration later found that only about a third of replications were statistically significant, one reading of the gap is exactly the reading this paper licenses. Many original papers had used freedom that the replications, often better specified and less willing to shop for a result, refused to use.

The same lens falls on the effects Folio treats as cautionary. A large preregistered multilab study of ego depletion, the one organised by Martin Hagger and colleagues in 2016, did not recover the original effect. Hormonal claims about power posing failed in a similar way. Those later facts are not in Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn. The habit of mind is. A surprising result from a flexible analysis is a reason to ask what else was tried, not a reason to rewrite a textbook. Pre-registration, open data and the simple decency of reporting the result without the covariate are this paper's legacy, and they are the practices the card points to when it says the crisis was also a correction.
