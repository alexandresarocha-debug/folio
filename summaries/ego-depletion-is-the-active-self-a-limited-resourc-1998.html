Background

In 1998 Roy Baumeister, Ellen Bratslavsky, Mark Muraven and Dianne Tice asked whether self-control behaves like a muscle. Their paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, Ego Depletion: Is the Active Self a Limited Resource?, answered that it does. An act of self-control, they argued, draws down a limited inner resource, so that the next act has less to draw on. The demonstration that launched the programme is the one Folio's card retells. People who had been told to resist freshly baked chocolate-chip cookies, and to eat radishes instead, gave up sooner on a subsequent unsolvable puzzle than people who had been allowed to eat the cookies. The resource account, which Baumeister called ego depletion, organised hundreds of studies and a popular book before large preregistered replications found the effect close to zero. This paper is the beginning of that arc, and it has to be read as a beginning.

The card's caution is part of the reading. Treat ego depletion as unproven. A walkthrough that recounted the radishes as established physiology would be repeating the claim the replications put in doubt. A walkthrough that mocked the radishes without saying what the original studies actually did would be dodging the interest of the idea. The idea was specific, testable and, for a time, enormously productive.

The question

Does using the active self on one task leave less of it for the next? Baumeister and his colleagues were opposing a picture in which self-control is a skill or a personality trait, available at full strength whenever it is called, and differing across people but not across the previous half-hour. Their alternative was a state. Whatever the trait, a person who has just restrained an impulse, suppressed a thought, or forced a choice is in a temporarily poorer condition for the next restraint. The question is whether that poorer condition shows up in behaviour under controlled comparison: same second task, different first task, one of them demanding self-control and the other not.

How the studies were set up

The cookie study is the prototype. Participants arrived hungry. In one condition the room contained warm cookies and a bowl of radishes, and the instruction was to eat the radishes and leave the cookies alone. In another, the cookies were permitted. A third group did not face the food. Everyone then received puzzles presented as a measure of problem-solving. The puzzles could not be solved. The measure was how long people persisted before giving up. If resisting a temptation spends a resource, the radish group should persist for less time than the group that ate the cookies, and perhaps for less time than the group that was never tempted. The forbidden cookies are there to make the first task a genuine override of an impulse, rather than a neutral warm-up.

The paper did not rest on one contrast. Other experiments varied the first act of self-control. Some participants suppressed their emotional expression while watching an upsetting film. Some tried not to think of a white bear, the classic thought-suppression task. Later work in the same programme, and further studies in the paper's spirit, treated effortful choice itself as depleting, on the view that choosing is something the active self does. The second tasks varied as well: persistence, physical stamina, solvable problems that reward continued effort. The family resemblance is the sequence. A first task that spends self-control, a second task that needs it, and a comparison group whose first task does not spend it. Ego depletion is the name for a worse second task in that design.

What they found

In the cookie experiment, the people who had resisted the cookies and eaten radishes gave up sooner on the unsolvable puzzles than the people who had been free to eat. The restraint that looked, from outside, like a small piece of politeness was followed by less persistence on a task that had nothing to do with food. The other experiments in the paper found the same shape of result. After an act of self-regulation, performance or persistence on the next demanding task declined relative to a control group that had not regulated. The decline was not, in the authors' reading, a matter of being in a bad mood, of thinking the experiment was silly, or of failing to understand the second task. Those alternatives were the ones the comparisons were built to press.

The theoretical sentence they attached to the pattern was strong. The active self, the executive that initiates action, makes choices and overrides responses, operates on a limited resource. The resource is shared across dissimilar acts, which is why cookies can affect puzzles and why an emotion suppressed in one room can show up as a weaker handgrip in the next. It replenishes with rest. It can, they suggested, be trained, which is where the muscle metaphor did more than illustrate. A muscle tires in the short run and strengthens in the long run. The paper offered that double life as the reason a limited resource need not mean a grim determinism. The short-run claim is the one the experiments tested. The long-run claim was a promise to a programme.

How the authors read the results

Baumeister and colleagues read the studies as evidence for a single hidden quantity, spent by many surface activities. That unification was the paper's appeal. Willpower, which folk psychology had treated as a stuff one has more or less of, became a stuff one has recently spent. The unification also generated predictions faster than most social-psychological theories of the period. Any two acts of self-control, however unlike in content, should interfere if they are placed close together. Any manipulation that restored the resource should repair the second act. The later glucose hypothesis, the claim that a sugary drink undoes depletion because the resource is fuel, was one such prediction, pursued in subsequent papers rather than in this one. It is part of the programme's history and not a finding of 1998. The 1998 claim is the limited resource and the sequential impairment.

They were explicit that the resource is inferred, not observed. Nobody measured a tank. The tank is the name for the pattern: impairment that transfers across tasks, that follows acts of control more than acts of equal duration that do not require control, and that looks like fatigue of an executive rather than like the absence of a skill. Inference of that kind is legitimate, and it is only as strong as the pattern. The paper's confidence is the confidence of a pattern that had just appeared in several forms. The confidence of the field, later, outran the pattern.

Limits

The studies are small, the second task is often persistence at something unpleasant, and the participants are the usual students. An unsolvable puzzle is a convenient way to measure giving up. It is also a strange stand-in for the self-control of ordinary life, which is rarely a matter of how long one wrestles with a trick that has no solution. The paper shows a sequential effect in the laboratory tasks it chose. It does not show that a morning of resisting biscuits leaves a person unable to work in the afternoon, and it does not identify the resource with a known biological store. Those extensions came later, in a spirit of productive boldness, and they travelled further than the original contrasts could underwrite.

There is a limit of interpretation that was visible even before the replications. Giving up sooner on a dull puzzle can be a change in motivation, in boredom, or in what the person thinks the experimenter wants, rather than a change in a fuel. The authors argued against several of those readings. They could not, with the designs of 1998, separate a spent resource from a shifted priority. Later theories, including the belief account of Job, Dweck and Walton and the motivational account associated with Michael Inzlicht, occupy exactly that gap. They are alternatives. They are not what this paper concluded.

Replications and critiques

The programme that followed was very large, and then it was the textbook example of a programme the replication crisis unwound. Meta-analyses that took publication bias seriously found much smaller effects. A preregistered multi-laboratory replication in 2016, led by Martin Hagger and involving many laboratories and a very large sample, found an effect close to zero. A further multi-site preregistered test in 2021, designed to meet the objection that the first replication had used a weak manipulation, found at best a very small effect. Vadillo, Gold and Osman, looking at the ego-depletion data inside Many Labs 3, likewise failed to uncover the effect. The glucose story was challenged on separate grounds, including the awkward fact that the brain's use of glucose barely changes across the kinds of tasks supposed to drain it.

Baumeister and colleagues replied, to the 2016 replication in particular, that the manipulation had been too weak to constitute a fair test. That reply is a reason to read the 2021 study, which tried to make the test one that proponents would recognise as paradigmatic. It is not a reason to treat the 1998 experiments as having been secretly conclusive. Small studies with flexible analysis can produce a striking pattern that a registered protocol will not reproduce. The cookie result is a real event in the history of the field. Whether it is a real feature of the mind is what the later papers are for.

Why it matters here

Folio's card asks whether willpower is a tank that runs dry, and it answers, on the present evidence, that the tank is unproven. This paper is why the question was asked with such confidence for so long. The radishes, the cookies and the unsolvable puzzle are the image. The limited resource is the theory. The muscle metaphor is the promise that the limit would be both a short-run cost and a long-run training. Read the paper for that structure, and for the ambition of treating self-control as one quantity. Read Hagger, Vohs and Vadillo for the registered attempts to see the quantity again. Read Job, Dweck and Walton for the possibility that what runs dry is a belief about willpower rather than a fuel. The 1998 article is the claim. The rest of the card is the claim being checked.
