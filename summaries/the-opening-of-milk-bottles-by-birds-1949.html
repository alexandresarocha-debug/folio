In 1921, at Swaythling near Southampton, birds described as tits were seen prising open the waxboard tops of milk bottles on doorsteps and drinking the milk. By the late 1940s the habit was widespread in many parts of England and in parts of Wales, Scotland and Ireland, and at least eleven bird species had been recorded doing it. James Fisher and Robert Hinde set out, for the British Trust for Ornithology, to map how it had spread.

They gathered records in several ways. Questionnaires filled in by 126 members gave 141 records, ornithologists and other sources added more, and appeals in the press brought in over 200 further useful replies. In 223 cases observers could say the year the habit was first noticed. Blue tits and great tits dominated: of 400 identifications, 246 were blue tits, 142 great tits, 11 coal tits and 1 a marsh tit.

The birds were inventive. They hammered through metal foil caps and tore them off in strips, or peeled cardboard caps layer by layer; some carried the cap away and pecked the cream from its underside. Bottles were often attacked within minutes of delivery, parties of tits were reported following the milkman's cart down the street, and several blue tits were found drowned head first in bottles. In badly affected areas milkmen were told to put tins or jars over the bottles.

The pattern of spread was the key evidence. Tits rarely move more than about fifteen miles, yet by 1947 there were 89 records more than fifteen miles from any earlier one, so the habit must have been invented afresh many times. Around such starting points it spread locally, as in East Anglia, where records moved from Lowestoft to Norwich and then became widespread. The authors concluded that a few exceptional birds discovered the trick and that others then learned it 'by some sort of imitation', though 'we have no evidence as to the precise nature of the process.'

What this means in real life: the milk-bottle story became one of the most widely cited examples of innovation and culture in wild animals, and a reminder that animals adapt quickly to the opportunities people create.

Caution: the data were casual records from volunteers and newspaper readers, so first dates and gaps partly reflect who was looking. The survey could not show how the learning happened: birds might have copied a technique, or simply been drawn to opened bottles and then worked out the rest alone. Field experiments were needed to test this.
