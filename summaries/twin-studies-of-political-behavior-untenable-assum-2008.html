This is the best-known critique of the twin studies of politics. Jon Beckwith and Corey Morris wrote it as a direct reply to Alford, Funk and Hibbing's 2005 paper, which they note had drawn considerable media attention.

Their target is one assumption, the equal environments assumption. The twin method compares how alike identical twins are with how alike non-identical twins are, and credits the extra similarity to genes. That only works if identical twins do not also share more similar experiences that matter for the trait. If identical twins spend more time together, are treated more alike or influence each other more, some of what looks genetic could be environmental, and heritability would be overestimated.

Alford and colleagues had cited several studies as evidence that this assumption holds. Beckwith and Morris reviewed those studies and argued that they are weak, with significant methodological and design flaws. They went further: much of the evidence in them, they wrote, actually shows that trait-relevant equal environments assumptions were violated. They concluded that the assumption remains untenable and that twin studies are an insufficient method for drawing meaningful conclusions about complex human behaviour.

They are careful about what they do not claim. They do not say genes play no role in behaviour. They also stress what heritability is: a figure for one population, at one time, in one environment, which will almost certainly change if the environment changes.

What this means in real life: a figure like 0.40 for political attitudes is an estimate that rests on assumptions, not a measured fact like height. Its accuracy depends on how alike twins' lives really are.

The other side: twin researchers have tested the assumption directly. Studies summarised by Hatemi and colleagues in 2014 found that similarity in rearing, such as sharing a room or attending the same class, had almost no influence on the heritability of ideology. Twins followed from ages 9 to 18 showed no difference in attitude similarity by twin type during childhood. Non-identical twins grew less alike once they left home. Critics reply that such tests cannot capture every relevant difference, so the disagreement is still open.
