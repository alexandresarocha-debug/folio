After the 1985 experiment by Stasser and Titus, other researchers used the same design, known as the hidden profile: the right answer is only visible if group members put their separate pieces of information together. By 2011 enough studies existed to pool them. Li Lu, Y. Connie Yuan and Poppy Lauretta McLeod did that in a meta-analysis published in Personality and Social Psychology Review in 2012.

They gathered 65 studies, which gave 101 independent effects covering 3,189 groups. Their results were consistent with the original. First, groups mentioned common information, meaning what several members already held, a lot more than unique information held by only one member. The gap was about two standard deviations, which is large. Second, hidden profile groups were about eight times less likely to find the correct solution than groups in which everyone had all the information. In the 24 effect sizes that tested this, the odds ratio was 8.05.

The effect was not the same everywhere. It was worse in bigger groups, and with more information to process. On tasks where the right answer can be shown to be right once found, the cost of a hidden profile was smaller than on tasks that depend on judgement, where the odds ratio was 15.18.

Two ways of measuring information pooling both predicted better decisions. One was the share of the unique information that got mentioned at all, and the other was how much of the total discussion was about unique information. The first, called coverage, mattered more. In plain terms, getting each rare fact mentioned at all matters more than how long the group spends on it. The medium of communication, face to face or by computer, made no difference to how much unique information was pooled or to how good the decisions were.

What this means in real life: do not assume that putting smart people in a room means they will share what they know. Make sure each person's unique information is stated at least once. Plan for that in meetings, especially large ones.

A limit: most of these studies are laboratory tasks with a set right answer. Real decisions are messier.

Source. Lu, L., Yuan, Y. C., and McLeod, P. L. (2012). Twenty-five years of hidden profiles in group decision making: A meta-analysis. Personality and Social Psychology Review, 16(1), 54-75. https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868311417243
