Jonathan Haidt's 2012 book The Righteous Mind begins with a story his lab had used for years. Julie and Mark, adult siblings, have sex once while on vacation. She is on birth control, he uses a condom, they are unharmed, and they keep the night as a secret that leaves them closer. Listeners condemn it. They mention a pregnancy or a damaged relationship. When the story has already closed those doors, many say they simply know it is wrong. In the interview study, with 30 people, 20% called it okay at the start and 32% called it okay at the end. Haidt's image for the split is an elephant and a rider. The intuition moves. The reasons talk about where it went.

The book's map of what the intuition responds to has six foundations: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty. The measurements underneath the first five come from work with Jesse Graham and Brian Nosek. On a website questionnaire, liberals treated harm and fairness as the moral concerns that counted, and conservatives endorsed those and also loyalty, authority, and purity. Sermons showed the same split in public speech. In a corpus of 69 Unitarian sermons, words tied to harm were 0.44% of the text and words tied to purity were 0.25%. In 34 Southern Baptist sermons, harm words were 0.26% and purity words were 0.64%. Authority words were 0.46% of the Unitarian text and 0.98% of the Baptist text.

A fight at work about a rule, or a family argument about a holiday, often stalls because the two sides are using different items on that list. One person is asking who was hurt and whether the split was fair. The other is also asking what the act means for the group, for someone in authority, or for a boundary that should stay special. Seeing the second set of concerns describes the disagreement. It does not require anyone to adopt them.

Royzman, Kim, and Leeman later found that listeners often would not grant that Julie and Mark's night was really free of harm. With that disbelief included, their count of people who condemned the act and truly had no reason left was about zero.

Source. Haidt, J. (2012). The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion. New York: Pantheon. https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/73535/the-righteous-mind-by-jonathan-haidt/
