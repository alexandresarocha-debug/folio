A book in three claims

The Righteous Mind, published by Jonathan Haidt in 2012, is the long version of a theory he had been building since the social intuitionist paper of 2001. It is written for a general reader and aimed at a political culture that Haidt thinks can no longer hear itself. The book is organised as three claims, each occupying a part, and the claims are meant to stack. First, moral intuitions come first and strategic reasoning comes second. Second, there is more to morality than harm and fairness. Third, morality binds people into groups and, in binding them, blinds them to the moralities of other groups. A reader who accepts only the first claim has a psychology of judgement. A reader who accepts all three has Haidt's account of why political argument feels both obvious and hopeless, and of what a less hopeless argument would have to take seriously.

The tone is personal in a way the 2001 paper is not. Haidt describes a change in his own political sympathies, away from a confident liberalism that could see conservative morality only as a failure of compassion, and towards the view that conservatives and liberals rest on different but genuine moral foundations. That autobiographical frame is part of the rhetoric. It is also a risk. A theory of why one's opponents are not monsters can slide into a brief for the opponents. The book is fairest when it is read as an argument about the contents and the functions of moral intuition, and most slippery when the American party system of its decade is treated as a clean exhibit of those contents.

The rider and the elephant

The first part restates the social intuitionist model in the metaphor Haidt had used in The Happiness Hypothesis. Conscious reasoning is a rider sitting on an elephant. The elephant is the mass of automatic processes, including the moral emotions and the rapid evaluations that fire when we see a cruelty, a betrayal, a desecration or a kindness. The rider can nudge, rehearse, and occasionally persuade, and the rider is the one who speaks. Most of the time the rider's speaking explains and defends the direction the elephant is already going. The image is friendlier than the emotional dog of the 2001 title, and it admits more influence from the rider, but the causal order is the same. Intuition first, justification second.

Haidt uses the image to redescribe ordinary moral talk. When we argue about a political scandal or a family quarrel, we experience ourselves as weighing reasons and then deciding. On his account we usually decide, in the sense that an evaluation has already landed, and the weighing is the search for shareable words. This is why fresh facts so often fail to move anyone in a dispute, and why a vivid story sometimes does. Facts offered to the rider are irrelevant if the elephant's evaluation is what would have to change. A story works, when it works, because it gives the elephant a new experience, not because it completes a syllogism. Haidt's practical advice follows from the image. If you want to change a mind, you talk to the elephant. You cultivate liking, you find a foundation the other person already feels, and you stop imagining that a better paragraph will do the job the paragraph was never the cause of.

Foundations, not a single moral sense

The second part is moral foundations theory, developed with colleagues and set out here for a non-specialist. The claim is that human morality is built on several intuitive foundations, rather than on a single principle such as harm or justice. The foundations Haidt emphasises are care, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity, with liberty added as a further foundation that helps explain some libertarian and anti-authoritarian judgements. Each foundation is a preparedness to feel a particular family of moral emotions, shaped by evolution for a recurrent social problem and then edited by culture. Care responds to suffering and nurturance. Fairness responds to cheating and, in a later refinement, to proportionality. Loyalty responds to the demands of the group. Authority responds to rank, order and respect. Sanctity responds to degradation and to the sense that some things are not to be treated as commodities. Liberty responds to bullies and to domination.

Cultures and political temperaments, on this theory, differ mainly in which foundations they treat as part of morality proper. Haidt argues that people on the political left, especially in Western democracies, build their morality mostly on care and fairness, and often regard loyalty, authority and sanctity as prejudices rather than as moral concerns. People on the political right draw more evenly on the whole set. The argument is not that conservatives care more, in the ordinary sense of the verb. It is that conservative morality is broader, and that liberal morality is a narrowing that has a history, tied to what Haidt, borrowing a piece of jargon, treats as the outlook of societies that are Western, educated, industrialised, rich and democratic. The narrowing sees the binding foundations as threats to individuals. The broader set sees them as the conditions that make group life possible. Much of the book's political heat is in that redescription. Practices a liberal reader wants to call oppression, Haidt asks the reader to see, at least for a moment, as a sanctity or an authority intuition doing what it is for.

Binding and blinding

The third part gives the foundations a social function. Morality is not only a device for deciding how individuals should treat one another. It is the equipment with which humans form coalitions that can cooperate, compete and sacrifice. Shared intuitions bind a group. People who flinch at the same betrayals and the same desecrations can trust one another and can punish free riders. The same shared intuitions blind the group. Once a moral matrix is in place, the rival matrix looks not merely different but immoral, and evidence against one's own side is hard to see because seeing it would loosen the binding. Haidt thinks this is a feature of a groupish species, not an accidental defect of modern media. The righteous mind of the title is the mind that is sure it is standing up for morality when it is also standing up for its team.

The practical conclusion is epistemic humility of a particular kind. You are not going to reason your way out of a moral matrix you have not first noticed, and you will not notice it by introspecting, because introspection returns the rider's brief. You notice it by granting, at least as an anthropological exercise, that a foundation you do not feel might still be a moral foundation and not a mistake. Haidt asks liberal readers in particular to try this with loyalty, authority and sanctity, because those are the ones he thinks they have defined out of morality. Whether the exercise ends in agreement is, for him, less important than whether it ends in an accurate description of what the other side is responding to. A politics that can describe its opponents only as deficient in care will keep being surprised by them.

What the criticisms take away

The criticisms that Folio's card records are aimed mostly at the second claim, and they are serious. The foundations may not be as distinct as the theory needs them to be. Questionnaire items meant to tap loyalty, authority and sanctity can blur, and the factor structure of the measures has been disputed. If the foundations are not cleanly separable, then the elegant picture of liberals using two and conservatives using five is partly a picture of the questionnaire. The further claim that the foundations are innate modules, rather than recurrent themes in moral talk, is stronger than the survey evidence. Surveys show what people endorse. They do not show an evolutionary architecture.

Other critics, Gray chief among them, argue that perceived harm is doing the work that Haidt distributes across several foundations. A judgement that looks like sanctity or loyalty, on this rival view, is still a judgement about a victim and a perpetrator, seen through a cultural story about who can be hurt. And the dumbfounding evidence that opened the first part has looked less robust in later attempts to reproduce it. The Righteous Mind remains a powerful account of why moral argument feels post hoc and why political tribes talk past each other. It is a weaker account, taken as science, of how many distinct psychological systems morality contains. The binding-and-blinding chapter can be true even if the catalogue of foundations is revised. People do form teams around moralised values, and teams do make their members worse at seeing what the other team values. That claim does not require the catalogue to be final.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The elephant and the rider, treats this book as the place where the 2001 model becomes a public theory of politics. The rider and the elephant are the card's metaphor for intuition first and reasoning second. The foundations, care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty, are the card's account of what the intuitions are about. The survey finding that liberals emphasise care and fairness while conservatives draw more evenly on all of them is the empirical pattern the next paper, by Graham, Haidt and Nosek, is cited to support. The card's criticisms, about distinctness, innateness and measurement, are the reasons the book cannot be the last word on the pattern. Read The Righteous Mind for the stacked argument, from the order of judgement, through the plurality of foundations, to the groupish function of morality. Read the 2001 paper for the model stated without the political narrative, and the 2009 paper for the measurements the narrative relies on.
