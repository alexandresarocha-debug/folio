Background

By the middle of the 1980s, a long argument was stuck. Technical experts ranked hazards by expected fatalities, and often by the deaths one could expect per year across a population. Public rankings did not match. Nuclear power, in particular, sat near the top of public concern and far lower on the experts' fatality lists, while familiar hazards that killed steadily attracted less demand for action. One response was to call the public ignorant and to prescribe better numbers. Paul Slovic, summarising a decade of work with Baruch Fischhoff, Sarah Lichtenstein and others, thought the mismatch was more interesting than ignorance. His 1987 paper in Science, Perception of Risk, is the short map of that research programme, known as the psychometric approach.

Availability is part of the map, and it is why the paper sits on Folio's availability card. Dramatic, imaginable, heavily reported deaths are overestimated. Quiet ones are underestimated. Slovic's point is that availability does not exhaust the disagreement. People also care about qualities of a hazard that never appear in a death count.

The question

When lay people judge that something is risky, what are they judging? If the answer is expected fatalities, then disagreement with experts is mostly error, and the remedy is education. If the answer is a bundle of qualities, then two people can share the same fatality figures and still disagree, because they are answering different questions. Slovic asked which qualities travel together, whether those qualities form a stable picture across hazards, and where experts and lay people actually part company. He also asked what an accident means. On a purely statistical view, an accident updates a frequency. On the view he defends, some accidents are signals that the system producing them is not understood and not under control.

How the studies were set up

The psychometric studies asked groups of citizens, students, and members of groups such as the League of Women Voters, together with experts in risk assessment, to look at a long list of hazards. The list ran from mundane activities, such as swimming and skiing, through to technologies such as nuclear power, pesticides and commercial aviation. People rated each hazard on a set of qualities. Was the risk voluntary or imposed? Was it known to science, and known to the person exposed? Was it controllable? Did it kill at once or years later? Could a single event be catastrophic? Were the consequences equitable, or borne by people who did not take the benefits? How much dread did it evoke? They also gave an overall judgement of how risky the hazard was, and sometimes a judgement of how much regulation it required.

The analysis looked for the structure under those ratings. If many of the qualities rise and fall together, a long questionnaire may be reducible to a few dimensions, and hazards can be placed on a map. Separate comparisons asked experts and lay people to rank the same hazards, and set those rankings against the best available estimates of actual fatalities. A further strand looked at accidents as news. When a technology fails in an unfamiliar way, do people treat the event as one draw from a known distribution, or as evidence that the distribution itself is the wrong model?

What they found

The qualities were not a jumble. They collapsed, across studies of this kind, into two broad factors that Slovic made famous. One is dread. It gathers together lack of control, catastrophic potential, fatal consequences, inequitable distribution, and a felt horror at the hazard. The other is the unknown. It gathers together hazards that are new, unobservable, delayed in their harm, and poorly understood by science or by the exposed person. Nuclear power sits high on dread and towards the unknown side of the map. Familiar, voluntary activities, the ones people choose and feel they can stop, sit elsewhere, even when they cause a steady number of injuries. The map is the paper's central picture. A hazard's position on it predicted the public's desire for stricter regulation better than a simple story about death rates.

Lay rankings and expert rankings came apart in a systematic way. Experts' judgements tracked annual fatalities more closely. Lay judgements were pulled towards the dreaded and the unknown, and towards hazards that had received vivid attention. Availability shows up here as one contributor, not as the whole account. A risk that is easy to picture and easy to recall is judged larger, which is Tversky and Kahneman's point. Slovic added that the same risk is also judged differently, not just larger, when it is imposed, uncapped, and mysterious. People were not failing to give the experts' answer. They were giving an answer to a richer question, and then labelling that answer risk.

Accidents at dreaded, poorly understood technologies carried what Slovic called signal value. A single event said more than its body count, because it suggested that the institution in charge did not know what it was doing. An accident in a familiar, voluntary activity said less. It could be filed as misfortune inside a system people already understood. That difference helps explain why one crash, one leak or one contamination episode can reorder a public agenda even when the change in expected fatalities is small.

How Slovic read the results

The paper's moral is that there is no neutral definition of risk waiting behind the argument. Choosing to rank hazards by expected deaths is a choice. It leaves out consent, catastrophe, equity and the trustworthiness of the people running the technology. Experts are not free of framing either. Their numbers depend on which consequences are counted, over what period, and for whom. Slovic did not conclude that fatality estimates are unimportant. He concluded that telling the public it has failed a statistics test will not settle a conflict that is partly about values and partly about trust.

He also kept the cognitive piece. Lay estimates of how many people die from various causes do show the availability pattern: dramatic causes are overestimated relative to common, undramatic ones. Media coverage feeds the pattern. The psychometric map does not replace that finding. It sits beside it. Misjudged frequencies and differently weighted qualities can both be present in the same argument about a power plant, a pesticide or a vaccine.

Limits

The method asks people to rate named hazards on scales the investigators chose. What comes out depends on which hazards and which scales went in. Factor analysis will always return factors. The claim that two dimensions are the right summary of risk perception is a claim about this family of questionnaires, not a discovery that the mind contains exactly two risk senses. The samples, too, were the available citizens of a particular place and decade, heavy with American students and club members, and the hazard list is a hazard list of its time. A map drawn around nuclear power in the years after Three Mile Island and Chernobyl is an honest map of that anxiety. It is not a universal geography of fear.

Ratings of qualities are also not decisions. A person can call a technology dreadful on a questionnaire and still live beside it, vote for it, or work in it. The paper is about expressed perception and the desire for regulation, not about every behaviour that risk might influence. And the expert side of the comparison assumes a fatality table good enough to serve as a benchmark. For new or poorly measured hazards, that table is itself a judgement, with its own unknowns.

Replications and critiques

The two-factor picture has been recovered often enough, in enough countries and hazard sets, to be more than a one-sample accident. It has also been criticised as a method that builds its conclusion into its adjective list, and as a static snapshot that misses how perception changes when people have to trade risks against benefits, jobs and cost. Later work by Slovic himself moved towards risk as feeling, the idea that a quick affective impression, good or bad, guides the judgement before any tally of qualities. That later line does not retract the 1987 map. It offers a mechanism, a swift sense of goodness or badness, that may sit underneath the factors.

The comparison with experts has worn less simply. Experts disagree with one another once the hazard is genuinely uncertain, and public concern has sometimes pointed at harms that formal assessments were slow to count. A fair use of the paper is modest. Lay and expert rankings diverge for intelligible reasons, including availability and including values. Neither ranking is a substitute for the other. Policy that consults only the death table will look blind to the people who bear the risk. Policy that consults only dread will spend against the picture in the news and miss the quiet toll.

Why it matters here

The availability card says that vivid causes of death are overestimated beside common killers, and it names Slovic as the person who documented the misperception. This paper is that documentation, and it is also the warning not to stop there. Ease of recall explains why a spectacular hazard looms. It does not explain why two hazards with the same spectacular death can still be judged differently if one is chosen and the other is imposed, or if one is understood and the other is a signal that nobody is in charge. On the Folio path, read Tversky and Kahneman for the shortcut, Schwarz for the feeling of ease, Kuran and Sunstein for the cascade, and Slovic for the qualities that a frequency can never exhaust.
