In a survey Paul Slovic reprinted in Science in 1987, four groups ranked 30 activities and technologies by how much risk of death they posed for the country as a whole. The groups were 40 members of the League of Women Voters, 30 college students, 25 members of a business and professional club, and 15 people whose job was risk assessment. Rank 1 was the hazard they judged worst.

The League and the students both put nuclear power first. The business-club members put it eighth. The experts put it twentieth. The experts put motor vehicles first and smoking second. The League put motor vehicles second, handguns third, and smoking fourth. The students put handguns second and smoking third. Swimming, which the experts ranked 10th, was ranked 30th by the students, the safest thing on their list. Medical X-rays were 7th for the experts and 22nd for the League.

Slovic's point is that the public ranking is not a botched version of the experts' death counts. People were also reacting to how a hazard feels. Nuclear power was seen as new, hard to control, and capable of a catastrophe, even by people who knew the routine annual deaths were low. X-rays and swimming do kill, but the deaths come one at a time and feel voluntary, so they drop down the public list. He sorts those reactions along two rough lines: how dreaded and catastrophic a hazard seems, and how new or poorly understood it seems. Hazards high on both draw the demand for strict rules.

That split still decides arguments in a clinic, a town hearing, or a product recall. A patient can fear a rare, vivid complication more than a common one the doctor thinks of as the real danger. A community can fight a plant whose disaster would be awful and accept a road whose deaths are scattered through the year. Slovic's practical claim is that telling people the annual death toll does not answer the question they are asking, which is also about control, dread, and whether the next accident would be a catastrophe. Experts who skip that question talk past the people they need to persuade.
