In 1988 Richard Lenski started twelve populations of E. coli from the same ancestor, each in its own flask, in a liquid with a small amount of glucose. The experiment was designed to run for a very long time and to see what evolution does when it is allowed to repeat. Each day a sample was moved to fresh medium. Samples were frozen at intervals, which makes a kind of fossil record that can be brought back to life.

The liquid also contained citrate. E. coli can use citrate in some conditions without oxygen, but with oxygen it cannot normally use it as food. For more than 30,000 generations no population changed that, even though, as the paper says, each tested billions of mutations. Then one of the twelve, called Ara-3, changed. Around generation 33,000 that flask turned markedly cloudier, which meant the bacteria had found a new food. Going back to the frozen samples, the team found that citrate users were present by generation 31,500. They also checked that these were not contaminants: the new bacteria carried genetic markers unique to Ara-3.

The authors asked why it happened so late and only once. They set out two ideas. The first was that the needed mutation was simply extremely rare. The second was that it was an ordinary mutation, but one that only worked, or could only occur, in a population that had already changed in other ways. To tell the two apart they replayed evolution. They took the ancestor and clones frozen at different times from the Ara-3 line, and grew very large numbers of cells, watching for citrate users.

From the ancestor, among 8.4 trillion cells, there were none. From 60 clones sampled in the first 15,000 generations, among 9 trillion cells, there were none. But later clones evolved the ability significantly more often, which showed that a so-called potentiating change had arisen by 20,000 generations. It raised the chance of the later step, without making the bacteria mutate faster in general. So the outcome depended on the particular history of that one population.

What this means in real life: where you end up can depend on steps that looked unrelated at the time. In evolution, in careers and in organisations, a capability may only become reachable after earlier changes made for other reasons. The authors suggest that history matters most when it enables key innovations that are hard to reach by gradual steps.

Criticism: this is one population, and the interpretation has been questioned. A 2016 commentary by Roth, Maisnier-Patin and Silhavy asked whether the delay is better explained by intermittent selection than by contingency.
