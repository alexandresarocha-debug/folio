Why do groups often decide worse than their best-informed member could? In 1985 Garold Stasser and William Titus at Miami University tested one reason with a simple setup. Students read descriptions of three imaginary candidates for student body president, then met in groups of four to choose the best one. Each candidate had 16 pieces of information. Candidate A was the best on paper, with 8 positive items, 4 neutral and 4 negative. Candidates B and C each had only 4 positive, 8 neutral and 4 negative.

In one condition every member got the full profiles. In the other conditions the information was split. Each member saw only two of A's eight positive items, so that no one could see A's strengths alone, but the four together held everything. If the group simply talked and pooled what it knew, it should have reached the same answer as the first group.

The first group did. Before discussing, 67 percent of individuals chose A, and afterwards 83 percent of the 18 groups chose A. The split groups were different. In the main split condition only 20 percent of members favoured A before discussion, and 75 percent favoured B. After talking, only 24 percent of the 21 groups chose A, and 71 percent chose B. In a second split version, 12 percent of the 17 groups that reached a decision chose A. The authors wrote that discussion tended to keep, rather than correct, the members' lopsided pictures of the candidates. Groups mostly talked about what several members already held in common, and about information that matched what members already preferred.

What this means in real life: the information that one person holds alone is the information most likely to stay unsaid. This is a risk for hiring panels, hospital teams and boards. The most practical remedies are to ask each person to write down what they know before the meeting, and to ask, what do you know that the rest of us may not?

The setting was artificial: students, imaginary candidates and a short task. But the finding has been repeated many times since, and a meta-analysis in 2012 pulled together 65 studies. Stasser and Titus did not test trivial against important topics. Their finding is about the sharing of information, not about bikeshedding as such.
