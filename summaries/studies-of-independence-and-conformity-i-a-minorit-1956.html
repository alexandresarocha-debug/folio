Background

Solomon Asch's monograph Studies of independence and conformity appeared in 1956, after a shorter report that had already begun to make the line-judgement task famous. The task is the one Folio's card describes. A person sits with a group and says which of three comparison lines matches a reference line in length. The answer is obvious. The other people in the room are confederates, and on certain trials they all give the same wrong answer. The real participant answers near the end. The textbook memory of the result is that people cave in. Asch's own title puts independence first, and the monograph is written to keep it there. Conformity is real, limited, and strikingly sensitive to whether the majority is unanimous. The card's warning, that the result is often misremembered, is a warning Asch himself would have recognised.

The question

Asch asked how a person maintains, or surrenders, a plainly true judgement when a unanimous group contradicts it. He chose a perceptual task on purpose. If he had asked about politics or taste, a yield could have been a change of opinion in the ordinary sense, or politeness about a matter with no right answer. Lines have a right answer, and in the absence of the group people hardly ever miss it. A yield on a line trial is therefore a yield against the evidence of one's own eyes, which is the pressure he wanted to isolate. He was equally interested in the people who did not yield. A psychology that can explain only the conformers has not understood a situation in which, as he was determined to show, independence is common.

He also asked what the pressure consists of. Is the participant persuaded that his eyes are wrong, or does he say the group's word while privately seeing the line as it is? Those are different events, and they imply different remedies. Asch built the method so that he could count the public yields and then, in interviews, ask people what they had experienced while they yielded or resisted. The monograph is a quantitative paper with a long qualitative centre.

How the studies were set up

College students were told they were taking part in a study of visual judgement. Groups sat in a row and announced their choices aloud. Only one person was a real participant, placed so that he spoke near the end. On neutral trials the confederates gave the correct match, so that the group would look like a group of competent peers and the task would look like a task with obvious answers. On critical trials every confederate gave the same wrong line. The errors were not subtle. The comparison lines differed by enough that a person judging alone would find the mistake startling. A control set of people judged the same lines with no group present, and their errors were rare. Anything the experimental participants did wrong, beyond that rare baseline, could be laid at the majority's door.

The variations are the part a one-number summary loses. Asch changed the size of the majority, from a single opponent up through a substantial group, to see when the pressure arrives and when it saturates. He gave some participants a partner who told the truth, breaking the unanimity in the direction of the evidence. He gave others a partner who was also wrong, but wrong in a different way, so that unanimity was broken without anyone actually supporting the participant's perception. He had some people write their answers privately after hearing the group, separating the knowledge of the majority's view from the requirement to declare oneself in public. He arranged, in further conditions, for the dissenter to appear late or to leave. Each variation holds the obviousness of the lines constant and changes the social structure.

What he found

Across the critical trials in the basic condition, participants gave the majority's wrong answer about a third of the time. Around three quarters of them conformed at least once. About a quarter never conformed at all. The arithmetic is the card's arithmetic, and it is easy to tell in either direction. A person who remembers only the three quarters will say that almost everyone folded. A person who remembers only the third of trials will understate how many people were moved at least once. Asch wanted both numbers, and he wanted a third fact that neither number is: most responses, even in the face of the unanimous group, were correct. The typical participant was not a conformist who occasionally slipped. The typical pattern was independence punctuated, for many people, by yields under strain.

The variations locate the strain. Conformity rose with the size of the majority up to about three or four people and then levelled off. A lone opponent produced little of the effect. A unanimous trio produced most of it. Adding further voices did not keep adding pressure in the same way. A single ally who gave the right answer dramatically reduced conformity. People who had yielded in the unanimous condition became, with a partner, capable of saying what they saw. Even an ally who was wrong in a different direction, and who therefore offered no confirmation of the truth, reduced the power of the majority. What the ally supplied was the break in unanimity. Private answers reduced conformity as well. Once the participant did not have to contradict the group out loud, more of his judgements came out correct.

How Asch read the interviews

After the trials he asked people what they had been doing. The interviews are the reason he refused a single mechanism. A few participants seemed, at moments, genuinely unsure of what they saw, and spoke as if the group had shaken their perception. More common were people who decided that the group was likely to be right and that their own eyes must be missing something, a change of judgement under informational doubt. Most important for his argument were the people who never lost the line and who gave the wrong answer anyway, because they did not want to look absurd, obstructive or poorly equipped for a simple task. He treated these as different events that a single error-count had piled together. Public conformity can be independence that has chosen, for the length of a sentence, not to show itself.

He was insistent that the independent participants were not a dull remainder. Many of them were troubled. They hesitated, wondered what was wrong with the group or with themselves, and spoke up regardless. Asch writes about them with unusual respect for an experimental paper. Independence, in his account, is an achievement maintained under a felt pressure, not the absence of social sensitivity. That is why a partner helps so much. The partner does not supply courage from nowhere. He makes it possible for a person who already sees the line to say so without accepting the role of sole dissenter. The practical lesson the card draws, that one ally can make it far easier to say what you see, is Asch's own lesson, stated as a result rather than as a moral.

Limits

The participants were students of one period, the groups were strangers assembled for an hour, and the task was a line. Asch chose the line so that the right answer would be beyond argument, which means he cannot tell us, from these data, how the same structures work when the right answer is genuinely unclear, or when the group is a person's real colleagues, or when the cost of dissent is a career rather than a blush. Later work, including Deutsch and Gerard on normative and informational influence and Bond and Smith's meta-analysis of the line task across cultures, takes up those questions. The monograph also depends on confederates who do not smirk, flinch or vary. A majority that looked unsure of its own wrong answer would not be the stimulus he studied.

There is a limit, too, in how the result was inherited. Asch's care about the quarter who never yielded, and about the majority of responses that stayed correct, was regularly dropped in the retelling. A 1990 paper by Ronald Friend, Yvonne Rafferty and Dana Bramel, which the card's deep dive cites, argued that textbooks had systematically exaggerated the conformity. The monograph itself is the correction. Anyone who has only met the study as a parable of sheep should read the tables as Asch arranged them: a real pressure, a minority of yields among the responses, a majority of people touched at least once, and a sturdy set of people whom the unanimous group never moved.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Asch's lines: the pressure to agree, is a reading of this monograph rather than of the parable. The numbers to keep are the ones he measured. Wrong answers on about a third of the critical trials. Around three quarters conforming at least once. About a quarter never conforming. Most answers independent. The structural facts are just as important. The pressure saturates at a small majority. A single supporting voice breaks it. A break in unanimity helps even when the breaker is not right. Writing the answer privately helps. Those are levers, not just findings, and they are why the card's practical lesson is about allies rather than about a vague warning that groups are dangerous.

The card points onwards to two companions that this paper makes necessary. Deutsch and Gerard, in 1955, put names on the split Asch heard in the interviews: informational influence, in which other people are used as evidence, and normative influence, in which other people are a source of approval and embarrassment. Bond and Smith, in 1996, asked whether the line effect is a human constant and found that it has been stronger in more collectivist settings and weaker in later American studies than in Asch's own decade. Neither companion replaces the monograph. They keep its method and refuse its myth. The thing Asch most wanted understood, that independence in the face of a unanimous error is ordinary and fragile and greatly strengthened by one ally, is the thing the card is for.
