Solomon Asch’s 1956 monograph reports what happens when one person has to say, out loud, which of three lines matches a standard line, after everyone else in the room has given the same wrong answer. The critical subjects were 123 male college students, ages seventeen to twenty-five, average age twenty, drawn from three colleges. The others, seven to nine of them, had been told to answer wrong on twelve of the eighteen trials. The misses were obvious. The majority’s line was off by three-quarters of an inch to an inch and three-quarters. Judging the same lines alone, a control group of thirty-seven students averaged 0.08 errors, under 1 percent. In the group, the majority pulled about one-third of the announced answers over to the wrong line. The mean number of errors, out of twelve critical trials, ran from 4.0 to 5.2 across the three colleges. Ninety-four of the 123 men went with the majority at least once. Twenty-four percent never did.

Most of the answers were still correct. Asch’s own summary is that the pressure was large and that it was not the strongest force in the room. The men who yielded were not calm about it. In the interviews afterward, some said they had wanted to conform on a particular trial and then felt disgusted with themselves. Others insisted they had simply said what they saw, sliding past the trials on which they had not. When Asch changed the procedure so that the real participant wrote his answer while the majority still spoke aloud, the extreme misses lost their pull. The errors that remained, ten of them, were all the smaller kind. On the trials where the majority picked the line farthest from the truth, nobody wrote that line down.

The everyday version is a meeting in which you speak near the end and the answer looks obvious to you and wrong in everyone else’s mouth. The chance of going along is real, on the order of one critical remark in three in this study, and it is higher when you have to say the thing in front of the people you are contradicting. Writing the judgment down before you speak, or hearing it read back later against a ruler, changes the cost. So does one other person who will not go along, which is why a lone dissenter in a hiring meeting or a ward round is doing more than stating a preference.

The rate did not travel intact. In a 1981 British replication, S. Perrin and C. Spencer found compliance on only one of 396 critical trials among student subjects. When the cost of disagreeing was high, youths on probation facing a majority of probation officers, compliance rose back to levels like Asch’s. The line effect depends on who is in the room and what dissent will cost them.
