A peace that was real

Margaret MacMillan’s The War That Ended Peace, published in 2013, is a history of the years before 1914 written against two simplifications. One simplification says the First World War was inevitable, the product of alliances, arms races, and empires that had been marching towards a cliff since the 1890s. The other says it was an accident, a sequence of blunders in July that a slightly wiser telegram could have stopped, with nothing much wrong underneath. MacMillan’s title is the alternative. There was a peace, the peace of the late nineteenth and very early twentieth century, and it was a substantial achievement: a global economy, a common elite culture, falling great-power violence in Europe itself, international conferences, and a widespread belief among the comfortable classes that war between the advanced states had become an anachronism. The war ended that peace. It did not merely interrupt a slide that was already finished. Her book asks how a peace that real was dismantled by people who, in many cases, believed they were preserving it.

She begins, characteristically, with a scene of confidence, the great exhibition in Paris in 1900, a world’s fair of technology, empire, and the conviction that the new century would be managed. The device is not nostalgia. It is a way of making the subsequent choices look like choices. The men she will follow through crises in Morocco, in Bosnia, and in the Balkans were not inhabitants of a tragic machine. They were inhabitants of a successful order, and they took risks with it because they had come to think that the risks were the way one defended one’s place in it. The book is long because the order was complicated, and because she refuses to let any one structural cause, nationalism, imperialism, the alliance system, the naval race, stand in for the decisions.

Forces, not fate

The thematic chapters take the forces one at a time and decline to promote any of them to a sufficient cause. Nationalism had intensified, particularly in the Balkans and in the internal politics of Austria-Hungary, and it had made compromise look like betrayal to publics that governments were less able to ignore. Imperial competition had produced real crises, above all in Morocco, where France and Germany tested each other’s nerves and Britain’s alignment. The naval race between Britain and Germany had created a new reason for suspicion and a new constituency, in both countries, for treating the other as the defining enemy. The alliance system, Franco-Russian on one side, Austro-German on the other, with Britain tied to France and Russia by ententes rather than by a straightforward alliance, had divided Europe into camps without quite making the camps automatic. MacMillan’s point about the alliances is that they were built as insurance and functioned, past a certain point, as a trap. A government stayed loyal to a dangerous ally because the loss of the ally looked worse than the risk of the ally’s quarrel. Deterrence, which was supposed to make war unthinkable, made retreat unthinkable first.

She is equally interested in the ideas that made these arrangements feel honorable. A code of masculine and national honour, shared across the officer corps and the ministries, treated backing down as a kind of social death. A strain of fatalism, more common as the crises accumulated, suggested that war would come anyway and that the strategic task was to make sure it came at a favourable moment. A counter-strain of internationalism, Hague conferences, arbitration treaties, socialist warnings, liberal faith in trade, was real and, in her account, insufficient rather than fraudulent. The peace movements did not fail because nobody sincerely wanted peace. They failed because they had less purchase on the ministries, at the moment of decision, than fear and honour did. MacMillan writes intellectual history here without turning the book into a history of ideas. The ideas appear as the language in which practical men justified practical steps.

The crises that were survived, and the one that was not

A large part of the book is a history of the crises that did not become the world war, which is her way of proving that nothing in the structure made 1914 unavoidable. The first Morocco crisis, the Bosnian crisis of 1908, the second Morocco crisis, the Balkan wars: each of them was a moment at which a great power considered whether a local humiliation or a local opportunity was worth a larger war, and each of them was contained. Containment taught two lessons, and the lessons contradicted each other. It taught that international conferences and last-minute restraint could work. It also taught some of the participants that the other side would always, in the end, give way, and that one’s own side had perhaps been too quick to compromise. Austria-Hungary drew, from Bosnia and from the Balkan wars, something close to the opposite of caution: the conviction that the next crisis had to be resolved by a success, because the pattern of retreat was becoming the policy. Germany’s leaders drew a related conclusion about the balance of power and about the reliability of their ally. The survived crises, in this reading, were not dress rehearsals for an inevitable premiere. They were successes that were misremembered as defeats.

When she reaches 1914, the narrative converges with the story Christopher Clark tells in much greater July detail, and the emphasis differs. MacMillan grants the complexity, the divided cabinets, the Serbian background, the mobilisation timetables. She is more willing than Clark to say that Austria-Hungary and Germany bear the heavier share of responsibility for converting a Balkan crime into a European war. Austria sought a war with Serbia. Germany encouraged Austria in terms that were reckless about the Russian response and then discovered a Russian response it had half expected. The other powers made the enlargement possible: Russia by mobilising, France by the tightness of its alliance, Britain by a policy that had neither deterred Germany clearly nor kept Britain clearly disengaged. Shared responsibility, in her handling, is compatible with unequal responsibility. The phrase “sleepwalkers” would strike her as slightly too kind to men who, in Vienna and Berlin, were not walking in their sleep when they chose to force the issue. They were taking a gamble they had discussed, and the gamble failed.

People, and the refusal of inevitability

Throughout, MacMillan insists on persons. Grey, Bethmann Hollweg, Berchtold, Poincaré, Sazonov, Wilhelm II, Franz Joseph: she gives them enough biographical room that their decisions look like the decisions of particular men with particular fears, rather than like outputs of a system. This is a methodological choice and a moral one. If the war was the product of impersonal forces alone, then the forces were to blame and the historian’s job is to name them. If, as she thinks, those forces created pressures within which different decisions were available, then the men who held office in 1914 are not excused by the pressures. They are judged by the use they made of a discretion they really had. She does not pretend the discretion was wide. A Habsburg minister in 1914 could not choose to be a Scandinavian neutral. He could choose whether to send an ultimatum designed to be rejected. A German chancellor could choose whether to restrain him. The book’s moral energy sits in that remaining gap between pressure and decision, which is exactly the gap that inevitability arguments close too early.

The comparison she invites, without writing a book about the present, is with any international order that has become too successful for its participants to imagine it ending. The pre-war peace was defended by people who could not quite believe they were the ones ending it, because ending it felt, from inside each capital, like someone else’s choice. That psychological fact is the overlap with Clark, and MacMillan would not deny the overlap. She would add that some of them were more willing than others to be the someone else. A history that cannot say so, for fear of sounding like the war-guilt clause of 1919, has overcorrected. A history that can say only that has not understood the Balkans, the alliances, or the decade of survived crises. Her book is the attempt to hold both.

The kind of history it is

The War That Ended Peace is not a monograph from a newly opened archive. It is a synthesis, written by a historian whose authority is in the composition of a large narrative that other historians can recognise as fair. Its judgements on particular telegrams are judgements a specialist may revise. Its architecture is the thing to keep: peace as a real and perishable achievement, forces as pressures rather than as fate, crises survived as evidence against inevitability, and 1914 as a choice in which Vienna and Berlin were the prime movers and the other capitals were not spectators. Readers coming from Tuchman will find almost none of the Marne and almost all of the world that made the Marne possible. Readers coming from Clark will find a more traditional willingness to rank responsibility, and less patience with the idea that clarity about rank is a return to propaganda.

Why it matters here

On Folio’s card about how Europe stumbled into war, MacMillan is the long view that keeps Clark and Tuchman in proportion. The card describes Clark’s shift from why to how, and his redistribution of attention across all the capitals. It then points to Tuchman for the first month of fighting and to MacMillan for a balanced account of the road to war. Balanced, in her case, does not mean undecided. It means that she has weighed the naval race, the alliances, the Balkans, and the July decisions in one scale, and has still come out willing to say that the governments which pushed a local crisis into a general war carry the greater weight. Use her book when the card’s word “stumbled” starts to sound like “could not have done otherwise”.
