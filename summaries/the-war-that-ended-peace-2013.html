On 1 July 1911 the German gunboat Panther anchored off Agadir, on the Atlantic coast of Morocco. Germany wanted compensation for the French advance toward a protectorate. On 21 July David Lloyd George, at the Mansion House in London, warned that if Britain were treated as of no account where her interests were vitally affected, peace at that price would be a humiliation a great country could not endure. The crisis ended without war. An agreement that November left France a free hand in Morocco and gave Germany territory from the French Congo. The protectorate itself was formalized the next year, at Fez, on 30 March 1912. Margaret MacMillan's The War That Ended Peace (2013) is built from episodes of this kind. The same powers that went to war in 1914 had already been to the edge, and had stepped back, in the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911, in the Bosnian crisis of 1908, and through the Balkan Wars of 1912 and 1913.

The arms race and the peace conferences ran in the same years, which is her evidence that neither made the ending inevitable. HMS Dreadnought, launched at Portsmouth on 10 February 1906, made the existing battle fleets look old at a stroke, and Germany answered with ships of its own. The same governments met at The Hague, from May to July 1899 and again from June to October 1907, twenty-six states at the first conference and forty-four at the second, to write rules for war and to talk about limiting arms. Norman Angell's The Great Illusion (1910) told a large public that a general war would wreck the trade it was supposed to protect. MacMillan's claim is that this peace was real. Alliances, dreadnoughts, empires, and nationalist presses had been lived with for years. Nothing in that list forced July 1914 to be the crisis that was not walked back. People who had compromised before decided, this time, not to.

The use of that claim, when someone is deciding why the war happened, is to refuse a list of "causes" that were already present in 1911. A cause that was there when the powers settled Agadir cannot, by itself, explain why they did not settle Sarajevo. The useful comparison is between a crisis that ended in a colonial bargain and a crisis that ended in the Austro-Hungarian declaration of war on Serbia on 28 July 1914. What differed was the willingness, in Vienna and then in the other capitals, to treat this particular gamble as one that could still be won, or that could no longer be refused.

The paper of that July is the limit on any story in which they were sure to pull back. Germany's assurance to Austria on 5 and 6 July, the blank cheque, and the Austro-Hungarian ministerial council of 7 July, which chose a note written so that Serbia could not accept it whole, are decisions. MacMillan does not deny them. Her argument is that similar decisions had been reversed before, so hindsight is what makes 1914 look like the only possible ending. Fritz Fischer's reading of the German archives, in Griff nach der Weltmacht (1961), remains the contrary weight: Berlin, on that evidence, was not a capital that stumbled into a war it had been desperate to avoid.
