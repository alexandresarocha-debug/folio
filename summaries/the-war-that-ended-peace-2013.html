On 14 April 1900, President Émile Loubet opened the Paris Universal Exposition with a speech about justice and human kindness. The grounds were unfinished, the press disliked the giant statue of a woman in an evening dress modeled on Sarah Bernhardt, and the fair went on to draw over 50 million visitors. Bosnia had its own small pavilion, set between the Austrian and Hungarian buildings, though the province was still formally Ottoman. Germany's pavilion carried a line about pulling up the anchor and throwing oneself into the conquest of the waves, rumored to be Kaiser Wilhelm II's own. In the Palace of Electricity a German crane could lift 25,000 kilos. Margaret MacMillan starts here because the peace these governments were about to risk was a going concern: a world fair, a shared elite culture, and a widespread belief among comfortable people that a war among the advanced states had become an anachronism.

The crises that followed were survived, which is her evidence that nothing in the structure made 1914 compulsory. On 6 October 1908 Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina, the province it had occupied since 1878 and the one with the little pavilion in Paris. Russia protested, then accepted a bargain in 1909, and there was no European war. In July 1911 the German gunboat Panther anchored off Agadir, and that crisis was bargained down too. The Balkan wars of 1912 and 1913 redrew the map and were kept from becoming a war of the great powers. Each success also taught some of the participants that the other side would climb down. Austria-Hungary drew the harder lesson that the next crisis had to be won outright.

In July 1914 that choice was available and was taken. Austria-Hungary decided to answer the murders in Sarajevo with a war on Serbia. Germany encouraged the decision in terms that were reckless about Russia. Russia mobilized. France held to its ally. Britain had an entente that neither deterred Germany clearly nor kept Britain clearly out. MacMillan grants the divided cabinets and the mobilization schedules. She still ranks the weight. The governments that pushed a local punishment into a continental war carry more of it than the governments that then had to answer.

The contrary account is Fritz Fischer's, from 1961, in which German war aims are the sufficient cause, and the contrary popular account is that a single telegram could have stopped an accident. Her book is the long record that makes both of those too simple: a peace that was real, a decade of crises that ended short of war, and a July in which Vienna and Berlin chose to force the issue.
