Margaret MacMillan begins The War That Ended Peace (2013) at the Paris Exposition, which ran from 14 April to 12 November 1900. It was a fair of confidence. Countries built palaces to their industry, visitors moved between them on new electric lines, and the Berlin chamber of commerce wrote to its Paris counterpart that the exhibition was destined to bring the civilized nations closer together in shared work. Europe had not had a general war since 1815. Trade, telegraph cables, and international exhibitions were easy to read as proof that the peace was now the nature of things.

Six years later a ship launched at Portsmouth made that reading harder. The keel of HMS Dreadnought was laid on 2 October 1905. King Edward VII launched her on 10 February 1906. She was commissioned on 2 December. She carried ten 12-inch guns and no middle-sized battery, and she was the first battleship driven by steam turbines. Fisher had forced the pace so that Britain would be the first navy to have the new type. The side effect was immediate. Every battleship afloat, including Britain's, was suddenly of the previous kind. Counting ships no longer favored the country that had been building longest. Germany, which under Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz had been constructing a fleet since the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900, could start the new count closer to equal. The race that followed was a race in dreadnoughts. A weapon meant to preserve a lead had wiped the ledger and opened a fresh one, in public, where newspapers could turn each keel into a threat.

MacMillan sets the peace movement down beside the fleet, not as a footnote. The Hague conferences of 1899 and 1907 tried arbitration and rules of war and did not stop the building. Norman Angell's The Great Illusion, published in 1910, argued that conquest could no longer capture another country's trade or wealth, because modern prosperity sat in credit, skill, and markets rather than in land a soldier could seize. The book was widely read. It described a real fact about industrial economies and mistook that fact for a brake. Governments could believe war would be ruinous and still decide that backing down would be worse. MacMillan's portraits, Wilhelm II, Nicholas II, Edward Grey, Franz Joseph, and the soldiers who served them, are there to show those decisions as personal and reversible. The long peace, in her account, was something people stopped maintaining. It was not a machine that had to explode.

The fair and the battleship belong to the same decade, and that is the detail to keep when 1914 is described as a surprise that burst out of a peaceful world. Europe had the exhibitions, the trade, and the peace societies. It also had a new class of ship that reset an arms race at one launch, and politicians who treated prestige and alliance as reasons to accept a risk they had been told was terrible. The war was the end of a peace that had to be chosen again each time a crisis arrived.
