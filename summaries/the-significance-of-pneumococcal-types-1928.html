Frederick Griffith was a medical officer of the British Ministry of Health, working in its pathological laboratory on the bacteria that cause lobar pneumonia. Pneumococci come in distinct types, recognised by the sugary capsule around each cell. Encapsulated forms grow as smooth (S) colonies and are virulent; forms that have lost the capsule grow as rough (R) colonies and are largely harmless.

His key experiments mixed the living and the dead. Mice were injected under the skin with a small dose of a living, attenuated R strain derived from Type II, together with a large dose of a virulent Type I culture that had been killed by heating to 60°C. Neither dose on its own should have caused a fatal infection. Yet in many experiments the mice died, and from their blood Griffith recovered virulent, encapsulated pneumococci of the type of the heated culture, not of the living strain. In one typical run, four mice died within three to five days, with capsulated diplococci in the blood.

He mapped the conditions with care. Heating the Type I culture to 80°C destroyed its power to bring about the change, while a Type II culture steamed at 100°C still worked. Simply incubating the two cultures together in the test tube did not produce the change; it needed the living animal.

Griffith was cautious about what had happened. He suggested that the dead bacteria might supply some specific protein that served as raw material, or 'pabulum', letting the R form rebuild the capsule of the dead type. He did not identify that substance, and he did not frame the result as a discovery about genes.

What this means in real life: transformation, the uptake of genetic material from outside the cell, turned out to be one way bacteria can swap traits, and it became a basic tool of molecular biology.

Caution: Griffith's work was done in mice with crude, heat-killed cultures, and not every experiment produced the change. The identity of the active substance stayed unknown for another sixteen years, until Avery, MacLeod and McCarty traced it to DNA.
