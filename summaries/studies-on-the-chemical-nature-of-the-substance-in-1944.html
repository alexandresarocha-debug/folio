Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty, working at the hospital of the Rockefeller Institute in New York, set out to purify the substance behind Griffith's transformation and find out what it was made of. Their working model was the change of a non-encapsulated R variant of Type II pneumococcus into fully encapsulated Type III, a reaction that earlier workers in the same laboratory had learned to produce in the test tube rather than in mice.

From heat-killed Type III bacteria they extracted a fraction that kept its transforming power through repeated purification. Chemical analysis of four purified preparations gave nitrogen-to-phosphorus ratios between 1.58 and 1.75, close to the 1.67 expected for DNA. The purified material showed little or no serological activity, meaning the capsular sugar had been almost completely removed. It was highly potent: in one test, 0.003 micrograms of the active fraction still induced transformation, a final concentration of about one part in 600 million.

The enzyme tests pointed the same way. Crystalline trypsin and chymotrypsin, which digest proteins, and crystalline ribonuclease, which digests RNA, caused no loss of activity. Only enzyme preparations that could break down known samples of DNA destroyed the transforming principle. The change was permanent: the transformed cells went on making the Type III capsule in successive generations, and more of the transforming substance could be recovered from them than had been added in the first place.

The authors concluded that the evidence supports the belief that a nucleic acid of the DNA type is the fundamental unit of the transforming principle. In his 1985 commentary on the paper, McCarty recalled that the team was convinced by spring 1942, and spent another year strengthening the evidence because nucleic acids were widely thought to be all much alike.

What this means in real life: this was the first experimental evidence that DNA carries hereditary information, the foundation for genetics, biotechnology and DNA-based medicine.

Caution: the authors themselves wrote that the purity was established only within the limits of their methods, and critics argued that a trace of protein might be responsible. McCarty later noted that citations lagged in the early years, and that follow-up work with a purified DNA-destroying enzyme, which made protein contamination unlikely, was not widely cited at first.
