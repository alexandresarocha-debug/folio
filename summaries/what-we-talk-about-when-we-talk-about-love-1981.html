Seventeen stories, cut close to the bone

What We Talk About When We Talk About Love, published by Knopf in 1981, is Raymond Carver's second major collection and the book that fixed his name to a certain kind of American short story. There are seventeen stories. They are short. The people in them work, drink, split up, wait for news, and fail to say the thing that would make the next hour easier. The sentences are stripped. A reader meets the manner at once, in Why Don't You Dance?, where a man has put his furniture out on the lawn in the arrangement of a bedroom and a kitchen, and a young couple stops to buy. He offers them drinks. He puts on a record. He asks them to dance. The house is gone, or the marriage is, or both, and the yard sale is the only sentence the man seems able to utter.

The collection keeps returning to that kind of utterance. Viewfinder gives a man with hooks for hands, taking pictures of a house the narrator no longer lives in properly. Gazebo sits with Duane and Holly in a motel they manage, the affair already confessed, the future they had imagined for the place already lost. I Could See the Smallest Things and Sacks turn on insomnia, a parent's confession, the small hours when a fact arrives and nobody knows what to do with it. The Calm watches a fishing story get told in a barber's chair until the telling itself starts a quarrel. One More Thing ends on a man leaving, still talking, the last speech inadequate to the door. Together the stories make a book about domestic life in which the decisive event has often happened just before the first line, or is about to happen just after the last.

The title story, and what the talk cannot settle

The title story is the one people mean when they quote the book. Two couples, Mel and Terri, Nick and Laura, sit in a kitchen with a bottle of gin and talk about love. Nick narrates. Mel is a cardiologist, which the story lets you taste as a joke and as a claim: he works on hearts and cannot define the word they are all using. Terri tells of her old lover Ed, who beat her and then killed himself. She calls it love. Mel is appalled, and then he is drunk, and then he tells his own story, an elderly couple smashed up in a car accident, their bodies in casts, miserable because they cannot turn their heads to look at each other. The light in the kitchen fails. Someone goes for more drinks. The conversation does not arrive at a definition. Laura and Nick, newer to each other, are left inside a mood they did not invent and cannot politely exit.

Everything Stuck to Him, earlier in the book, is the memory piece that makes the title story hurt. A father tells his daughter about being young and married, a baby in the next room, a quarrel, a reconciliation, the feeling that nothing could come loose. The reader who has already moved through the other stories knows how much can come loose. Popular Mechanics, only a few pages, is the cruel opposite of that memory. A quarrel over leaving becomes a quarrel over the baby, and the baby is pulled between the two adults. The prose stays flat. The flatness is the shock. Carver does not step in to moralise the image. The image is the moral, and it is also a risk, because a story this short asks the reader to supply the scream the sentences withhold.

Violence at the edge of the weekend

Several stories push past marital silence into harm. Tell the Women We're Going follows two men through a beery afternoon that ends in an attack. So Much Water So Close to Home sends Stuart and his friends on a fishing trip where they find a dead girl in the river and continue the trip before reporting her. Claire, his wife, hears the story and cannot put it down. The title is the husband's excuse and the wife's horror: there was so much water, they were already there, the authorities were far. Claire's response takes up the rest of the story, including a funeral and a sense that her own life has been dirtied by what the men were willing to postpone. The Bath approaches dread from the other side. A boy is hit by a car on his birthday. His parents wait in the hospital. The baker who made the cake keeps calling because nobody has picked it up. The story ends in that waiting.

A Serious Talk and After the Denim catch the aftermath of marriages that have already failed. A man comes back to his old house at Christmas and cannot stop himself from small destructions. An older couple at bingo night brushes against someone else's bad news and against their own illness. The Third Thing That Killed My Father Off remembers a man and a peacock and a slow humiliation. Mr. Coffee and Mr. Fixit is a compressed farce of infidelity, the lover's mother, the junk in the garage. Read in order, the book alternates the very short shock with stories that allow a narrator a little more memory. The alternation is part of the design. Minimalism here is a repertoire, a set of distances from pain, some of them almost cruel in their brevity and some of them tender for a page or two.

The editor, and the manuscripts that came back

The spareness of the 1981 book is also an editorial fact. Gordon Lish, Carver's editor, cut the stories heavily. Years later the manuscript versions were published as Beginners, and readers could see another Carver beside the one the public had learned to call Carver. Titles had changed. Where Is Everyone? had become Mr. Coffee and Mr. Fixit. A Small, Good Thing had become The Bath. If It Please You had become After the Denim. Mine had become Popular Mechanics. Distance had become Everything Stuck to Him. Beginners had become the title story. The manuscript of the hospital story continues past the waiting: the parents go to the baker, and the baker feeds them, and the feeding is a form of mercy. The 1981 Bath stops before that mercy. Both versions are Carver's work at different moments of pressure. The published book is the one Lish's cuts made public, and it is the one that taught a generation of writers to hear omission as a style.

Carver's later collection Cathedral moves away from that extreme. The stories get longer, the speakers more willing to stay in the room, the endings more often touched by a change the characters can feel. Knowing this later turn helps a reader of the 1981 book avoid a false lesson. The lesson is that silence can carry dread, shame, and love when the writer knows the scene he has refused to explain. The false lesson is that explanation is always a failure of nerve. The Bath is powerful. A Small, Good Thing is powerful in another key, and it restores motives and kindness the cut had removed. Hemingway's condition, that omission must rest on knowledge, applies to editors as well as to writers. A cut made because the remaining words can hold the weight is one kind of act. A cut made to enforce a manner is another.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the iceberg names this collection as the place where Hemingway's principle meets a later minimalism, and where the subsequent publication of Beginners lets a reader see how much had been taken out. The stories themselves are the evidence. Why Don't You Dance? never delivers the speech that would explain the lawn. The title story never defines love. So Much Water So Close to Home lets a moral horror arrive as a decision about a weekend. The Bath withholds the bakery. Reading the collection beside the manuscript versions is the most direct way to feel the cost and the force of leaving things out. The card's warning sits in that comparison. Understatement works when it rests on knowledge. When the knowledge has been edited out of reach, the iceberg can tip into a hole.
