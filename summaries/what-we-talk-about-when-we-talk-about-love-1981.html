In the title story of Raymond Carver's 1981 collection, two couples sit in a kitchen drinking gin and try to decide what love is. Mel McGinnis, a cardiologist, and his wife Terri argue it out with Nick and Laura. Terri says her former husband Ed loved her. Ed beat her and dragged her around by her ankles, and he later killed himself. Mel will not call that love. His story is an old couple pulled from a wreck and sealed in casts. What broke the husband was that he could not turn his head to see his wife. The gin runs out, the kitchen goes dark, and the word they came to define is still sitting there, unsettleable.

Another story in the book, "Why Don't You Dance?," leaves a whole marriage off the page. A man sets his bedroom furniture on the front lawn, the bed made, a television plugged in, and a young couple stops. He puts records on. The girl dances with him in the yard. Afterward she tells people what happened, and the telling never produces the explanation. The wife is gone, and the story does not say where.

Those silences are partly an editor's. On July 8, 1980, Carver wrote his editor at Knopf, Gordon Lish, and asked him to stop the book. Lish had cut two stories by nearly seventy percent and many others by almost half. The manuscript as a whole was cut by about forty percent. The draft called "Beginners" lost more than a third of its length and came back with the title the collection now carries. Knopf published the cut version in April 1981. Carver saw what the cuts had done and was, in his own words in that letter, awed by them, and he also wrote that his sanity was on the line.

When you tell someone about a breakup, or write a scene, the force is often the fact you leave unnamed. A reader can mistake every cut for depth. Sometimes the missing piece is knowledge the writer is holding under the sentence. Sometimes, as with this book, it is another person's knife. Both versions can now be read side by side, so you can see which dark rooms were Carver's and which were opened by the edit.
