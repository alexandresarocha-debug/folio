On the morning of Sunday 28 June 1914 the European continent was at peace, and Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie arrived at Sarajevo station. Thirty-seven days later the continent was at war. Christopher Clark, opening The Sleepwalkers (2012), gives the scale of what followed: 65 million troops mobilized, three empires gone, 20 million military and civilian dead, 21 million wounded. He quotes Fritz Stern calling it the first calamity of the twentieth century, the one from which the others sprang. Clark's question is not the usual one. He wants how the war came about, not a single distant why. A why, he writes, piles imperialism, arms, and alliances on top of the actors until they look like servants of forces they did not choose. The files show something sharper and less grand. Kings, ministers, ambassadors, and generals walked toward the danger in watchful, calculated steps.

He does not start in Sarajevo. Shortly after two in the morning on 11 June 1903, twenty-eight officers of the Serbian army entered the royal palace in Belgrade. They blew the doors of the bedchamber with dynamite, searched the darkened building for nearly two hours, and killed King Alexandar Obrenović and Queen Draga. The bodies were mutilated. The queen's was thrown from a window into the garden. By morning the Obrenović line was finished, and Petar Karađorđević, then in Switzerland, was announced as king. Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, helped organize the murders, was badly wounded in the palace, and lived. He later sat at the center of the nationalist network behind the 1914 plot. Clark's point in opening eleven years early is that Serbian politics was not a pretext the great powers found lying about. It was a cause with its own officers, its own dynasty, and its own dead.

The conclusion's image for the men of 1914 is the one in the title. "The protagonists of 1914 were sleepwalkers, watchful but unseeing, haunted by dreams, yet blind to the reality of the horror they were about to bring into the world." Watchful is doing real work. Clark is not saying they stumbled in a daze. He is saying they calculated, on partial information, inside governments that did not speak with one voice, and still did not see the war they were assembling. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, which assigned the guilt to Germany and its allies, has kept the argument stuck on a charge sheet ever since. His alternative is to name the decisions, Belgrade's included, and let the blame arise from the sequence rather than from a verdict chosen in advance.

That is the use of the book when someone has to say what happened in July 1914. A story with one guilty capital will not match a crisis that ran through Vienna, Berlin, St. Petersburg, Paris, London, and Belgrade, each of them misreading the others and each of them able, at points, to choose differently. Shared responsibility is not the same claim as nobody chose.

Historians in the line of Fritz Fischer, John Röhl among them, answer that the German and Austrian records of early July look like a decision, not a trance. On 5 and 6 July 1914, Berlin gave Vienna the assurance since called the blank cheque: Germany would stand behind Austria against Serbia. Clark narrates that promise. His critics put it at the center, as a choice for a local war that its authors knew might not stay local.
