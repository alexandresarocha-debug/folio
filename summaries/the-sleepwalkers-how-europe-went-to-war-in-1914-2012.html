From why to how

Christopher Clark’s The Sleepwalkers, published in 2012, is a history of the outbreak of the First World War that changes the question on purpose. For decades the dominant scholarly answer to the question of why the war happened had been German. Fritz Fischer’s work in the 1960s argued that Germany’s leaders pursued an aggressive war of expansion, and the Fischer controversy fixed the habits of a generation: look for a primary culprit, look in Berlin, and treat the July crisis of 1914 as the execution of a policy more than as a collapse of judgement. Clark does not write an acquittal of Berlin. He writes a book in which the search for a single culprit is the wrong shape of inquiry. He asks how a continent of wary, heavily armed, internally divided governments moved, in weeks, from a political murder in Sarajevo to a general war that most of them had claimed, often sincerely, to fear. The how-question spreads agency across capitals. It also changes the evidence that matters. Cabinet minutes, ambassadorial habits, the timing of mobilisations, and the internal politics of each state count for more than a manifesto of war aims discovered after the fact.

The title is the interpretation. Sleepwalkers are not unconscious. They are people moving with their eyes open inside a dream they do not recognise as a dream, confident in the next step and unable to see the staircase. Clark’s statesmen were watchful, busy, and well informed by the standards of their offices. They were also captured by pictures of their own situation, pictures of prestige, of the ally who must not be let down, of the opponent who would climb down if pressed, that did not survive contact with the other sleepers. The metaphor has been attacked as a way of dissolving responsibility into atmosphere. It is better read as a claim about failure of political attention. The book’s bulk is a demonstration of what, in each capital, that failure consisted in.

Beginning in Belgrade

The most distinctive structural decision is to begin not in Berlin and not on 28 June 1914, but in Serbia in the years after the violent overthrow of the Obrenović dynasty in 1903. Clark’s Serbia is a new, insecure, aggressively national state, its politics penetrated by secret societies, its officer corps a force in public life, its irredentism aimed at the South Slav lands of Austria-Hungary. The Black Hand and the figure of Apis, Dragutin Dimitrijević, matter because the assassination of Franz Ferdinand was not a thunderbolt from a clear sky. It came out of a milieu in which nationalist officers, intelligence work, and the arming of young Bosnians overlapped, and in which the civilian government did not cleanly control the men who made foreign policy in the shadows. Clark’s account of the Sarajevo plot ties it to Serbian networks without turning the entire Serbian state into a monolith that voted for a European war. The distinction is important to him. Complicity and incoherence are different findings, and he thinks the evidence supports a messy complicity.

From Belgrade he widens to the Balkan wars of 1912 and 1913, which had just destroyed European Turkey as a great territorial fact and had shown that a local war could redraw maps while the great powers stood, anxious and divided, at the edge. Austria-Hungary’s conclusion, that another Serbian victory would be an existential threat to a multinational empire, and Russia’s conclusion, that another Austrian punishment of a Slav client would be an intolerable humiliation, were both in place before Princip fired. The July crisis, in this framing, is the second Balkan crisis, the one the powers failed to keep local. Starting here is Clark’s way of refusing the habit in which the Balkans are scenery for a German plot. The scenery had its own politics, and those politics set the terms on which the great powers then miscalculated.

Six capitals, none of them a chess player

The long central part of the book is a comparative portrait of the governments that would fight. Austria-Hungary is a state that had come to believe a diplomatic success against Serbia was necessary to its survival as a great power, and that designed an ultimatum meant to be rejected. Germany issued the so-called blank cheque and then discovered, later than its rhetoric allowed, that Austrian determination plus Russian determination was a world war. Clark’s Germany is reckless and internally confused rather than the executor of a single blueprint written years earlier. The distinction will not satisfy historians who see, in the military’s weight and in the talk of a preventive war, something closer to intent. It is the distinction the book is for. Russia appears as a state still rebuilding from 1905, bound to Serbia and to France, and increasingly willing to mobilise rather than accept another Balkan humiliation. France appears as a republic whose leaders were determined not to be separated from their Russian ally and not to present themselves, to their own public or to Britain, as the aggressor. Britain appears last and least decided: a liberal cabinet absorbed by Ireland, a foreign secretary, Sir Edward Grey, who had constructed an entente without a clear alliance obligation, and a naval and military alignment with France that was more binding in practice than in the letters Grey was willing to write.

The effect of the portraits is to replace the image of a chess game, one mind against another, with the image of several governments, each arguing with itself. Hawks and doves were not distributed one per country. They were present in every cabinet, and the July crisis shifted the balance inside each of them at different speeds. Clark is very good on the mechanics of that shift: on the way a mobilisation timetable, once started, punished hesitation; on the way alliance loyalty was experienced as honour rather than as a clause; on the way each side’s intelligence about the other was filtered through a decade of scares. Nobody’s plan was a plan for the war they got. Germany’s strategic design, the descendant of the Schlieffen plan, required an attack on France through Belgium if the war became a war with Russia, and so converted an eastern quarrel into a western one and brought Britain in. The plan was not destiny. It was a constraint that narrow men treated as destiny once the political crisis had been allowed to become a military one.

July, day by day

The narrative of the crisis itself is the book’s set piece, and it is where the sleepwalker metaphor either earns its place or becomes irritating. Clark follows the ultimatum to Serbia, the Serbian reply, the arguments in St Petersburg over partial and then general mobilisation, the German declarations, the invasion of Belgium, and the last, thin attempts at localisation and mediation. His emphasis falls on interaction. A step that was defensive in one capital’s story, a mobilisation ordered so as not to be caught unready, was offensive in another’s, evidence that the enemy had chosen war. Because the steps were public and hard to reverse, each government spent the crisis reacting to a version of the others that was already out of date. Sleepwalking, in this precise sense, is the failure to wake into the other side’s timetable. Clark does not claim the principals were gentle. Austrian leaders wanted a war with Serbia. German leaders preferred the risk of a larger war to the risk of restraining their ally and calling it a defeat. Russian leaders preferred the risk of general war to another retreat. The question he keeps asking is why the institutions around these preferences, the alliances that were supposed to deter, the diplomats who were supposed to slow things down, the monarchs who were supposed to remember they were related, failed to open a gap between a Balkan punishment and a continental catastrophe.

The argument about blame

The reception of the book became part of its meaning. In Germany it was widely read and widely welcomed, including by readers who wanted relief from a century of sole responsibility. In other quarters it was criticised as an elegant dilution, a redistribution of guilt so fine that Austria’s determination and Germany’s encouragement no longer had the weight Fischer had given them. The fair statement of Clark’s position is that responsibility was shared and was not equal in kind. Serbia’s nationalist underground supplied the occasion. Austria chose to make the occasion a war. Germany chose to underwrite that choice. Russia chose mobilisation. France chose to stand with Russia. Britain chose, late, to treat the invasion of Belgium and the balance of the continent as its own war. Shared is not the same word as accidental, and Clark does not say accidental. He says that a crisis managed by men who could not see past their own next move produced an outcome none of their separate plans had described. Critics who insist on the primacy of Berlin and Vienna are disagreeing about weights, not about the chronology. The book’s vulnerability is that its literary sympathy is more evenly spread than its own evidence, at certain pages, will really support. Its strength is that any return to a single-cause story now has to pass through the Serbian background, the Russian mobilisation decision, and the French and British constraints, which a German-centred narrative used to be allowed to treat as reactions.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on The Sleepwalkers is this shift from why to how, the starting point in Serbian politics and the alliance system, and the image of leaders who were watchful and still blind to the war they were assembling. The card sets Barbara Tuchman’s The Guns of August beside it, a book about the first month of fighting and about plans once they are in motion, and Margaret MacMillan’s The War That Ended Peace, a book about the longer road from 1900 to 1914 and about a peace that was real before it was thrown away. The three books divide the subject by timescale. MacMillan asks how a globalised, prosperous Europe kept choosing crises.
