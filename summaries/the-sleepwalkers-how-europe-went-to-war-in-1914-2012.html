Christopher Clark begins The Sleepwalkers (2012) in Belgrade, not in a German war council. On 11 June 1903 army officers murdered King Alexander Obrenović and Queen Draga in the palace and brought Peter Karađorđević to the throne. Several of the regicides stayed powerful in the army. One of them, Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, later sat at the center of the Black Hand, the secret society "Unification or Death," founded in 1911. Clark's point in starting there is that the July crisis of 1914 had a Serbian prehistory. Officers tied to that network armed the young men who went to Sarajevo. On 28 June 1914 Gavrilo Princip, nineteen, shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie. A bilateral quarrel between Austria-Hungary and Serbia became a European war because the other capitals misread what their allies and their enemies would do next.

The decisions of that month are on paper. On 5 and 6 July, Kaiser Wilhelm II and Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg assured Austria-Hungary of German support if it moved against Serbia. The phrase historians use is the blank cheque. On 7 July the Austro-Hungarian common ministers decided to send Serbia a note written so that rejection was the likely end. Count István Tisza, the Hungarian premier, resisted a war and then came over. The ultimatum was delivered on 23 July, with forty-eight hours to answer. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July. Russia mobilized, Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August, and the invasion of Belgium on 4 August brought Britain in. Clark's name for the men who ran this sequence is sleepwalkers: watchful, but unseeing. He is rejecting the picture in which one capital wrote the script and the others executed it. Fritz Fischer's Griff nach der Weltmacht (1961) had put the main responsibility on Berlin, using German files and, among them, Bethmann's September Program of 9 September 1914, a list of expansionist war aims drawn up once the fighting had started.

What to do with the metaphor, when deciding how the war began, is to keep the documents next to it. A shared failure of foresight can be true of a system and still false about a particular meeting. Vienna on 7 July chose an ultimatum it expected would mean war with Serbia. Berlin on 5 and 6 July chose to back that choice. Those are decisions, with names and dates. Clark's contribution is the rest of the map: Serbian officers who had already used murder as a political tool, Russian and French calculations about a war they thought they might have to fight, and British hesitation. The sleepwalker image covers that wider blindness. It fits a cabinet that has decided on war less well.

John Röhl and other critics of the book have said the metaphor softens those choices until a deliberate policy looks like a shared accident. Clark does not deny the blank cheque. He denies that it was the unfolding of a single German plan laid years earlier. The argument then turns on a narrower question: how much of 1914 was a plan, and how much was a chain of gambles, each of which the gambler thought he could still win.
