Shortly after two in the morning on 11 June 1903, twenty-eight officers of the Serbian army entered the royal palace in Belgrade. They blew the oak doors of the king's apartments with dynamite, searched the building for nearly two hours by candlelight, and killed King Alexander Obrenović and Queen Draga. The bodies were thrown from a bedroom balcony. The same night the queen's brothers, the prime minister, and the minister of war were killed. Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as Apis, had drafted the conspirators' oath and joined the squad that broke in. He was shot at the main entrance and missed the last of it. By evening the British minister, Sir George Bonham, reported the town quiet and the killings "hailed with open satisfaction." Parliament recalled Petar Karađorđević from Swiss exile and restored the constitution of 1888. Christopher Clark begins the road to 1914 there, inside a Serbian officer corps that had just learned it could kill a king and keep the state.

The line runs to Sarajevo. Apis later sat at the head of the Black Hand and of Serbian military intelligence. In 1914 the assassins' handler in Belgrade was Milan Ciganović, a Black Hand man on the state railways, who reported to Major Vojislav Tankosić, who reported to Apis. On 27 May, Gavrilo Princip and two others were issued four revolvers and six bombs from the Serbian state arsenal at Kragujevac, plus cyanide, and were taken across the Drina by the network's border men. On 28 June, Princip shot Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie. Clark's point is messy complicity. The Serbian government of Nikola Pašić did not vote for a European war. Men inside the Serbian state armed the boys who produced the occasion for one.

From there the July decisions are choices with dates, made in cabinets that could not see the next capital's timetable. Austria-Hungary chose to turn the murder into a war with Serbia. Late on 4 July, foreign minister Berchtold sent Count Alexander Hoyos, thirty-six and already for a military solution, to Berlin with the papers by hand, so the request for German support would be delivered by a hawk. Russia chose general mobilization rather than another Balkan retreat. France stood with Russia. Britain came in as Germany went through Belgium. Shared responsibility, in Clark's account, still has an order. The occasion, the decision for a local war, and the decision to underwrite it are not the same act.

Fritz Fischer's Griff nach der Weltmacht (1961) remains the contrary case: that Germany's leaders pursued an expansionist war and used the crisis to launch it. Clark's evidence is the record of those weeks across the capitals, Serbia included. It does not clear Berlin of the promise it gave Vienna. It refuses to let that promise be the whole story.
