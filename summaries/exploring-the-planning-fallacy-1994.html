Background

People who have missed deadlines before will cheerfully name a new deadline they are about to miss. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky had given this habit a name, the planning fallacy, and a diagnosis. Planners take the inside view. They imagine the steps of the project in front of them, picture those steps going well, and treat the picture as a forecast. They set aside the outside view, which is the dull record of how long similar projects actually took. Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin and Michael Ross set out, in a 1994 paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, to catch that habit in the act. Exploring the Planning Fallacy is the experimental paper Folio's card relies on for the student evidence, as distinct from the later evidence about bridges and budgets.

The card's headline numbers come from one of their studies. Psychology students estimated when they would finish their honours theses. The average prediction was about 34 days. The average actual completion was about 56 days. Only around 30 per cent finished by the date they had predicted. Even a request for a worst case did not reliably produce a date the thesis would meet. The paper is the machinery behind those figures: what people were thinking while they named them, and what happened when the outside view was forced into the conversation.

The question

Why do people underestimate how long their own tasks will take, even when they know, in the abstract, that tasks run late? Buehler, Griffin and Ross wanted the underestimate in a place where memory of past lateness was available and still might go unused. A thesis is ideal for that purpose. The student has done long pieces of work before, has usually been late, and is now staring at a particular set of chapters that feel, from the inside, like a different story. The question is whether the forecast is built from the chapters or from the history. A second question follows. If the history is dragged into the open, does the forecast move, and do people drag it into the open on their own?

How the studies were set up

In the thesis study, students who were part way through an honours project predicted when they would finish. They were also asked for a worst-case date, a time by which they would finish if things went badly. The experimenters then waited. Predicted dates and actual dates could be compared without relying on anyone's memory of how late she had been. That comparison is the paper's anchor in the world. Optimism that shows up only in a story about last year is weaker than optimism that is on the record before the year is over.

Other studies asked people to think aloud while predicting how long a task would take, or to recall relevant past projects before they named a date. The think-aloud protocols are the window on the inside view. If Kahneman and Tversky were right, the speech should be full of plans, steps and intentions, and thin on remembered overruns. The recall instructions are the experimental outside view. If people have the knowledge and merely fail to consult it, then an instruction to consult it should lengthen the forecast, and the absence of the instruction should leave the knowledge sitting unused. Further tasks, smaller than a thesis, let the same contrast be run on a shorter clock, so that the finding would not stand or fall on one species of student project.

What they found

The thesis predictions were optimistic in the way the card describes. On average, students expected to finish in about 34 days and finished in about 56. Only around 30 per cent were done by their own predicted date. The worst-case question, which might have been expected to recruit the memory of disaster, often produced a date that was still too kind. People could imagine a mishap. They imagined a mishap inside a plan they still believed, rather than stepping outside the plan and asking what had happened last time they had believed one.

The think-aloud records showed the inside view at work. Participants talked about the specific steps of the specific project, about how they intended to proceed, and about the obstacles they could already name and therefore, they felt, manage. They rarely volunteered the distribution of their own past completions. When the procedure required them to recall relevant past projects, and to consider how those projects had gone, the forecasts became less sanguine. The knowledge had been there. The spontaneous forecast had not consulted it. Buehler, Griffin and Ross read that pair of findings as the definition of the fallacy made observable. The error is not ignorance of one's history. It is a way of building a prediction in which the history does not get a vote.

How the authors read the results

They read the results as support for the inside-view account, and as a reason to prefer the outside view as a remedy. A plan is a causal story about the future of one project. It is concrete, and it feels like information. A track record is a base rate about a class of projects, and it feels like a counsel of gloom offered by someone who has not understood the plan. The psychology of the fallacy is the victory of the story over the class. Because the story is about the future, it can be edited. Obstacles that would ruin the date are either left out or promoted to problems the planner intends to solve. The track record cannot be edited in the same motion. It has already happened. The only way it loses is by not being asked for.

The authors are correspondingly specific about what would count as a cure. Exhorting people to try harder, or to remember that planning is hard, leaves the inside view in charge. Asking for the class of similar cases, and starting the forecast from how those cases turned out, changes the information on the table. The thesis study is the illustration that the second move does not come naturally. Students who could have named their own late essays did not treat those essays as the reference class for this essay, until the procedure made them.

Limits

The participants are students, and a thesis, however consuming, is a small project beside a railway or a software migration. The paper establishes the fallacy and its mechanism in individual planning. It does not measure cost overruns, political incentives, or the difference between a person who is fooling herself and a person who is fooling a funder. Bent Flyvbjerg's work, which the card places next to this paper, is the extension to projects in which those extra forces are the story. Reading Buehler, Griffin and Ross as a finding about Olympic budgets would skip a step they did not take.

There is a limit inside the mechanism as well. Forcing a recall of past lateness improved predictions in their studies. It did not abolish optimism, and a laboratory instruction is not a habit. People who have just been shown the outside view can return, on the next unaided forecast, to the plan that flatters them. The paper shows that the outside view is available and neglected. It does not show that a single reminder installs it. Nor does it claim that every task is underestimated. A task one dreads can be overestimated, and a task with a hard external deadline can be finished because the deadline, rather than the forecast, is in charge. The fallacy is the systematic underweighting of the record in the kind of open-ended plan their participants were making.

Replications and critiques

The planning fallacy became a standard entry in the judgement literature, and the basic pattern, optimistic forecasts in the face of discouraging personal history, has been observed well beyond honours theses. The theoretical argument has been less about whether the pattern exists than about how many mechanisms sit inside it. The inside view is the mechanism this paper defends. Wishful thinking, impression management, and a simple failure to remember past pain have all been proposed as accomplices. The think-aloud evidence is the paper's way of showing that the plan, and not only the wish, is on the person's mind at the moment of prediction. Later field work has added a mechanism the student studies cannot see clearly: strategic misrepresentation, in which a planner states a date she does not believe because a truthful date would cancel the project. That mechanism matters for public budgets. It is not required to explain a student alone with a thesis.

Kahneman has told a personal version of the same structure, which the card retells because it makes the outside view vivid. A team he led, writing a curriculum, was confident the work would take a short time. Asked for the record of similar teams, an experienced colleague gave a much longer typical duration, and a sobering rate of projects that never finished. The book took far longer than the team's inside view had allowed. The anecdote is Kahneman's, not a result of the 1994 experiments. It is useful because it is the same mistake the experiments isolated, committed by people who already understood the mistake in theory.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The planning fallacy and the outside view, takes this paper as the experimental core of the idea. The about 34 days, the about 56 days, and the roughly 30 per cent who finished on their predicted date are findings of these studies, and they are findings about students finishing theses, not about megaprojects. The mechanism the card teaches, the inside view crowding out the record, is what the think-aloud and the prompted-recall results display. Flyvbjerg's 2006 paper and his later book with Dan Gardner take the outside view into reference-class forecasting and into the politics of large projects. Read Buehler, Griffin and Ross for the fallacy at the scale where a person can still hear her own plan being built. The remedy they test is the remedy the rest of the card tries to make into a practice.
