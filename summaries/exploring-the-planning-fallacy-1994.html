Roger Buehler, Dale Griffin, and Michael Ross phoned 37 psychology students at the University of Waterloo who were finishing an honours thesis, 27 women and 10 men. Asked for their most accurate prediction, the students said they would submit in 33.9 days. They took 55.5 days. Eleven of the 37, 29.7 percent, finished by the date they had called their best guess. If everything went as well as it possibly could, they said 27.4 days, and 10.8 percent hit that date. If everything went as poorly as it possibly could, they said 48.6 days, and 48.7 percent were done by then. Four students had still not finished when the records stopped, two semesters later, and they were left out of the 55.5-day average. The predictions were not empty. They correlated 0.77 with the real dates. They were simply too kind, including the version people produced when invited to imagine a disaster.

The same tilt showed up the following week on ordinary jobs. Of 104 undergraduates, 101 could be reached again. For academic work such as essays and lab reports, they predicted 5.8 days and took 10.7. For jobs such as fixing a bicycle or cleaning an apartment, they predicted 5.0 days and took 9.2. They finished 37.1 percent of the academic projects and 42.5 percent of the others by the predicted time. Telling some of them, up front, that the researchers would check the forecast did not remove the optimism. They were also quite sure they would make the date.

The forecast is a story about this project: the steps, the obstacles already named, and the intention to handle them. It is a poor substitute for the question of how long the last similar jobs took, including the ones that stalled. When you promise a deadline for a report, a renovation, or a piece of software, the date that comes from walking through the plan is the inside view. The date that comes from the record of comparable work is the one the thesis students did not reach for until the procedure made them. Even the worst case they could picture from inside the plan was a date fewer than half of them met.
