Ernest Hemingway opens Death in the Afternoon (1932) by saying why he went to the bullring. After the war, the place he could still see violent death was Spain. "I was trying to learn to write, commencing with the simplest things, and one of the simplest things of all and the most fundamental is violent death." The book is the lesson written out: the breeding of the fighting bull, the difference between that animal and beef cattle, the picador, the banderillas, the cloth, the kill. He is hard on fraud, a bull whose horns have been shaved, a matador who picks an easy animal and then acts out courage, a crowd that applauds what it cannot see. An invented Old Lady interrupts him with the questions a skeptical reader would ask. He answers at length. The book is a manual because he assumes the American reader has not grown up with the corrida and is not entitled to skip the terms.

Inside that long explanation, in chapter 16, he states the rule later called the iceberg. "If a writer of prose knows enough about what he is writing about he may omit things that he knows and the reader, if the writer is writing truly enough, will have a feeling of those things as strongly as though the writer had stated them. The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water. A writer who omits things because he does not know them only makes hollow places in his writing." The fraction is his. The condition is the part that gets dropped when the sentence is quoted on its own. Omission is earned by knowledge. Leave out what you could have put in, and the absence can still carry weight. Leave out what you never learned, and the reader feels a hole. A book that stops to define the vara, the banderillas, and the line of a charge is not breaking the rule. It is doing the other job. You do not omit the anatomy of an art for a reader who has never seen the art. You may omit, in a story, the war that taught a man's hands, if you know the war and the hands are written truly.

The practical split is the one people lose. "Leave it out" is not a style. It is a result. If you are writing about a hospital, a divorce, a regiment, or a trade, and you do not know the sequence, the omission will read as vagueness, not as dignity. If you do know it, the thing you cut can make the remaining sentence heavier, because the reader senses the pressure of what was not said. Hemingway ties that pressure to death in public, under rules, where a flourish that is not aimed at the kill is only decoration. You can refuse his defense of the bullfight and still use the test. Before you cut, ask whether the missing part is solid under the water, or whether you are calling a gap an iceberg.
