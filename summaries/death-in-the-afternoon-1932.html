A book that teaches a spectacle

Death in the Afternoon, published in 1932, is Ernest Hemingway's long prose book on Spanish bullfighting. It is a work of instruction, history, and argument, written for readers, especially American readers, who have not grown up with the corrida and who may feel that they already know enough to condemn it. Hemingway takes the opposite bet. He thinks ignorance is what produces the easy reaction, and he sets out to explain the bull, the men, the ring, and the sequence of the afternoon in enough detail that a reader could sit down in a plaza de toros and know what was being attempted. The book has chapters of exposition, stretches of dialogue with an invented Old Lady who interrupts him, a glossary of Spanish terms, and photographs. It is a made object with several kinds of prose in it, including a story, A Natural History of the Dead, which grows out of the conversation with the Old Lady.

The subject is death in public, performed under rules. Hemingway describes the breeding of the fighting bull, the difference between that animal and cattle raised for beef, and the way the bull's power, sight, and habit of charging are used by the men in the ring. He divides the afternoon into its phases. Picadors on padded horses test and tire the bull with the vara. Banderilleros place the barbed sticks. The matador then works the bull with the cloth and, if the work has been honest, kills with the sword. Hemingway is severe about fraud: a bull whose horns have been tampered with, a matador who chooses an easy animal and then pretends to courage, a crowd that applauds what it does not understand. The book is a defence of a ritual he loves and a complaint against the versions of it that have gone slack.

What he wants the reader to be able to see

Much of the book's bulk is there because seeing is technical. The angle of a charge, the way a man cites the bull with his voice and his feet, the difference between a pass that moves the bull correctly and a pass that only looks florid from a cheap seat: Hemingway writes these things out. He writes about particular matadors, including Juan Belmonte and Joselito, and about the standard their rivalry set for the people who came after. He writes about Maera and about afternoons that failed. The praise is specific, and so is the disgust. A reader who wants a short essay on courage will find instead a manual that keeps returning to the kill. Hemingway thinks the kill is the point the rest of the afternoon is responsible to. Style in the ring, on this account, is the manner of approaching a real danger and a real death, and manner without that approach is decoration.

The Old Lady is his device for staging the reader's resistance. She wants sensation, or she wants to be spared, or she asks the blunt question he knows is coming. He answers at length, sometimes solemnly and sometimes with a hardness that is itself a performance. The device lets the book contain argument without pretending that the argument is a neutral encyclopaedia entry. It also lets him change key. A Natural History of the Dead, folded into this conversation, turns from the bullring to the dead of the Great War and to the prose of writers who had generalised about death without looking at it. The juxtaposition is deliberate. Bullfighting, war, and writing are three places, in this book, where a person can lie about what he has seen, and three places where the lie has a texture a trained reader can feel.

The familiar image of omission

Inside this expansive book sits the short passage that later readers lifted out and called the iceberg theory. Hemingway offers the familiar image of an iceberg, only one eighth of it above the water, and he ties the image to prose. A writer who knows enough may leave things out, and the knowing will still be felt in what remains. A writer who leaves things out because he does not know them produces a hollow. The dignity he is talking about depends on the hidden part being real. Death in the Afternoon states the principle in the middle of a book that seems, at first glance, to obey the opposite principle. Very little about the corrida is omitted. The terms are defined. The sequence is repeated until a novice could follow it. The apparent contradiction is the point of reading the passage where it actually occurs.

He can explain the bullfight at this length because his reader is assumed not to know it. Omission, as he states it here, is a liberty earned by knowledge and spent for a reader who can receive the unsaid. You do not omit the anatomy of an art when the reader has never seen the art. You might omit, in a story, the war that has shaped a man's hands, if you know the war and if the hands are written truly. The iceberg passage is advice about narrative economy from a writer who, on this subject, has chosen pedagogy. Putting the advice back in the book stops it from becoming a slogan about brevity for its own sake. Brevity is what you may use when the underwater part is solid. Explanation is what you owe when it is not yet shared.

Death, emotion, and the prose around them

The book's other lasting claim is about emotion. Hemingway distinguishes feeling that comes from what is actually happening in the ring from feeling that the crowd and the performer counterfeit for each other. He is interested in the moment when a spectator who understands the danger suddenly feels it, and in the way a matador can fake that moment. This is why the glossary and the photographs belong to the aesthetic argument. They are attempts to widen the set of people who can tell the difference. A reader in London or New York cannot inherit the knowledge of a Spanish aficionado by birth. The book tries to lend it, with the risks that any such lending involves: the instructor's vanity, the romance of a foreign ritual, the possibility of explaining your way around the cruelty.

Hemingway does not soften the cruelty. Horses are gored. Bulls die. Men are injured and sometimes killed. He describes these facts and still holds that the form, when it is honest, gives death a clarity that modern life and modern writing often blur. A reader is free to refuse the holding. The book is useful either way, because it shows a writer tying his sentences to a practice with rules, a history, a vocabulary, and a public that can be wrong. The prose is often plain, and it is also opinionated, comic, and sometimes bullying. Death in the Afternoon is a reminder that the famous advice about omission came from a writer who could also write a long book of knowledge, and who thought the long book was justified by the thing it was trying to make visible.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the iceberg begins here, in a book about bullfighting, because this is where Hemingway states the familiar image and attaches a condition to it. The condition is knowledge. Leaving the war out of a fishing story, or leaving the name of an operation out of a conversation in a bar, works only if the writer could have put it in and chose the stronger shape. The card then follows the idea into his later interviews and into Raymond Carver's pared-down stories. Those later stops make more sense after this one. Death in the Afternoon shows the principle beside its limit: a subject the reader does not share has to be taught, term by term, before anyone has earned the right to be silent about it.
