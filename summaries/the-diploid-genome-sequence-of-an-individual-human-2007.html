The first human genome sequences were mosaics built from several people, and they recorded only one copy of each chromosome. But each of us carries two copies, one from each parent. In 2007 Samuel Levy and colleagues published a diploid genome of a single named person, meaning both copies were read and kept apart. The donor was J. Craig Venter, who is also an author of the paper.

The team sequenced about 32 million random DNA fragments with the older Sanger method, giving about 7.5-fold coverage of each region, and assembled 2,810 million bases. Comparing the result with the public reference genome, they found more than 4.1 million variants covering 12.3 million bases. Of these, 3,213,401 were SNPs, single-letter changes. There were also 53,823 block substitutions, 292,102 heterozygous insertions or deletions, 559,473 homozygous ones and 90 inversions, plus copy-number differences.

The key result is a matter of counting. SNPs made up 78 percent of the variant events, but the other 22 percent, the insertions, deletions and larger changes, accounted for about 74 percent of the variant DNA letters. If you only count single letters, people look extremely alike. If you count every changed letter, they look less alike.

That is why the paper gave a conservative estimate that at least 0.5 percent variation exists between two copies of a genome. The authors summed the heterozygous SNPs and other small changes, the larger variants and about 10 million bases of copy-number variation, and divided by the genome size. In their words, only 99.5 percent similarity exists between the two chromosomal copies of an individual, and variation between two people is as much as five times higher than earlier estimates. They also found that 44 percent of annotated genes in this one man carried at least one variant on one copy.

What this means in real life: the 99.9 and 99.5 percent figures are both correct. They answer different questions, one about sites and one about total letters. When someone quotes a figure, ask what was counted.

Caution: this is one person, sequenced with 2007 technology. The authors note that their method missed many heterozygous insertions and deletions, and they corrected for this with a model. Later projects such as 1000 Genomes, with thousands of people, confirm the general picture but give ranges rather than a single number.
