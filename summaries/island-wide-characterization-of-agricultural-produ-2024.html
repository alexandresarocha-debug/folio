A key part of the collapse story is that Rapa Nui once had a large population, fed by extensive rock gardens: fields mulched with stones, which raise soil nutrients and hold in moisture for crops such as sweet potato. If the gardens were vast, the population could have been large, and its fall dramatic.

Dylan Davis and colleagues mapped rock gardens across the whole island using shortwave infrared satellite imagery and machine learning, then checked the output by hand. They found a total of just 0.76 square kilometres of rock gardens, about one fifth of the most conservative previous estimate. They also estimated that around 25 square kilometres of the island had been disturbed by urban growth and modern farming, which could have destroyed some gardens.

Using their new figures with published yields of sweet potato, they estimated that the population could not have exceeded about 4,000 people. An earlier model using larger garden estimates had concluded that the population could have exceeded 16,000. The new estimate fits the roughly 3,000 people that early European visitors reported in the 18th century.

The authors conclude that the island never supported the huge population that the collapse narrative assumes, and so there was no giant population to collapse.

What this means in real life: remote sensing and machine learning can test historical claims across a whole landscape, rather than relying on small samples extrapolated to an entire island.

Caution: the authors stress that the population estimates are based only on sweet potatoes grown in rock gardens. Marine foods and other crops such as bananas, yams, taro and sugar cane would have raised the island's carrying capacity, and some gardens may have been destroyed by later land use or missed by the satellite method.
