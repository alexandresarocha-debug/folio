Does money make people happier? Comparing rich and poor people mixes up money with everything else that differs between them. Jonathan Gardner and Andrew Oswald used lottery wins instead, because a win is largely a matter of luck.

Their data came from the British Household Panel Survey, a nationally representative sample of more than 5,000 British households and over 10,000 adults, interviewed every year from 1991. The survey includes the General Health Questionnaire, a standard 12-question measure of psychological strain covering sleep, concentration, confidence, feeling useful and feeling happy.

Out of 33,605 person-years in the data, more than 26,000 had no observed win and 4,822 had small wins, between one pound and £999. Wins of £1,000 or more, up to £120,000, were uncommon: there were 137. Those are the focus of the paper, with the no-win and small-win groups as comparisons.

The medium-sized winners went on to show significantly better psychological health than both comparison groups. Two years after a win, their average mental wellbeing had improved by 1.4 points on the questionnaire's scale, where lower strain means better health.

The authors present this as longitudinal evidence of a kind the oft-quoted cross-sectional study by Brickman and colleagues, based on a small sample of winners, could not provide.

What this means in real life: a windfall that eases money worries may improve mental health, though the benefit may take time to show.

Caution: the number of medium-sized winners was small, and people who play the lottery more often are more likely to win. The prizes were far smaller than the jackpots in the classic study.

Source. Gardner, J., and Oswald, A. J. (2007). Money and mental wellbeing: a longitudinal study of medium-sized lottery wins. Journal of Health Economics, 26(1), 49-60. doi:10.1016/j.jhealeco.2006.08.004. Working paper: https://docs.iza.org/dp2233.pdf
