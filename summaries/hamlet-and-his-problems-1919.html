In 1919 T. S. Eliot called Hamlet an artistic failure, and he named the scene that shows why. Hamlet's disgust is real, and it is too large for the facts on the stage. The feeling is occasioned by his mother, Gertrude, "but his mother is not an adequate equivalent for it; his disgust envelops and exceeds her." He cannot understand the feeling, because he cannot find an object for it, and so it poisons his life and blocks every action. Make Gertrude more guilty, Eliot says, and you would have written the formula for a different emotion. Her very slightness is what stirs a disgust she cannot represent.

The contrast is Macbeth. "The state of mind of Lady Macbeth walking in her sleep has been communicated to you by a skilful accumulation of imagined sensory impressions." Macbeth's words when he hears that his wife is dead feel as if the events released them. Eliot's name for this match is the objective correlative: "a set of objects, a situation, a chain of events which shall be the formula of that particular emotion," so that once the senses have taken in the facts, the emotion arrives with them. Hamlet, on this test, keeps announcing a feeling the play's objects are too small to produce.

A scene in a story, a film, or a speech works when the thing a person can see is enough to produce the feeling, and fails when the writer has to declare how large the feeling is because the events will not carry it. A woman sleepwalking, a repeated phrase, a smashed object, can do what an announcement of despair cannot. Later critics have objected that no set of objects produces one emotion in every reader, which is a premise Eliot's test relies on. The practical demand survives the objection. If the feeling exceeds the facts you have put in the room, the room is the problem.
