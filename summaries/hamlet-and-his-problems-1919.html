A short essay with a severe verdict

Hamlet and His Problems is an essay T. S. Eliot published in 1919 and collected the next year in The Sacred Wood. It is only a few pages long, and it opens a quarrel that has lasted ever since. Eliot argues that criticism of Hamlet has spent its energy on the prince as a person, a puzzle of motive and delay, and has neglected the play as a made object that either does or does not succeed. He thinks the most influential critics have remade the hero in their own image. Goethe's Hamlet, on this account, is a version of Goethe's own sensitive failure of action. Coleridge's Hamlet is a version of Coleridge. The essay's first move is to clear that kind of identification out of the way, so that a question about construction can be asked.

He then takes up a historical claim associated with scholars such as J. M. Robertson. Hamlet, in this view, is a play Shakespeare reworked from earlier revenge material, including a lost play of the kind Thomas Kyd wrote, and the old material resists the new motives Shakespeare wants to put in it. Eliot treats the play as a stratification. Layers of plot remain from the revenge tradition, the ghost, the delayed killing, the play within the play, while the feeling Shakespeare is actually interested in belongs to a different problem, one the inherited story cannot quite state. The essay asks you to consider that some of the play's famous opacity is a sign of this mismatch, an artist struggling with a job the received fable will not let him finish.

The objective correlative

In the middle of that argument Eliot states the idea that survived the argument. The only way of expressing emotion in the form of art, he claims, is by finding an objective correlative: a set of objects, a situation, a chain of events which will be the formula of that particular emotion, so that when the external facts are given, the emotion is evoked at once. The phrase has a prehistory he does not go into. The American painter Washington Allston had used it earlier in the nineteenth century. Eliot's use is the one that entered literary criticism, and it is a craftsman's test. Feeling in art has to arrive as something the senses and the sequence can carry. If you have the feeling and you lack the formula of facts, you have a personal intensity the work cannot transmit.

His contrast is Macbeth. The sleepwalking scene, the blood, the knocking at the gate, the chain of consequences that begins with a prophecy and a murder: these, he thinks, are adequate to Lady Macbeth's state and to the play's horror. The external facts are the right facts, and the emotion arrives because they are present. Hamlet, in his judgement, fails the same test. The disgust the prince pours over his mother's remarriage exceeds what the plot has made available. A hasty and, in the play's terms, incestuous marriage is a great wrong inside the world of the court. Eliot does not think it is a sufficient equivalent for the nausea Hamlet expresses, a nausea that seems to cover life itself. The emotion is in excess of the facts. The play is therefore, in the sentence that made the essay notorious, an artistic failure, however interesting a failure it remains.

What the verdict leaves you free to think

Many readers and many critics simply reject the verdict. Hamlet has been playable for four centuries, and the excess Eliot complains of is, for a large tradition, the subject: a grief and a revulsion that will not fit the revenge plot they have been given, a mind too large for the job. You can hold that view and still keep the tool. The objective correlative does not require you to demote Shakespeare. It requires you to ask, of any scene, which objects and which events are doing the feeling's work. If the answer is only the name of the feeling, the scene is thinner than it thinks. If the answer is a chain of facts that would produce the feeling in a reader who had not been told its name, the scene has the formula Eliot demanded. The essay can be wrong about one play and right about the demand.

The demand is stricter than the classroom slogan that says show, do not tell. Eliot is a poet of statements as well as of images, and the essay does not forbid a character to say that he suffers. It says the saying will be artistically real when a situation supports it, and baffling when the situation is too small. A novelist who writes that a woman was heartbroken has not automatically failed. She has failed if nothing in the preceding chain makes heartbreak the inevitable reading, and if the adjective is being asked to do the scene's work alone. Conversely, a page of objects with no organising emotion is a catalogue. The correlative is a match between the two, external formula and inner state, precise enough that the match feels like discovery.

The neighbouring poem and the neighbouring essay

Eliot's own early poetry is the best illustration of the standard he was setting in 1919. The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock, published a few years earlier, never delivers a clinical name for its speaker's condition as its main event. It gives an evening spread out against the sky, streets that follow like an argument, a room where women talk of Michelangelo, coffee spoons by which a life might be measured out, a man who turns back from a question he had prepared to ask. Anxiety, sexual fear, and social paralysis are intelligible because those objects and that failed errand are the formula. Whether every reader names the emotion in the same words is less important, on Eliot's test, than whether the emotion arrives when the facts are given. Prufrock is a small dramatic situation that has found its correlative. The essay on Hamlet is the theory of why that kind of situation works, applied, with deliberate provocation, to the most famous play in the language.

The Sacred Wood places the essay near Tradition and the Individual Talent, the piece in which Eliot argues that poetry is an escape from personality and from emotion in the raw, a making in which the significant emotion is the one that has found its form. The objective correlative is that argument translated into a test a writer can apply to a scene. Personality and feeling remain the material. They become art when they have been precipitated into objects and events that carry them without a standing explanation. Readers who dislike the impersonality doctrine still use the test, often under other names, whenever they ask a draft where the feeling has gone and receive, as an answer, only the feeling's label.

Why it matters here

The card on the objective correlative takes its definition and its quarrel from this essay. Eliot's claim that Hamlet is an artistic failure is the provocation. His comparison with the sleepwalking scene is the positive instance. The phrase itself is older than he is, and the judgement of the play is one a reader may refuse. What the card wants from the essay is the tool. Emotion in art travels by a formula of objects, a situation, a chain of events. Folio's deeper reading points at Hamlet so you can test Eliot against the play, and at Prufrock so you can see the same writer meeting his own standard. Read the essay for the few pages in which a young critic risks a masterpiece against a definition, and then keep the definition, which has outlived the risk.
