How alike are we and chimpanzees? In 2005 an international consortium published a draft genome of the common chimpanzee and lined it up against the human genome. The draft came from one male chimpanzee, Clint, and covered about 94 percent of his genome. The best matching stretches of the two genomes covered about 2.4 billion letters of high-quality sequence.

Counting single-letter changes, the paper found a genome-wide difference of 1.23 percent between a copy of the human genome and a copy of the chimpanzee genome. Some of that is ordinary variation within each species, so the authors estimated that the fixed difference between the species is about 1.06 percent or less. These are the numbers behind the popular claim that we share about 98 to 99 percent of our DNA with chimps.

But single letters are only part of the story. The two genomes also differ by insertions and deletions. These events are much rarer, about 5 million compared with about 35 million single-letter changes, but many are long. The authors estimated that each genome contains 40 to 45 million letters of its own sequence, a total of about 90 million. That corresponds to about 3 percent of both genomes and, in the paper's words, dwarfs the 1.23 percent difference from single letters. Add the two together and the overall match is roughly 96 percent.

Genes look even closer. About 29 percent of matching human and chimpanzee proteins are identical, and the typical protein differs by only two amino acids, one change on each lineage since our common ancestor. The paper also found that a kind of short jumping DNA element, called a SINE, has been three times more active in humans, while chimpanzees acquired two new families of retroviral elements.

What this means in real life: 98 percent is a fair headline for single-letter changes, and 96 percent is fairer once you count inserted and deleted DNA. Either way, the difference between humans and chimps is more than ten times the 0.1 percent single-letter difference between two people.

Caution: this was a draft built from about 3.6 reads per letter on the main chromosomes, and much of the hardest repetitive DNA could not be compared at all. The 3 percent indel figure is an estimate built partly from mapping chimpanzee fragments to the human genome. Any single percentage depends on what is counted and which parts of the genomes can be lined up.

Source. The Chimpanzee Sequencing and Analysis Consortium (2005). Initial sequence of the chimpanzee genome and comparison with the human genome. Nature, 437(7055), 69-87. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature04072
