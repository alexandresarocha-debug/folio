The same question, with more people and more labs

In 2016 Maria Eugenia Panero and colleagues published a paper whose title asks the question directly: Does reading a single passage of literary fiction really improve theory of mind? It is an attempt at replication of Kidd and Castano's 2013 report. Three research groups took part. Seven hundred and ninety-two participants were randomly assigned to one of four conditions: literary fiction, popular fiction, non-fiction, or no reading. The main outcome was the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, the same advanced theory-of-mind measure that had carried the earlier result. The groups worked with the original materials and design closely enough that a failure, if it came, would be a failure of the effect under the conditions that were supposed to produce it.

The institutions behind the groups included researchers at Pennsylvania, Pace, Boston College, and Oklahoma, a spread that matters for a finding born in one laboratory and then told as a general fact about reading. Replication here means more than repeating a procedure once in the next room. It means asking whether the procedure still yields the literary advantage when other hands run it, on a sample large enough that a modest effect would have a fair chance to show. The paper is written in that spirit. It treats Kidd and Castano's claim as important, famous, and specific, and it tests the specific claim.

What turned up on the day of the test

On the day-of measure, the literary passages did not win. Panero and colleagues found no significant advantage in Eyes-test scores for literary fiction compared with popular fiction, compared with non-fiction, or compared with reading nothing at all. A single assigned passage, under these conditions, did not produce the temporary enhancement the 2013 paper had reported. The abstract's conclusion is correspondingly narrow. The one-session effect was the thing at issue, and the one-session effect did not appear. A reader who stops at that sentence has the paper's negative result, and still does not have the paper's whole argument, because the authors also measured something slower.

They gave participants the Author Recognition Test, a checklist procedure that estimates lifetime exposure to fiction by asking people which names on a mixed list are real authors. Across conditions, that score predicted Eyes-test performance. People who recognised more authors tended to do better at reading eyes, whether or not the text they had just been assigned was literary. This is the pattern that makes the paper more interesting than a simple demolition. The brief experimental contrast went quiet. The correlation between a life of reading and the test stayed noisy and alive, which is what earlier correlational work had suggested. Panero and colleagues offer two readings of that correlation, and they decline to choose between them on these data. People who are already good at theory of mind may be drawn to fiction. A long habit of reading may gradually strengthen theory of mind. They add a third caution that any honest summary has to keep: verbal ability and other advantages can sit underneath both the reading and the test score.

Why a famous effect can shrink when the room gets larger

The replication is a lesson in what the original design was sensitive to. Kidd and Castano's literary texts were short and chosen by a reputation rule. The Eyes test is a particular skill, naming states from a cropped face, with a word list in front of you. An effect that depends on a precise mix of passage, participant pool, and scoring can be real in one set of rooms and absent in another without anyone having cheated. Panero's paper is written to keep that possibility open and still to insist on the practical consequence. If the effect needs the original laboratory's exact weather in order to appear, it is a poor foundation for advice about education or for headlines about empathy. A result that will guide practice has to survive a change of hands.

Kidd and Castano replied. They argued about exclusions, about whether everyone had truly read, about random assignment, about the author-recognition measure. The replicators answered those points in a further note, and they observed that even the reanalysis Kidd and Castano preferred on the replication data did not restore the original pattern of a unique, immediate literary advantage. The exchange is worth knowing because it shows the claim being given a second chance on the critics' own terms. The second chance still did not yield the 2013 result. Later commentary has continued to argue about methods, as it should. The state of the evidence after this paper is no longer the state of the evidence in the week Science published the first report.

What the paper leaves standing

Panero and colleagues are explicit that they have not shown reading to be useless for social understanding. Their last move is to separate three claims that the public discussion had glued together. The first claim, that one passage of literary fiction uniquely and immediately improves theory of mind, is the claim their experiment fails to support. The second claim, that people who have read a lot of fiction do better on a mind-reading test, remains compatible with their author-recognition result. The third claim, that the second fact is caused by the reading, is not settled by an experiment that only manipulates a single sitting. Cause, if it runs at all, may run over years, and it may run in the other direction, and it may be partly a third factor wearing the mask of both.

That separation is the paper's service to the topic. It lets a reader keep the novel and lose the overclaim. A literature class, a private habit, a childhood of stories: none of these was randomised in 2016, and the authors do not pretend to have evaluated them. What was randomised was a passage. The passage was the unit Kidd and Castano had offered as evidence. Meeting that unit with a larger sample and more laboratories is how a science of reading either confirms a spark or shows that the spark was fragile. Here it was fragile. The slower association between knowing authors and reading eyes is a different research problem, older and still open, and this paper hands it back in cleaner condition than it found it.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on fiction and empathy exists to stop the 2013 headline from standing alone. This replication is the reason. It uses the original kind of materials, a much larger group, and four conditions, and it does not find that a single literary passage outperforms popular fiction, non-fiction, or silence on the Eyes test. It does find, again, that lifetime familiarity with fiction predicts the test. The card's summary follows that shape: mixed evidence, modest effects in later averages, a robust-looking correlation whose cause is still uncertain. Read Panero when you want to see how a celebrated one-session effect behaves under replication. The behaviour is the finding. A reading life might still matter. An hour in the lab, on this evidence, did not carry the weight the first paper asked it to carry.
