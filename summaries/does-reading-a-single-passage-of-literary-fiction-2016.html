In 2016 three research groups redid the claim that one short piece of literary fiction immediately makes people better at reading minds. Maria Eugenia Panero, Deena Skolnick Weisberg, Jessica Black, Thalia R. Goldstein, Jennifer L. Barnes, Hiram Brownell, and Ellen Winner randomly assigned 792 adults to literary fiction, popular fiction, nonfiction, or no reading, using texts from the 2013 Science paper by David Comer Kidd and Emanuele Castano. Afterward everyone took the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test: photographs of eyes, and a choice of words for the feeling.

Literary fiction had no advantage. Against nonfiction, the adjusted means were 26.81 after literary fiction and 27.02 after nonfiction, F(1, 167) = 0.08, p = .775, d = 0.07. Against popular fiction, literary fiction scored 25.78 and popular fiction 25.99, F(1, 191) = 0.83, p = .363, d = 0.05. Against no reading at all, the means were 26.31 and 26.25, F(1, 53.6) = 0.08, p = .775, d = 0.01. In the pooled raw scores, people who had just read literary fiction averaged 26.24, and people who had read nothing averaged 26.06. The nonfiction group, 109 people, averaged 27.07.

What did predict the eyes test was the Author Recognition Test, a checklist of real writers salted with fake names, used as a measure of how much fiction a person has been exposed to over time. Across the 792 people that score correlated with the eyes test at r = .47. In Kidd and Castano's own data the same correlation was .26. Knowing authors, or whatever else that checklist also picks up, including verbal skill, tracked the mind-reading score. The passage just read did not.

For a meeting, a clinic, or a first date, the usable lesson is modest. Reading a prize story in the half hour beforehand is unlikely to change how accurately you name what someone feels. A long habit of reading fiction remains associated with that accuracy. The experiment cannot say whether the reading built the skill or whether people already good at other minds seek the books out.

Source. Maria Eugenia Panero, Deena Skolnick Weisberg, Jessica Black, Thalia R. Goldstein, Jennifer L. Barnes, Hiram Brownell, and Ellen Winner, "Does Reading a Single Passage of Literary Fiction Really Improve Theory of Mind? An Attempt at Replication," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2016). https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000064
