Are extraverts born or made? Kerry Jang and John Livesley of the University of British Columbia, with Philip Vernon, tested this with twins from the Vancouver area recruited through newspaper ads and media stories. They had 123 identical pairs and 127 non-identical pairs, aged 16 to 68, all raised together. Each twin filled in the 240-item NEO-PI-R questionnaire on their own, which scores the Big Five traits and six narrower facets within each.

For extraversion, identical twins correlated 0.55 and non-identical twins 0.23. Because identical twins were more than twice as alike, part of the genetic effect looked non-additive, meaning genes interacting rather than simply adding up. The best model put the genetic share of differences in extraversion at 0.53. The rest, 0.47, was environment that twins did not share. The other four traits came out at 0.41 for neuroticism, 0.61 for openness, 0.41 for agreeableness and 0.44 for conscientiousness.

The facets show the scale of heritability within one trait. Gregariousness, the liking for company, had a heritability of 0.52. Warmth was 0.43, assertiveness 0.42, excitement seeking 0.41, positive emotions 0.39, and activity, the pace of life, only 0.29. Elsewhere in the questionnaire, one openness facet, Feelings, could be explained by environment alone, so a figure near zero sits in the same test as figures above 0.5.

Just as striking, the shared family environment counted for almost nothing. It could be dropped from the model for every scale except two facets, Modesty and Self-Discipline. Twins raised in the same home were not more alike in extraversion than their genes predicted.

What this means in real life: whether you are outgoing or quiet is partly in your genes, about half of the differences in this sample, but the other half comes from experiences that even twins growing up together do not share. Parenting style seems to explain little of why one sibling is more outgoing than another.

Caution and replication: 250 volunteer pairs is a small sample, most pairs were women, and zygosity was decided by questionnaire rather than DNA. Later work supports the overall figure. A meta-analysis of six large twin cohorts with 29,501 twin pairs, reported by Stéphanie van den Berg and colleagues in 2016, put the broad heritability of extraversion at 49 percent, about half of it non-additive. But the same team then searched the DNA of 63,030 people of European ancestry and found no single variant that passed genome-wide significance, and common variants together explained an amount not significantly different from zero. Extraversion is highly polygenic, and its genes are still hard to find. van den Berg, S. M., de Moor, M. H. M., Verweij, K. J. H., et al. (2016).
