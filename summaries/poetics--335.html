What the notes are, and where the argument starts

Aristotle's Poetics, from the fourth century BC, survives as a short, damaged set of notes, probably for lectures, and it is still the most consequential argument in Western literary theory about what a tragedy is made of. The text we have is mainly about tragedy, with epic brought in for comparison. Comedy is promised and largely missing. The notes are practical in a way that surprises readers who expect philosophy to stay above the workshop. Aristotle is trying to say what a poet must construct, which parts are doing the real work, and why some plots produce pity and fear while others produce only shock, or only moral satisfaction, or nothing much at all. Folio's card takes one sentence of this argument as its title, that plot is the soul of tragedy. The sentence is near the centre. It is not the beginning, and it is not the whole.

The beginning is mimesis. Poetry, Aristotle argues, is a kind of imitation, and by imitation he means the representation of an action, not a mechanical copy of a surface. Human beings take a natural pleasure in imitation and learn by it. Poetry sorts itself by the means it uses, by the kinds of people it represents, and by the manner, narrative or dramatic, in which it represents them. Tragedy will be the imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude, presented in dramatic form rather than narrated, in language that is heightened in the ways appropriate to its parts, and so constructed that its incidents arouse pity and fear and bring about the catharsis of those emotions. Every term in that definition is a term the rest of the notes earn. The definition is the argument compressed, not a ceremonial opening.

Plot as the soul, and what that demotes

Tragedy, he says, has six parts: plot, character, thought, diction, song and spectacle. They are not equally important, and the ranking is the famous claim. Plot, the arrangement of the incidents, is the soul of tragedy. Character comes second. Thought, the reasoning and the arguments the persons of the play make, comes with character. Diction is the composition of the verses. Song is the lyric element. Spectacle, everything that depends on the staging and the eye, is the most effective with an audience and the least artistic, because it belongs to the resources of the theatre rather than to the poet's art. A tragedy should be able to do its work when it is read, or when its incidents are heard in order, without the costumes. That is how seriously he means the ranking. If the plot is right, the effect is already there. If the plot is wrong, performance cannot supply the missing soul.

The argument for the ranking is an argument about what tragedy imitates. It imitates an action, and an action is a structure of incidents, not a portrait of a personality and not a sequence of fine speeches. Character is what reveals choice, and choice matters because of the way it enters the action. A play that offered vivid people and no necessary connection among the things that happen would be, in his comparison, like a painting that put beautiful colours where the drawing of a body should be. The drawing is the plot. He pushes the claim far enough to say that a tragedy can do without character in a way it cannot do without plot, which is a theorist's exaggeration with a real point inside it. The point is that the unit of the art is the thing done, arranged so that it has to be done in this order.

Wholeness, probability, and the complex plot

A good plot is a whole, with a beginning, a middle and an end, of a length that can be held in memory and that is long enough for a change of fortune. Its unity is a unity of action, not the unity of a hero's life. Many things happen to one person that do not form one action, and a plot that included them would be a biography, not a tragedy. The incidents should follow one another by probability or necessity. One thing after another is not a plot. One thing because of another is. Aristotle's way of putting the poet's task is correspondingly severe. The poet's job is not to report what happened, which is the historian's job, but to represent what could happen according to probability or necessity. That is why he calls poetry more philosophical than history. History is stuck with Alcibiades and what Alcibiades did. Poetry can show the structure that makes a kind of action intelligible.

Within that discipline he distinguishes simple plots from complex ones. A complex plot contains peripeteia, reversal, or anagnorisis, recognition, or both. Reversal is a change in the action to its opposite, and it must arise from the structure of the incidents themselves, not from a coincidence pasted on. Recognition is a change from ignorance to knowledge, and it changes the relations of the people in the play, towards friendship or towards enmity. The best complex plots bind the two together, so that the moment of discovery is the moment the fortune turns. His example is Sophocles' Oedipus the King, the play the notes admire most. The messenger comes to relieve Oedipus of his fear and, by telling him who he is, produces the recognition that ruins him. Reversal and recognition coincide. The third element he adds to the discussion of plot is pathos, a destructive or painful action. Pity and fear have to have something to attach to, and the attachment is an action, not a mood in the audience waiting to be stirred by music.

Hamartia, the tragic hero, and catharsis

Not every change of fortune produces the tragic effect. Aristotle sorts the possibilities and throws most of them out. A thoroughly good person falling from happiness into misery shocks us. It does not arouse pity and fear in the sense he wants, because it feels like a pollution of the moral world. A bad person rising from misery to happiness satisfies neither pity nor fear nor our sense of what is fitting. A thorough villain falling may satisfy justice and still not produce pity, because pity is for undeserved misfortune, and it will not produce fear, because fear is for someone like ourselves. What remains is an intermediate figure, better than ordinary rather than perfect, whose misfortune comes through hamartia, an error, a great mistake, rather than through vice or depravity. Later readers have often translated hamartia as a flaw of character, and the word can lean that way. In the argument of the notes, the weight is on the error in the action, the mistake that a person of that stature can make and that probability can connect to the disaster. The hero is like us enough for fear, and better than we are enough for the fall to matter, and the fall is not a punishment fitted to a wicked life.

Catharsis is the term in the definition that the notes never unpack. Tragedy, through pity and fear, accomplishes the catharsis of such emotions. What that accomplishment is has been debated ever since. One reading hears a medical metaphor: the emotions are purged, discharged, so that the spectator leaves lighter. Another hears a ritual or moral metaphor: the emotions are purified. Another, attractive to readers who take the remarks about probability seriously, hears clarification: the spectator comes to see what pity and fear are properly about, because the plot has made a difficult action intelligible. The Poetics itself does not choose among these. A related discussion of catharsis and music in the Politics is one reason the medical reading has always had support. Folio's card is right to leave the term as a dispute rather than to award it to a single English word. What the argument does establish, without settling the word, is that pity and fear are the proper effects, that the plot produces them, and that effects produced by spectacle alone are cruder than effects produced by the arrangement of incidents.

The survival of the argument is easiest to see in the advice, repeated in modern manuals of screenwriting, that a plot should be built of therefore and but, not of and then. That advice is a popular descendant of the demand for probability or necessity, and of the demand that reversal grow out of the incidents. It is not a summary of the Poetics. The Poetics also wants a specific emotional end, a specific stature of hero, and a specific coincidence of recognition and reversal in the plots it calls best. A story can be causally tight and still fail every one of those further tests.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Aristotle's Poetics: plot is the soul of tragedy, is a close paraphrase of this argument, and the paraphrase is a good one. Mimesis, the six parts, the priority of plot, the whole with a beginning, middle and end, probability rather than mere sequence, peripeteia, anagnorisis, the messenger in Oedipus, hamartia as error or flaw, and catharsis as purgation, purification or clarification are all in the notes, in that order of dependence. The card also points at the lost book on comedy, the MacGuffin of Eco's The Name of the Rose, and at the fact that the text is short enough to read. What the card compresses, and what a reading of the notes restores, is the way each term answers a problem posed by the previous one. Plot is the soul because tragedy imitates action. Reversal and recognition are the best devices because a complex action must turn by its own logic. Hamartia is required because only a certain kind of fall yields pity and fear. Catharsis names the end those emotions are there to serve, and the notes leave the name darker than the rest of the machine. Later shapes of story, from Freytag's pyramid to the hero's journey, are arguments with this machine, even when they never mention it.
