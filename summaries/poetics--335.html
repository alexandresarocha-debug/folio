In Sophocles' Oedipus the King, a messenger comes to cheer Oedipus and to free him from his fear about his mother. The news does the opposite. By telling Oedipus who he is, the messenger brings on the recognition that ruins him. Aristotle, in the Poetics, uses that scene for what he means by a reversal. The action swings to its opposite, and the swing has to grow out of the events themselves, by probability or necessity, not from a shock pasted on at the end. The best plots, he says, tie that reversal to a recognition, a change from ignorance to knowledge. In Oedipus the two happen in the same moment. He gives a second case, the Lynceus: Lynceus is being led off to die, and Danaus goes with him meaning to kill him, but the earlier events end with Danaus dead and Lynceus saved.

From examples like these he ranks the parts of a tragedy. "The Plot, then, is the first principle, and, as it were, the soul of a tragedy: Character holds the second place." A tragedy imitates an action, not a personality. Character is what shows the choices people make, and those choices matter because of what they do inside the action. Beautiful colors laid on in a heap, he says, please less than a chalk outline of a person. The outline is the arrangement of incidents. Spectacle, everything aimed at the eye, "is the least artistic, and connected least with the art of poetry," because the power of a tragedy can be felt without actors, and the stage machinery belongs to the machinist more than to the poet.

The poet's job, on this account, is not to report what happened. That is the historian's job. The poet represents what may happen, according to probability or necessity. "Poetry, therefore, is a more philosophical and a higher thing than history," because poetry reaches toward the universal, "how a person of a certain type will on occasion speak or act," while history is stuck with particulars. His example of a particular is "what Alcibiades did or suffered." One thing after another is not yet a plot. One thing because of another is. If you can remove an event and the rest does not shift, he says, that event was never an organic part of the whole. The fall he thinks produces pity and fear is not a good person crushed, which only shocks, and not a villain punished, which may satisfy a sense of justice and still leave the audience cold. Pity is for undeserved misfortune. Fear is for someone enough like ourselves. What remains is a person "not eminently good and just," brought down "not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty." Oedipus is his instance.

His definition says that tragedy, through pity and fear, effects the catharsis of those emotions. The notes never say what catharsis means. Later readers have split over whether the word is a purging, a purification, or a clarification. The Poetics does not choose.
