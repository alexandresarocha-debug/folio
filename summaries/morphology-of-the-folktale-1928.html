A grammar, not a theme

Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale, published in Russian in 1928 and widely read in English only after the 1958 translation, asks a question that sounds modest and turns out to be severe. What is the unit of a fairy tale? Earlier collectors had classified tales by theme, by the kind of monster, or by the motif, a distinctive bit of business such as a magic ring or a wicked stepmother. Propp, working through a defined set of Russian wonder tales from Alexander Afanasyev's collection, argued that those units dissolve as soon as you look at what the tale is doing. A dragon, a witch, and a wicked tsar can perform the same act in the course of the story. The act is stable. The costume changes. He called the act a function, defined as an action considered from the point of view of its significance for the course of the tale. The morphology is the claim that a hundred tales are one structure of functions, appearing in a stable order, with not every tale containing every function, and with the characters understood as the roles that carry the functions out.

The decision to limit the corpus is part of the method, and it is the reason folklorists still set him beside Joseph Campbell. Campbell gathered myths from anywhere and read them as one psychic adventure. Propp stayed inside one genre, the magical tale, in one tradition, and he tried to describe all the moves that genre makes. He was not claiming a key to all narrative. He was claiming that, inside this shelf of tales, the sequence of actions is lawful in a way the choice of characters is not. A scientific description, on his view, had to start from that law rather than from a catalogue of colourful contents. The book is dry on purpose. Dryness is the alternative to retelling the tales as if their flavour were their structure.

Thirty-one functions, in an order

The functions begin at home. Someone is absent. An interdiction is announced, and the interdiction is violated. A villain tries to learn something, receives the information, and deceives the victim, who submits to the deception. Then comes the deed that sets the tale moving, either a villainy, a harm done, or a lack, something the family does not have and must obtain. The misfortune is made known, the hero agrees to act, and the hero departs. Away from home the hero is tested by a donor, responds to the test, and receives a magical agent. The hero is transferred to the place of the search. There may be a struggle with the villain, a mark left on the hero's body, a victory, and the liquidation of the original lack. The hero returns, is pursued, and is rescued from pursuit. Arriving unrecognised, the hero faces a false hero's claim, is set a difficult task, performs it, is recognised, and sees the false hero exposed. The hero is transfigured, the villain is punished, and the hero is married, often ascending to a throne.

Two features of this list are the theory. The first is sequence. Functions that appear, appear in this order. A tale may skip many of them. It does not, on Propp's account, freely reshuffle them, as if recognition could precede the departure that made recognition possible. He allows that real texts are messier than a schema, and he notes pairs and treblings, the habit of doing a thing three times, as devices inside the order rather than as rival orders. The second feature is the pairing of villainy and lack as alternative ways to open the quest. Some tales begin with a harm that must be undone. Some begin with a desire for something not yet possessed. The rest of the machine can serve either opening. That is why tales that feel different in their premises can share a skeleton. Propp also sees that a single text may contain more than one move, a complete run of functions, one quest closing and another beginning. The morphology is not a claim that every tale is one sitting of thirty-one steps. It is a claim that the steps, when they come, come as a grammar comes, in an order the teller can rely on and the audience can feel.

Seven spheres of action

Characters, in this account, are not psychological people. They are spheres of action. Propp names seven. The villain struggles against the hero and commits the harm. The donor tests the hero and provides the magical agent. The helper assists the hero on the journey, sometimes by being the magical agent in person. The princess, together with her father, is the sought person, the setter of difficult tasks, and the one who recognises and marries. The dispatcher sends the hero. The hero departs, reacts to the donor, and, if successful, is married. The false hero claims the achievement and is exposed. The stability of the tale is the stability of these spheres. A wolf can be a helper. A witch can be a donor in one episode and a villain in another. One person can fill two spheres, and one sphere can be divided between two people. What the tale cannot do, without becoming a different kind of tale, is leave a required action with nobody to perform it.

Propp draws a further distinction inside the hero's sphere that popular summaries often drop. There is a seeker hero, who sets out to repair someone else's lack, and a victim hero, who is seized or driven out and whose own misfortune is the thing to be liquidated. The functions gather differently around the two. The point of the distinction is the same as the point of the spheres. Looking at a tale and asking "what is the hero like?" is a later, novelistic question. The morphological question is "which actions does this figure carry, and which actions are carried by someone else?" Motivations, he notes, are far less stable than functions. Why a villain harms, or why a donor tests, can be rewritten from telling to telling. The harm and the test remain. Anyone building a theory of folk narrative on the inner lives of its people is, in Propp's view, building on the part of the tale that tellers treat as adjustable.

What the grammar does and does not claim

The influence of the book arrived late and then arrived everywhere. Structuralists, Claude Lévi-Strauss among them, took it as a landmark and argued with it, wanting a logic of oppositions as well as a sequence of events. A. J. Greimas reduced the spheres towards a smaller set of actants, pairs such as subject and object, helper and opponent. Researchers in computation have used the functions as a story grammar, because a sequence with optional steps and a fixed order is exactly the kind of object a generator can run. Each of these descendants keeps Propp's central bet. Narrative has a syntax. The bet's original scope is easy to lose once the descendants get ambitious. Propp was describing Russian wonder tales of a certain class. He was not describing the novel, the tragedy, or the mythologies Campbell would later fold into one hero. A wedding as the final function is a fact about this genre. It is not a fact about narrative as such.

Inside its scope, the bet still changes how a tale should be read. Two stories with different monsters and different landscapes can be the same tale morphologically, and two stories with the same monster can be different if the monster occupies a different sphere. The pleasure of a particular telling, its style, its local colour, its joke, is real and is not the object of the morphology. Propp is not a critic of beauty. He is a critic of the idea that beauty and theme are the only things to describe. Once the functions are visible, a teller's art can be seen as variation within a law, the way a speaker's art is variation within a language. That analogy was the gift he gave to later narratology. It is also the limit of the gift. A language can say things the grammar does not care about. So can a tale. The morphology tells you which of those things are the tale's skeleton and which are its flesh, and it tells you that, for this corpus, the skeleton is shared.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading treats Propp's thirty-one functions and seven roles as a grammar of the fairy tale, and it sets that grammar beside the hero's journey as the more empirical of the two. This book is the grammar. The functions run, when they appear, from absence and interdiction through villainy or lack, departure, the donor's test, the magical agent, struggle and victory, return and pursuit, the false hero, recognition, and the wedding. The roles are villain, donor, helper, princess and her father, dispatcher, hero, and false hero, stable as spheres while the creatures who fill them change. Campbell's monomyth is a psychological synthesis across cultures, with no counted corpus and with a talent for absorbing counterexamples. Propp is the alternative a folklorist means by empirical: one genre, one collection, a sequence stated sharply enough that a tale can fail to match it. The hero's journey card points here for that reason. Use Campbell as one powerful pattern. Use this morphology when you want to see what a pattern looks like when it has been held to a body of tales and forbidden to mean more than the tales support.
