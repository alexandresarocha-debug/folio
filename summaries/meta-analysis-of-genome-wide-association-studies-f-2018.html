Twin studies say personality is partly heritable. So where are the genes? Neuroticism, the tendency to feel worry, sadness and irritation, is the best-studied trait, and in 2018 Mats Nagel and colleagues ran one of the largest searches for it. They combined data from 449,484 people: 372,903 from the UK Biobank, 59,206 customers of 23andMe and 17,375 from the Genetics of Personality Consortium. Everyone filled in a neuroticism questionnaire.

The team scanned almost 15 million DNA variants. Before this study, only 16 genomic regions had been linked to neuroticism. Nagel and colleagues found 136 independent regions, 124 of them new, implicating 599 genes. The signal pointed to the brain, including dopamine-making cells, a type of neuron called medium spiny neurons, and serotonin-making neurons, and to pathways such as the growth of new neurons.

Two details answer the question of how small DNA differences can shape a person. First, almost none of the strongest signals change a protein. Of 170 lead variants, only 4 sat in the parts of genes that code for protein, while 88 were inside introns, the non-coding stretches within genes, and 52 lay between genes. Of 17,794 variants closely linked to them, just 0.9 percent were in coding regions. Most of the action seems to be in switches that change how much a gene is used. Second, each variant has a tiny effect. Personality is polygenic: thousands of variants each nudge it a little, and no single gene for anxiety or worry exists.

The study also measured how much all the common variants explain together. The answer, called SNP-based heritability, was 0.10, about 10 percent of the differences in neuroticism. That is well below the 40 to 47 percent from twin studies. Part of the gap may come from rare variants that such scans miss, and part from limits of the twin method.

What this means in real life: a DNA test cannot read your personality. Even with half a million people, the measured genetics explain a small share of the differences, and the environment, chance and life experiences explain most of the rest.

Caution and replication: this kind of study is a reaction to an earlier failure. Researchers had long studied a few candidate genes picked for their biology, such as the serotonin transporter variant 5-HTTLPR, often reporting large effects in samples far smaller than today's. In 2019 Richard Border and colleagues tested 18 much-studied candidate genes for depression in samples of 62,138 to 443,264 people and found no clear evidence for any of them. They concluded that most of those reported associations were likely false positives. Genome-wide studies with huge samples are the more reliable approach, though their results also depend on how the trait was measured.
