What fiction is for, on their account

In 2008 Raymond A. Mar and Keith Oatley published an essay in Perspectives on Psychological Science with a title that is already a theory: The Function of Fiction is the Abstraction and Simulation of Social Experience. They are psychologists writing towards both their own field and the humanities. The question is functional. Human beings spend a remarkable amount of time in stories that they know to be invented. Mar and Oatley ask what that time is doing. Their answer has two linked parts. Fiction simulates social experience, and it abstracts social experience. Simulation is the running of a social world in the mind. Abstraction is the stripping of a particular tale down to patterns that can travel to a new case.

The analogy they make famous is the flight simulator. A pilot practises situations in a machine so that the first real emergency is not the first time the hands have moved. A reader, on their account, practises situations in a story so that other minds, other bargains, other betrayals, are met at least once before they are met in a room where the cost is real. The analogy is an analogy. A novel does not certify you to do anything. What it shares with the simulator is offline practice: consequences that feel vivid and remain, in the actual world, unincurred. Mar and Oatley think the content of the practice is social. Plots run on agents, goals, beliefs, and emotions. Even a story about a journey or a storm becomes fiction of the kind they mean when human motives organise the events.

Abstraction, and the model inside the particular

Simulation alone would be a theory of practice. Abstraction is their theory of transfer. A story about one household's jealousy is thick with detail that will never recur. If fiction only stored details, its lessons would be trapped inside their original furniture. Mar and Oatley argue that readers also extract a model: a simplified structure of agents and motives that can be recognised when the furniture changes. The model is what makes a tale useful in a life that will not repeat the tale. They connect this to a broader idea of fiction as a kind of explanation, closer to a worked example in science than to a list of true statements about the world. The truth on offer is truth about how a social pattern goes, carried by people who never existed.

This is why they spend time on the difference between fiction and expository prose. An essay can tell you that jealousy distorts perception. A story can put you inside the distortion for the length of a scene, and then let you recognise the shape of it later. The essay's advantage is explicitness and checkability. The story's advantage, on their account, is the run of the simulation and the memory of having run it. They are interested in transportation, the absorbed state in which a reader temporarily privileges the story's world, because absorption is the condition under which the simulation feels like experience. They are interested in identification for the same reason. A reader who takes a character's goals as temporarily her own is a reader doing the practice, in the first person.

The evidence they could point to in 2008

The paper is a theory with a review attached, written before Kidd and Castano's one-sitting experiments and before the replications that complicated them. The empirical literature Mar and Oatley lean on is mostly correlational. People who read more fiction, measured in ways that try to avoid simple boasting, tend to score higher on tests of empathy and theory of mind. Work using author-recognition methods, including Mar's own, is part of that picture. So are studies of how readers build models of characters' minds as they go. The authors treat the correlations as suggestive of a function, and they are plain that a correlation does not name a cause. People who are already socially attentive may seek fiction. Verbal ability may lift both reading and test scores. A taste for stories may be a companion of the skill the tests measure, sharing a cause the researchers have not isolated.

They also draw on the cognitive psychology of narrative: how readers track goals, how they update a situation model, how emotion in a text is emotion understood as someone else's state. None of this requires the strong claim that a single short passage will raise a laboratory score this afternoon. The function they propose would show itself across many stories, as a gradual expertise in social patterns, the way many hours in a simulator show themselves in a pilot who has not yet flown the emergency. A paper written in that key can survive later news that one clever afternoon experiment was fragile. The function claim was never a claim about an afternoon.

Where the theory is strong, and where it waits

The strength of the paper is the clarity of the two terms. Abstraction stops the theory from collapsing into the idea that readers merely collect facts about fictional people. Simulation stops it from collapsing into the idea that readers merely extract morals and throw the story away. Together they describe a double product: a felt run-through, and a portable pattern. The account also gives critics a way to talk about why some fiction might exercise social cognition more than others, without pretending that exercise is the only reason to read. A comedy of manners, a detective story built on hidden motives, a modernist novel of many partial minds: these are different simulators, and the theory can say so. It does not need every book to be a solemn lesson in kindness.

The place the theory waits is the place the later experiments rushed to fill. If simulation is a real cause of better social understanding, assigned reading should sometimes move the measures, under the right dose and the right test. Mar and Oatley offer reasons to expect a movement and reasons to expect it to be entangled with everything else a person brings to a book. The decade after their paper tried the movement in short form, announced a success, failed to reproduce the success, and then averaged the attempts into a small effect. That history fits a theory of gradual expertise better than it fits a theory of sudden transformation. It also leaves the original correlations unexplained. A function can be true of a species and still hard to catch in a room.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on fiction and empathy begins with this idea, the flight simulator for social life, before it tells the story of the 2013 experiment and the 2016 failure to confirm it. The order is the responsible one. Mar and Oatley supply the reason anyone ran those experiments. They also supply the scale at which the reason still makes sense after the experiments cooled: a reading life as practice and as model-building, with cause still entangled with the kind of person who chooses the practice. Read them when the headlines have made the topic seem like a yes-or-no about a single passage. Their paper is the argument that the interesting unit was never the passage. It was the capacity of stories to let us run other people's situations, and to keep the pattern when the story is over.
