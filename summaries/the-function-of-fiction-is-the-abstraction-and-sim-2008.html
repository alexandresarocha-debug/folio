The evidence Raymond A. Mar and Keith Oatley put next to their theory is a 2006 study of 94 people at the University of Toronto, ages 17 to 57. Each person checked off authors they recognized, a way of measuring how much fiction and how much nonfiction they had actually met, and then took tests of social ability. Once age, years of English, intelligence, nonfiction reading, and a habit of ticking fake names were held constant, people who knew more fiction authors did better on the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test. The matching slice of nonfiction reading pointed the other way. Mar and Oatley treat that pattern as a sign that fiction and the ability to read other people travel together.

Their account of why is a simulation. A reader can feel real fear when a killer comes through a door on the page, though nothing in the room has changed. Stories, they argue, compress social life the way a model compresses a system. Fewer parts remain, the important causes are left in, and the reader runs another person's intentions to see what follows. They also note that people readily see chasing and fighting in a film of simple circles and triangles, which is their illustration of how little it takes to start attributing minds.

The useful difference in daily life is between an org chart and a scene. The chart lists roles. The scene lets you practice the guess about what two people want. The 2006 result is a correlation. It can mean that reading builds the skill, or that people who are already good at other minds seek fiction out, or both. Mar and Oatley say the relation is not explained away by personality, citing a further analysis, and they leave the causal direction open.

Later experiments narrowed it. Panero and colleagues in 2016 found no immediate gain on the eyes test from one literary passage, in 792 people. Dodell-Feder and Tamir in 2018 put the average experimental advantage of fiction at g = .15. The short sitting does not carry the claim. What Mar and Oatley actually had in hand was a lifetime association.
