In the third chapter of his history, Edward Gibbon named what he thought was the best time to be alive. "If a man were called to fix the period in the history of the world, during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous, he would, without hesitation, name that which elapsed from the death of Domitian to the accession of Commodus." That is AD 96 to AD 180, the reigns of Nerva, Trajan, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, and Marcus Aurelius. The first volume, which carries the story from that height into the troubles that followed, was published in London by William Strahan and Thomas Cadell on 17 February 1776.

The same volume ends with a second, more explosive piece of work. In chapters 15 and 16 Gibbon set providence aside and explained the spread of Christianity by five "secondary causes": an exclusive zeal, the promise of a life after death, a claim to miracles, a strict moral code, and the church's discipline and care for its own people. He also argued that the persecutions were patchier, and the martyrs fewer, than church tradition held. Readers treated the irony as an attack. He kept going. Volumes 2 and 3 appeared on 1 March 1781. At the close of chapter 38, finishing the western story, he wrote, "I have described the triumph of barbarism and religion." The last three volumes, published together on 8 May 1788, follow the eastern Roman state until Ottoman troops took Constantinople on 29 May 1453.

Along the way he tells the defeats in plain political terms. The emperor Valens was killed at Adrianople on 9 August 378. Alaric's Goths sacked Rome for three days in August 410. In 476 the western imperial title lapsed. Gibbon presents each of these as a real loss of soldiers, tax, or office, inside a world that still had cities, laws, and, in the east, an emperor. The practical question his dates force on anyone retelling the fall is which thing is supposed to have ended: a mood of civic confidence after 180, a western court in 476, or a Roman state that was still collecting taxes in 1453.

The famous sentence does not survive that question cleanly. The eastern empire was at least as Christian as the western one, and Gibbon's own last volumes keep a Roman emperor on the throne for another thousand years. Later historians have kept his narrative and dropped his moral causes. The dates and the battles remain the part a reader can check.
