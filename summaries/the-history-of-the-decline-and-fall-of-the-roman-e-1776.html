The happiest age, and the story that follows

Edward Gibbon's The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire is a narrative of how a political order that he regarded as unusually successful came apart, and of what replaced it. The first volume appeared in 1776. The remaining volumes followed through the 1780s, and the work was complete by 1789. It begins in the second century, under the Antonine emperors, and it ends with the Ottoman capture of Constantinople in 1453. The scale is the point. Gibbon was not writing a monograph on one reign or one battle. He was trying to hold in one story the principate, the third-century breakdown, the Christian empire, the end of imperial rule in the west, the long survival of the eastern Roman state, the rise of Islam, the crusades, and the last siege.

The opening claim is comparative and moral as well as political. In the age of the Antonines, Gibbon thought, the Roman world combined extent, peace, and a tolerable government more successfully than any society he knew. The provinces were protected by a disciplined army. The principate preserved the forms of the old republic while concentrating power in one man. Succession by adoption, rather than by blood, had for a time produced able rulers. Laws were harsh by later standards, and slavery was normal, but within the limits of an ancient empire the second century looked to him like a high plateau of human affairs. Everything after that plateau is, in his telling, a descent, interrupted by recoveries that never quite restore the original balance.

How the imperial body failed

The narrative of decay begins in earnest with Commodus and runs through the Severan dynasty into the third-century crisis. Gibbon treats the Severan settlement as a bargain with the soldiers at the expense of civilians. The army is paid, privileged, and increasingly recruited from the frontiers and from beyond them. Emperors are made and unmade by camps. In the middle of the third century the story becomes a catalogue of short reigns, usurpations, Persian attacks under the new Sasanian dynasty, and raids across the European rivers. The silver coinage is debased. Provinces throw up their own emperors because the centre cannot protect them. Gibbon's point is not that any one of these facts was unprecedented. It is that they arrived together, and that the political culture which should have corrected them had already narrowed into the will of the army.

He is at his most interested when a recovery interrupts the decline, because a recovery lets him say what he thinks the empire still was. Aurelian's reunification, Diocletian's reconstruction, and Constantine's new capital are not dismissed as illusions. They are presented as the work of able autocrats who stabilised a damaged state by making it more bureaucratic, more ceremonial, and more expensive. Gibbon can admire the energy and still think the cure changed the patient. The later empire, in his account, purchased survival by abandoning the civic habits of the earlier one. Senators became courtiers. Cities remained splendid and became less free. The division of rule, begun as a practical answer to distance and war, hardened into a standing distinction between east and west.

Christianity as a social fact

Chapters fifteen and sixteen of the first volume made Gibbon notorious. They ask how a small Jewish sect became the religion of the empire, and they answer in the register of a philosopher rather than of a believer. Gibbon sets aside what a Christian would call the primary cause, the truth of the revelation, and examines secondary causes: an uncompromising zeal inherited from Judaism but detached from its ethnic boundary, the promise of a future life, the claim to miraculous powers, a severe moral code, and the church's own discipline and mutual aid. He then turns to the Roman persecutions and argues that they were less constant, and the number of martyrs smaller, than ecclesiastical tradition held. The tone is cool. Sacred history is treated as a social movement that can be explained with the same tools as any other.

The offence was not a single insult. It was the method. Gibbon wrote as an Enlightenment ironist who had passed through a youthful conversion to Catholicism and a later, settled scepticism. He respected some Christian virtues and some Christian leaders. He did not respect what he took to be the redirection of loyalty from the city to the church, from public office to the monastery, and from the defence of the frontier to arguments about the nature of Christ. In the later narrative, theological controversy, the wealth of the clergy, and the growth of monasticism appear as drains on the talent and the treasure of the state. Bishops become political figures. Emperors spend political capital on councils. A portion of the population that might have served, governed, or paid tax is, in his view, occupied with another world.

This is the part of the book that later historians have been least willing to accept as an explanation of collapse, and Gibbon's own later volumes make the difficulty obvious. The eastern empire was at least as Christian as the western one, and it survived. If Christianity were a sufficient poison, Constantinople should have died with Ravenna. Gibbon's answer, spread across the later books rather than stated as a clean rebuttal, is that Christianity acted together with other weaknesses, and that the east had advantages of money, walls, and diplomacy which the west lost. That answer keeps the religious thesis alive only by making it one strand among several. Readers who quote him as if he had proved that the church destroyed Rome are reading the scandal of chapter fifteen and skipping the structure of the whole work.

The western fall and the long eastern afterword

The middle of the history follows the fourth and fifth centuries in the west. Julian's attempt to restore the old cults fails. The Gothic war culminates in the disaster at Adrianople, where the emperor Valens is killed. Later Gothic, Vandal, and Hunnic wars, Alaric's sack of Rome, the loss of Africa, and the deposition of the last western emperor are told as the dismemberment of a state that could still win campaigns and could no longer keep a stable centre. Gibbon does not treat 410 as the end of the physical city, and he does not treat 476 as the end of Roman civilisation. He does treat the loss of the western imperial office, and of the tax base that supported a Roman army, as a real political ending. His own retrospective phrase for that ending is the triumph of barbarism and religion. The phrase is a summary, written after the western narrative, not a substitute for it. Barbarism, in his usage, means both the armed peoples who entered the empire and the barbarisation of the Roman army itself. Religion means the Christian transformation he had already analysed.

He then refuses to stop. The later volumes are a history of the eastern Roman empire and of the powers around it, down to 1453. Justinian's codification of Roman law is described as a lasting monument even where Gibbon dislikes the reign. The wars of reconquest, the great plague, the rise of Islam, the loss of Syria, Egypt, and Africa, the controversy over icons, the crusades, the Fourth Crusade's capture of Constantinople, and the final Ottoman siege are all given room. The judgement on this eastern millennium is severe. Gibbon often writes as if Byzantine politics were a repetitive story of palace intrigue, theological fury, and military decline, a long afterlife rather than a second classical age. He can pause to admire a general, a legal achievement, or a moment of courage, and then return to a tone of distaste. The east survives in his pages, but it survives under a moral cloud imported from his account of the west.

What the evidence can and cannot show

The limits are as important as the learning. Gibbon had almost none of the evidence that later changed the debate. He did not have a pottery chronology, a coin-loss map, an ice core, or a tree-ring reconstruction. He inferred the condition of ordinary people from laws, anecdotes, and the complaints of elites. His causal language slides between political analysis and moral diagnosis. Luxury, effeminacy, and the loss of virtue are asked to do more explanatory work than a modern economic historian will allow. His account of Christianity is an argument about incentives and institutions, but it is also a polemic against enthusiasm, and the polemic sometimes outruns the evidence. His portrait of the eastern empire inherits the contempt of several of his sources and of the Enlightenment authors who had already decided that Byzantium was a byword for decline. A reader can learn from the architecture of the narrative, the attention to succession and civil war, and the refusal to stop in 476, and still reject the moral that barbarism and religion are the names of the cause.

Why it matters here

Folio's card gibbon-decline takes its title from that famous retrospective verdict and uses the book as the starting point of a much longer argument about Rome. The card's claim is not that Gibbon was right in every cause he named. It is that he set the terms: a long decline, an internal weakening, a final external blow, and a suspicion of Christianity that later scholars have had to answer. The same card points onward to Peter Brown's picture of late antiquity as transformation and to Bryan Ward-Perkins's insistence that the material collapse in the west was real. Those replies are only intelligible if Gibbon's own shape is clear. He is the writer who made barbarism and religion into a sentence. He is also the writer who followed the eastern empire to 1453 and thereby left the door open for every later argument about what, exactly, fell, and what went on.
