Edward Gibbon finished the writing of The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire at Lausanne on 27 June 1787, and the sixth volume came out in 1788. The first had appeared in 1776. In the last chapter he looks at the ruins of the city and says that in the preceding volumes he has described "the triumph of barbarism and religion." He then asks how much of the physical damage either party actually did. The Gothic sack of 410 and the Vandal sack of 455, on his account, were hunts for portable loot. The soldiers left within days. They had neither the leisure nor, he thought, a plan to take Rome apart stone by stone. He dated the whole project to an afternoon in his own life. In his memoirs he says the idea came to him on 15 October 1764, while he sat among the ruins of the Capitol.

He turns the same question on the Christians and then limits it. Temples of the old gods were an abomination to them, and some were demolished. Ordinary civic buildings, he writes, could stand without scandal. The Pantheon survived because it was converted into a church. The change of religion, in this chapter, came by decree of emperors and of the senate, and by time, rather than by a crowd pulling the city down in an afternoon. Behind that scene sits the claim the earlier volumes had already made. From the age of the Antonines, which he called the happiest period in human history, civic habits weakened, the army made and unmade emperors, and talent and money moved toward the church. Barbarian armies, in his telling, struck a state that had already lost its strength.

That choice of cause still decides what a person looks for. A moral story sends you to sermons, luxury, and laws about monks. A different story can grant Gibbon his reading of the texts and still ask for other evidence. The eastern half of the empire was at least as Christian as the west, and it lasted until the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople on 29 May 1453, which Gibbon himself narrated. Peter Brown's The World of Late Antiquity (1971) treated these centuries as a religious transformation that built new institutions. Bryan Ward-Perkins, in 2005, set the western loss of ordinary pottery, tile, and small change beside that cultural story. The prose is why the book is still read. The diagnosis has to survive an east that stayed Christian, and a west whose household goods changed whether or not anyone's virtue did.
