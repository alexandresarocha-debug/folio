A bias with a market attached

In 1989 Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber published The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings: An Experimental Analysis, in the Journal of Political Economy. The phrase they put into circulation is now used by psychologists and by writing teachers, often at a distance from the experiment that justified it. The paper's own question is an economist's question. Standard analyses of asymmetric information assume that a better-informed person can still figure out what a worse-informed person will think. If I know something you do not, and I need to predict your prediction, I should be able to set my extra knowledge aside and use only what we share. Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber argue that this assumption fails in a systematic way. Better-informed people cannot ignore their private information, even when ignoring it would make them better off. More information is then a disadvantage. That is the curse.

They are explicit about the normative rule being broken. If your information includes mine, then your best guess about my best guess should be the same as my best guess. The law of iterated expectations says so. A curse-of-knowledge error is a pull away from that law, in a particular direction: the informed person's estimate of the uninformed person's estimate is dragged towards the truth that only the informed person has seen. You know the answer, so you think others are closer to the answer than they are. The paper's contribution is to show that this drag survives in settings economists respect, settings with incentives, feedback, and a market, where a defender of rational models might have expected the drag to disappear.

Earnings, and a dividend that is someone else's guess

The design has two stages, and the second stage is what makes the curse costly. In the first, students predicted the earnings of real companies from the sort of report an investor might see, together with a run of past earnings. Those predictions are the uninformed judgements, uninformed in the precise sense that the predictors did not know how the year in question had actually come out. In the second stage, other people were told the actual earnings and then asked to trade an asset whose payoff depended on the first group's predictions. The traders knew the truth. The money depended on the earlier guesses. A trader who could set the truth aside and reconstruct the guess would price the asset well. A trader cursed by what he knew would price the asset too close to the actual earnings, and too far from the forecast the less-informed group had really made.

The paper compares judgements made by individuals with prices produced in these markets. Feedback, the chance to learn from outcomes, does little in their results. Market forces do more. The authors' own summary is that the market reduces the curse by about half and does not eliminate it. That sentence is the result to remember, because it answers the hope that competition will wash a bias out. Competition, in this experiment, shrinks the bias and leaves a bias. Informed traders still behave as if the uninformed know more than the uninformed know. The curse is smaller when money and other traders are in the room, and it is still there, which means a model that assumes it away will mis-describe even a market built to punish it.

Why economists should mind, on their argument

The discussion draws consequences for situations in which one side knows more and has to anticipate the other side's beliefs. Bargaining, the behaviour of firms that hold private information, the relation between a principal and an agent, and choices under uncertainty all assume, in standard versions, that informed parties can simulate uninformed ones. If the simulation is cursed, offers will be mis-set, regulations will be mis-guessed, and a private signal will leak into a public price beyond what the holder of the signal intended. The paper is an experimental analysis with a theoretical temper. It wants the curse added to the list of ways real judgement departs from the textbook agent, alongside other departures that experimental economics was documenting in the same years. It also wants a specific shape of departure, a directional one, so that the error can be modelled and looked for in data.

A reader from outside economics can follow the logic without the models. Information has a cost that the usual celebration of information forgets. Knowing more can make you worse at a task whose correct performance requires you to inhabit a mind that knows less. The task is common. Anyone who explains, sells, teaches, negotiates, or writes is in the position of the second-stage trader. The payoff, being understood, depends on a forecast of a less-informed mind. The private knowledge that makes you an expert is the knowledge you must, for that forecast, decline to use. Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber show that declining is hard even when the payoff is literal money and the truth is a number on a page. A number is the easiest kind of truth to set aside, and their traders still could not do it cleanly.

From the trading floor to the paragraph

Nothing in the 1989 paper is about prose. The bridge is later, and it is conceptual. Steven Pinker takes the name and the mechanism into The Sense of Style because a specialist writing for a general reader is an informed trader whose asset pays off only if he can reconstruct the reader's ignorance. Jargon, missing examples, and abbreviations are the literary form of a price pulled towards the insider's truth. The tapping study people often pair with the curse, in which knockers of a tune wildly overestimate a listener's chance of naming the song, is a cleaner classroom picture of the same structure. Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber supply something the classroom picture does not: evidence that incentives and markets, the forces supposed to train the error out of professionals, reduce it and leave it standing.

That remainder is the sober part. A writer who has been told about the curse is in the position of a trader who has been told about the curse. Information about the bias is itself information, and it does not automatically install the ability to simulate a novice. The paper's market result is a warning against the fantasy that a sufficiently sharp environment cures the problem. It also suggests why practice and feedback have to be of a special kind. Feedback that tells you the right earnings again will only feed the curse. Feedback that tells you what the uninformed actually guessed, or what an actual reader actually failed to understand, is the kind that can teach. Their first stage, the collection of real uninformed predictions, is the expensive and necessary half of the design. Without it, the informed have nothing true to aim at but the truth they already know.

Why it matters here

The card on the curse of knowledge and classic style uses this paper for the origin of the term and for the finding that matters: people who know more cannot reliably ignore what they know when their job is to predict the less informed, and a market cuts the error roughly in half without ending it. Pinker's writing advice is a translation of that finding into sentences. Thomas and Turner's classic style is a stance that tries to defeat the curse by making the writer point at a shareable world. Read the 1989 experiment when the writing advice starts to sound like a matter of taste. The curse began as a priced mistake in a controlled market, directional, stubborn, and reduced by competition. The paragraph you cannot see is unclear to others is the same mistake, with a reader in the role of the first-stage forecaster and your expertise in the role of the number you were told.
