Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber paid 51 Wharton students to estimate 1980 earnings for eight real companies. Each student had a Value Line report, a short description of the business, and the earnings figures through 1979, and earned $1 for every guess within 10 percent of the actual number. Two months later a second group of Wharton students, trading in groups of nine, saw the same reports with the actual 1980 earnings filled in. They bought and sold assets that paid a dividend equal to the first group's average guess. To price an asset correctly they had to ignore the true earnings and predict what the less-informed students had predicted. Prices and judgments landed between those two numbers. They could not set the extra fact aside.

Individual judges showed the same pull, and being told the right answer afterward did not fix it. University of Chicago MBA students earned $1 for a guess within 10 percent of the uninformed group's average. Nineteen of them got incentives only. Thirteen also got feedback after each company. The two groups were about equally biased. Trading cut the bias by about half and did not remove it. The drop was significant at the 5 percent level for three of the eight companies. Markets in the experiment averaged 12.8 trades a period, 5.8 of them short sales, so a less biased trader had a way to bet against the error. The error shrank and stayed.

This is the specialist who cannot brief a newcomer, the physician who forgets which result the patient has not been told, the manager who prices a product as if the buyer had seen the spreadsheet. More information made these people worse at the single judgment they were paid to make, which was the judgment of someone who knew less. A market with money on the line reduced the mistake. It did not turn experts back into accurate readers of the uninformed.

Source. Colin Camerer, George Loewenstein, and Martin Weber, "The Curse of Knowledge in Economic Settings: An Experimental Analysis," Journal of Political Economy 97, no. 5 (1989): 1232–1254. https://doi.org/10.1086/261651
