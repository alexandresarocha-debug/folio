Dell's mita paper is one of dozens that ask whether colonial rule still shows up in today's economies. In April 2025 three economists at the University of Warwick, James Fenske, Bishnupriya Gupta and Anwesh Mukhopadhyay, published a review of this literature, written for a handbook on the economic history of colonialism. It is useful here because it puts the mita result in context and says plainly where such studies can go wrong.

They begin with the classic claim by Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson in 2001 that places where early European settlers died less often ended up with better institutions. Later work moved beyond property rights. They describe, for example, a finding that Indian districts under indirect British rule, as princely states, have had better access to schools, health facilities and roads since independence, and a finding that the more slaves were shipped from an African country between 1400 and 1900, the poorer it is today. Dell's study is one of three examples they single out. It compares districts on either side of the mita border, which helps weed out other local differences such as geography.

The review then lists four standard criticisms of persistence studies. The first is compression of history, for example regressing development in 1995 on population density in 1500, without looking at what happened in between. The second is spatial correlation, meaning neighbouring places resemble each other for reasons that can fool the statistics. The third is data quality, and the fourth is how to interpret the size of coefficients. The authors praise Dell for tracing the mita's effects through intermediate points, such as haciendas in 1689, literacy in 1876, schooling in 1940, land inequality in 1994 and schooling in 2001.

They also stress that colonial effects do not always last. Many colonial policies failed to persist or were reversed. The review notes that the population effects of the mita and of another colonial labour institution in Peru were eroded by migration before independence, and that some effects dissipate where later policies equalise access to public goods.

What this means in real life: a good persistence claim names a mechanism, shows the steps between then and now, and says where it stops. Be most careful with claims that skip from a distant date to a modern number.

Note that this is a working paper, a review of other people's studies, and not a new experiment.

Source. Fenske, J., Gupta, B., and Mukhopadhyay, A. (2025). Colonial Persistence. Warwick Economics Research Papers No. 1557 (also CAGE Discussion Paper 752). Prepared for The Routledge Handbook of the Economic History of Colonialism.
