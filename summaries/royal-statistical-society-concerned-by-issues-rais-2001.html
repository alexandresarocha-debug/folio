In October 2001, while Sally Clark was in prison and her first appeal had failed, the Royal Statistical Society took the unusual step of issuing a public statement about a criminal case. It was released on 23 October 2001 and expressed the Society's concern at the misuse of statistics in the courts.

The statement describes what happened. A medical expert witness drew on published studies to obtain a figure for the frequency of sudden infant death syndrome, or cot death, in families with some of the characteristics of the defendant's family. He then squared this figure to obtain a value of 1 in 73 million for two cases in such a family.

This approach, the Society said, is in general statistically invalid. It would only be valid if cot deaths arose independently within families, and there are very strong reasons to suppose they do not: unknown genetic or environmental factors may predispose some families, so that a second case becomes much more likely. The well-publicised figure of 1 in 73 million thus has no statistical basis. It cannot be defended as a ballpark figure, because the error is likely to be very large and in one particular direction.

The statement then named a second error. Some press reports had described the figure as the chance that the deaths were accidental. That is a serious error of logic known as the prosecutor's fallacy. The jury has to weigh two competing explanations, cot death or murder. Two cot deaths and two murders are each quite unlikely, but one of them has apparently happened. What matters is the relative likelihood of the deaths under each explanation, not just how unlikely they are under one.

The Society noted that the Court of Appeal had already issued guidance on probabilities in DNA evidence, and urged the courts to make sure statistical evidence is presented only by appropriately qualified statistical experts.

What this means in real life: a small probability of the evidence under one explanation is not the probability that the explanation is false.

Caution: the statement addresses the statistics only. It did not, and could not, decide the medical evidence in the case.
