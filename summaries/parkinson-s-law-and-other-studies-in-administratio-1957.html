Parkinson's Law began as an essay in The Economist in November 1955, which put forward the famous line that work expands to fill the time available for its completion. Cyril Northcote Parkinson, a British historian who was teaching in Malaya at the time, then collected essays in a book. It was published in Boston in 1957 and in London in 1958 as Parkinson's Law, or the Pursuit of Progress. The tone is deadpan and the book is satire. Nothing in it was measured, and the numbers are made up for the joke.

The part everyone remembers is in the third chapter, titled High Finance, or the Point of Vanishing Interest. Parkinson describes a finance committee with three items on the agenda. The first is a 10 million pound contract to build an atomic reactor. The second is a 350 pound bicycle shed for the clerical staff. The third is 21 pounds a year for refreshments for a welfare committee.

The reactor goes through in about two and a half minutes. It is too large and too technical for most members, so they assume that those who propose it know what they are doing. One member does raise a concern but cannot explain it to the others. The shed is another matter. Mr Softleigh says an aluminium roof is too dear and suggests asbestos. Mr Holdfast wants galvanised iron. Mr Daring asks whether the shed is needed at all. Parkinson writes that a sum of 350 pounds is within everybody's comprehension, and that everyone can visualise a bicycle shed. The talk goes on for forty-five minutes, with the possible result of saving some 50 pounds, and members sit back with a feeling of accomplishment.

The coffee is the best part. Some members may not tell asbestos from galvanised iron, but every man there knows about coffee. This item occupies them for an hour and a quarter, and they end by asking the secretary to find more information and leaving it to the next meeting.

What this means in real life: the pattern is easy to recognise in any group, from a company board to a neighbourhood committee. Simple fixes follow from it. Put the biggest decision first, and prepare a short briefing so that people can follow it. Give each item a time limit that matches the stakes. Let one person decide the small things.

A caution: this is satire. It describes a pattern that readers recognise, not a result that anyone measured. The modern evidence on group discussion, covered in the other two works, supports one ingredient of it.

Source. Parkinson, C. N. (1957). Parkinson's Law, and Other Studies in Administration. Boston: Houghton Mifflin. (UK edition: Parkinson's Law, or the Pursuit of Progress, London: John Murray, 1958.)
