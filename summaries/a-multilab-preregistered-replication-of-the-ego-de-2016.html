In 2016 Martin Hagger, Nikos Chatzisarantis, and labs on several continents published a registered test of ego depletion. Twenty-three laboratories and 2,141 people ran the same computer protocol. Roy Baumeister had recommended the tasks, which came from a 2014 study by Chandra Sripada, Daniel Kessler, and John Jonides. First came a letter-e task of 7 minutes and 30 seconds, after a short practice. In the hard version, people pressed a key when a word contained an e, and they had to withhold the press if that e sat next to a vowel or one letter away from one. In the easy version they pressed for every e. Then everyone did a multi-source interference task: three digits appeared, and the job was to press the key for the digit that differed from the other two, ignoring where it sat. The main measure was how much reaction times varied on the hard trials of that second task.

The hard letter task was genuinely harder. Accuracy fell by a large amount, d = −1.82. People rated it more difficult, d = 1.91, more effortful, d = 0.86, and more frustrating, d = 0.82. Fatigue ratings barely moved, d = 0.09, and that interval included zero. On the second task the depletion effect was d = 0.04 for reaction-time variability, with a 95 percent confidence interval from −0.07 to 0.15. Average reaction time gave the same d = 0.04. In three of the 23 labs the interval for the main effect excluded zero, and one of those three effects pointed in the wrong direction. An earlier meta-analysis by Hagger and colleagues, limited to published studies, had put the average effect near d = 0.62 across 198 tests.

The registered analysis, fixed before the data came in, did not find that effect. A hard bout of self-control felt hard, and the next task did not suffer for it. For a workday, a diet, or a difficult conversation, the feeling of effort is real and this experiment measured it. That feeling is weak evidence that a willpower tank has run dry and that the next hard task must go worse. A 2021 multisite test led by Kathleen Vohs, with 3,531 people and a preregistered analysis, likewise came back at d = 0.06, again indistinguishable from nothing.
