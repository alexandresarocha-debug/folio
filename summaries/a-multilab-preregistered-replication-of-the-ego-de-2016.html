Background

Ego depletion had, by the middle of the 2010s, the awkward status of a famous effect with a fraying literature. Baumeister and colleagues had proposed in 1998 that self-control draws on a limited resource, and a great many small studies had reported that a first act of control impairs a second. Meta-analyses that corrected for the tendency to publish successes and file away failures were finding much less. Martin Hagger and a large collaboration answered with the instrument the crisis had just made standard: a preregistered replication, run the same way in many laboratories, with the analysis fixed before the data arrived. Their 2016 paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science is that replication. Folio's card treats it as the moment the effect, under a shared protocol, came out very close to zero.

The scale is part of the argument. The card describes 23 laboratories and more than 2,000 participants. A null in a single room can be a clumsy afternoon. A null under a locked analysis plan, repeated across a collaboration of that size, is a fact about the protocol. What that fact means for the original theory is the argument the paper started and did not, by itself, finish.

The question

Does the ego-depletion effect appear when independent laboratories run one agreed procedure, without the freedom to stop early, switch outcomes, or report the comparison that worked? The question is deliberately narrower than the question Baumeister asked in 1998. It is not whether some form of mental fatigue exists. It is whether a specific, preregistered operationalisation of depletion, a first self-control task followed by a second task that the theory says should suffer, produces the predicted difference often enough to see. A theory that lives only in the operationalisations its supporters adjust after the fact is a different object from a theory that survives a protocol written down in advance. Hagger and colleagues chose the second standard.

How the replication was set up

A registered replication report is a crowd performing one score. The protocol, including the tasks, the exclusions, the sample size at each site and the analysis, is reviewed and frozen before data collection. Each laboratory runs that protocol. The individual datasets are then combined by a plan that does not depend on what the datasets happen to show. The point of the freezing is to remove the degrees of freedom that, in the original literature, let a small effect be nursed into significance. The point of the crowd is to stop the result from being a fact about one city's students, one hour of the day, or one experimenter's manner.

The procedure itself followed the depletion template. Participants completed an initial task designed to require self-control, or a version of the task that did not, and then completed a second task on which a depleted person should do worse. The same materials, the same instructions and the same rule for computing the effect were used across the laboratories. Sites contributed their own samples and their own small differences of room and hour, which is what a multi-lab study is for. If the effect depends on a delicate local atmosphere, the sites will scatter and the average will be unimpressive. If the effect is a robust feature of the two tasks, the sites will agree. Either outcome is informative, and the registration means both outcomes can be reported without a fight about which analysis was the real one.

What they found

The combined effect was very close to zero. Laboratories did not, as a group, find that the self-control task left people reliably worse at the task that followed. The estimate sat near the null, and the uncertainty around it left little room for an effect of the size the early literature had advertised. More than 2,000 participants is a sample in which a substantial depletion effect would have had difficulty hiding. Its absence is the result. Individual sites varied, as sites do, and the variation was not a quiet signal of a real effect that the average had muffled. The preregistered summary was a small number, indistinguishable for practical purposes from no difference.

The paper's restraint about that number matters. A replication of this kind does not declare a theory impossible. It declares that this protocol, run faithfully at this scale, did not produce the phenomenon the protocol was built to produce. That is a narrower sentence than ego depletion is false, and it is the sentence the data earn. The card's phrasing, an effect very close to zero, is the right phrasing. It describes the estimate. It does not smuggle in a metaphysical claim about willpower.

How the authors read the result

Hagger and the replicating teams read the outcome as a serious problem for the standard depletion procedure, and as a reason to doubt that the published record had been estimating a large, general effect. A literature composed of small positive studies, followed by a large preregistered study that finds nothing of the kind, is the pattern publication bias and flexible analysis are expected to paint. The replication does not need to accuse any particular 1998 experiment of error. It needs to show that the effect those experiments were taken to establish does not appear when the garden of forking paths is closed. On that showing, the burden moves. Anyone who still holds that a first act of self-control reliably impairs a second has to say which procedure would show it, in a protocol that is not revised after the impairment fails to arrive.

They also read the result as a methodological exhibit. Multi-lab registration was new enough, in 2016, that a famous effect falling over was news about the instrument as well as about the effect. The instrument had done the thing it was designed to do. It had made a small number hard to explain away as one laboratory's bad luck. Subsequent debates about ego depletion were debates about this number, not debates about whether someone, somewhere, had once found a significant t-test. That shift of venue is part of the paper's importance on a card about the replication crisis.

Limits

The limit proponents pressed immediately is the limit of the manipulation. Baumeister and colleagues argued that the replication had used a weak first task, too mild to deplete anyone, so that a null result was a failure to stage the phenomenon rather than a failure of the phenomenon to appear. The objection is not empty. A theory about a spent resource makes a prediction only when the first task spends the resource. If the registered task did not, the combined sample is a precise measurement of the wrong contrast. Hagger's paper cannot, from inside one protocol, prove that the protocol was strong. It can prove that a protocol many researchers were willing to treat as a fair test, and that resembled tasks the literature had accepted, did not work. Strength is the thing the next study had to vary on purpose.

A second limit is about scope. One pair of tasks is not every pair the theory might propose. A null on this pair leaves open a depletion effect that appears only for longer exertion, or only for particular second tasks, or only for people who believe willpower is limited. Those are possible amendments. They are also amendments that shrink the original claim, which was exciting precisely because it was general: control impairs control, across contents, because both draw on one tank. A theory that survives only in a corner the registered test did not visit is a theory that has lost the generality that made it a theory of willpower.

What happened next

The weak-manipulation reply set the agenda for the 2021 multi-site study, which was built with the explicit aim of using a paradigm proponents would accept as a real test, and which again found at best a very small effect. Vadillo, Gold and Osman's examination of Many Labs 3 pointed the same way. The belief-based account of Job, Dweck and Walton remained a live alternative, because it predicts the sequential impairment mainly in people who hold a limited theory of willpower, a moderator the 2016 protocol was not designed to crown or kill. The practical upshot the card draws is modest, and it fits this paper's result. Rest and motivation matter to effort. A fuel tank of the self, drained by a short laboratory chore and restored by the next sweet drink, does not have reliable evidence behind it.

The replication also belongs to the wider crisis as an example of a field willing to spend a large collaboration on one of its own celebrities. That willingness is easy to underrate once the result is known. Before the data, ego depletion was still a finding a textbook could print. After them, printing it required a footnote. The footnote is this paper.

Why it matters here

Folio's ego-depletion card is a story in three movements: a striking claim, a failed check, and a set of attempts to save or replace the claim. Hagger and colleagues are the failed check, at a scale of 23 laboratories and more than 2,000 people, with the analysis locked. The 1998 paper remains the source of the tank metaphor and of the cookie-and-radish image. This paper is the reason the card tells you to treat the tank as unproven rather than as a fact that later quibbles merely complicate. Read it for what a preregistered null looks like when the effect was supposed to be robust. Read the 2021 study for the answer to the objection that the test was too gentle. The two together are the evidentiary centre of the card.
