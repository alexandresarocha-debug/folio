Walter Herbranson and Julia Schroeder of Whitman College asked whether pigeons would fall for the Monty Hall dilemma. Most people stay with their first door, even though switching doubles the chance of winning. Pigeons cannot be told the rules, so they have to learn from many rounds of experience.

In their version, three response keys in an operant chamber served as the three doors and access to mixed grain was the prize. After the bird's first choice, one losing key was eliminated and the bird could stay with its first key or switch to the remaining one. In Experiment 1, six pigeons played up to 100 rounds a day for 30 days. On day one they switched on about 36% of trials. By day 30 they switched on about 96%, close to the best possible strategy.

In Experiment 2 the researchers reversed the odds, so that staying won two times in three. The pigeons adjusted and learned to stay. They seemed to be tracking how often each choice paid off.

Then the researchers ran the same task with people, avoiding the usual game-show wording. Twelve undergraduates who did not know the puzzle played 200 rounds each. In the condition matching the pigeons' first experiment, six students switched on about 57% of trials in the first 50 rounds and about 66% in the last 50, still far from always switching. The authors cite Donald Granberg and Thad Brown's 1995 finding that people given repeated rounds settled at switching about half the time.

What this means in real life: learning from outcomes can beat reasoning about them. People given clear feedback still cling to explanations that feel right.

Caution: the samples were small, six pigeons and six people in the key comparison. The pigeons' version differed from the human version in prizes, training and motivation, so this is not a contest of intelligence.
