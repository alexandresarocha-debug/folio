Most twin studies of intelligence recruit middle-class families. Eric Turkheimer and colleagues at the University of Virginia used an older American dataset that did not: the National Collaborative Perinatal Project, which enrolled pregnant women at 12 urban hospitals and followed their children to age 7, with many poor and minority families.

The sample had 623 twin births. After losses and one outlier pair, 319 pairs remained: 114 identical and 205 non-identical. The children took the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children at age 7. Family socioeconomic status was scored from parents' education, occupation and income. The families were poor by any standard: median income was between 6,000 and 7,000 dollars a year, and 25 percent were below the 1973 poverty line for a family of four.

Instead of one heritability for everyone, the team let the genetic and environmental parts vary with socioeconomic status. The result was striking. In the poorest families, the model suggested that about 60 percent of the variation in IQ came from the shared family environment and the genetic contribution was close to zero. In affluent families it was almost exactly the reverse.

The interpretation is that when children's circumstances vary hugely, differences in home and schooling dominate. When basic needs are met, more of the remaining differences track genes.

What this means in real life: a heritability figure belongs to the population it was measured in. A high heritability for IQ in comfortable families says nothing about what better conditions could do for poor children.

Replication: the effect is real but smaller and more local than this first study suggested. In 2016 Elliot Tucker-Drob and Timothy Bates pooled 14 studies with 24,926 twin and sibling pairs. In US studies they found clear support for a moderately sized gene by socioeconomic status effect. In studies from Western Europe and Australia, where social policies ensure more uniform access to good education and health care, the effect was zero or reversed. They also noted that Turkheimer's original effect was much larger than the pooled estimate. Tucker-Drob, E. M., and Bates, T. C. (2016).
