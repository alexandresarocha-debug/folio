David Gal and Derek Rucker set out to test the slogan that losses loom larger than gains, using a comparison that does not mix "loss" with "do nothing." In the usual mug study, keeping the mug is the passive option and buying one is the active option, so a preference for the mug you already hold can be inertia rather than a special hatred of losses. Their retention test makes both outcomes active. You pay to obtain the thing, or you pay to keep a thing you will otherwise lose. If losses really weigh more, people should pay more to keep it.

Across objects that included mugs, phones, internet service, and train service, willingness to pay to keep the good did not reliably exceed willingness to pay to get it. For an ordinary good the obtain price was often the higher one. People said they would pay $50.43 a month to get fiber-optic internet, and $43.75 a month to keep fiber-optic internet they already had. They were not confused about the wording. In that scenario, 85 percent of the people in the keep condition said they had thought about the choice as avoiding a loss. Only 9 percent of the people in the obtain condition said that.

A choice version, with 182 people, made the same point with a mug. Participants had either a team mug or $5, and both keeping and switching were posed as actions. The mug was chosen 26 percent of the time when they already had it and 26 percent of the time when they did not. When Gal and Rucker ran the old version, in which keeping the mug was the passive option, the familiar gap came back: the mug was chosen 41 percent of the time by people who held it and 18 percent of the time by people who did not.

For a shopper or a negotiator, the caution is about which fact you are using. People do hate to give up a price, a seat, or a plan they already treat as theirs, and a seller who says "don't lose this" is using a real reluctance to move. Gal and Rucker's claim is that the reluctance is often about staying put, not about losses being psychologically twice gains. A fee you have to act to avoid and a fee you have to act to pay can look much more alike than the slogan says.

In the same journal, Itamar Simonson and Ran Kivetz answered that questions about paying to keep an expiring service are a weak stand-in for actually handing over something you own. The mug gap in the old procedure, which Gal and Rucker themselves reproduced, is the piece that answer hangs on.
