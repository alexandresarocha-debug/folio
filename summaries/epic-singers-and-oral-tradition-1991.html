Albert Lord wrote about one singer more than any other. He first published a portrait of Avdo Međedović in the Journal of American Folklore in 1956, and the same title, Avdo Međedović, Guslar, is the fourth chapter of his 1991 book Epic Singers and Oral Tradition. Avdo lived in the village of Obrov near Bijelo Polje, in what is now Montenegro, and died in 1955 at about 85. Lord wrote that it may well be that he was the last of the truly great epic singers of the Balkan Slavic tradition.

Avdo's life was not literary. He was born around 1875 in the Ottoman Empire, into a Muslim family whose ancestors had been Serbian Orthodox. As a teenager he worked as a butcher, as his father and brother did. He then served in the army, where he was wounded and spent 45 days in hospital. Later he got a small farm, married and had three sons. He never learned to read.

Lord reports that Avdo had a repertory of 58 epics. In 1935 Parry recorded nine of them on phonograph discs, and his assistant Nikola Vujnović wrote down four more from Avdo's dictation. The songs are very long. Among the recorded ones is a song of 13,331 lines about Osmanbey Delibegović and Luka Pavičević, and a Wedding of Meho Smailagić of about 12,300 lines, which Avdo dictated over five days. On the fifth day he said he knew even longer songs. When Lord returned in 1950 and 1951 he recorded three more songs. He observed that Avdo could sing songs about the length of Homer's Odyssey, so that an illiterate butcher in a small town of the central Balkans was equalling Homer, at least in the length of a song.

Lord also used Avdo to test how singers learn. Avdo learned The Wedding of Meho from a friend who read him a printed pamphlet five or six times. The printed text had about 2,160 lines, and his own version was much longer and more descriptive. In one scene, an assembly of lords in the city of Kanidža, the print takes 141 lines and Avdo's version takes 1,053. In 1935 Lord also asked him to sing a song he had heard only once, from Mumin Vlahovljak. Mumin's song ran 2,294 lines, and Avdo's version was 6,313.

What this means in real life: skill can be the ability to expand and recombine, not only to store and repeat. And the best evidence for a theory may be one exceptional case, studied closely.

Caution: Avdo was exceptional, and Lord called him one of the truly great singers, so ordinary singers will not match his feats. His case supports the idea that long songs can be composed without writing, but not that every singer worked this way.

Source. Lord, A. B. (1991). Epic Singers and Oral Tradition. Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press. Chapter 4, 'Avdo Međedović, Guslar'; see also Lord, A. B. (1956), Avdo Međedović, Guslar, The Journal of American Folklore, 69(273), 320-330.
