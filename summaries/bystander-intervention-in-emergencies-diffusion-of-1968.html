John Darley and Bibb Latané began with the Kitty Genovese case, in which newspapers reported that 38 neighbours had watched a murder without calling the police. Commentators blamed apathy and the coldness of city life. Darley and Latané proposed something simpler: when several people know about an emergency, responsibility is spread among them, and each feels less pressure to act.

Their subjects were 72 New York University introductory psychology students, 59 women and 13 men. Each sat alone in a small room with headphones and a microphone, and was told the group would discuss personal problems of college life over an intercom, rather than face to face, to avoid embarrassment. The other voices were recorded in advance. One of them belonged to a student who mentioned he was prone to seizures and then, later in the discussion, seemed to have one. Subjects were led to believe that the discussion group had two, three or six members.

Group size made a large difference. Of students who believed they were the only other listener, 85% reported the emergency before the victim's voice was cut off, on average within 52 seconds. With one other listener the figure was 62%, at 93 seconds; with four others it was 31%, at 166 seconds. Everyone in the two-person condition eventually went for help, compared with 62% of those in six-person groups.

The students who did not respond were not indifferent. Many showed trembling hands and sweating palms, and asked whether the victim was all right when the experimenter appeared. Personality measures did not predict who helped.

What this means in real life: in an emergency, people may hold back because they assume someone else will act. If you need help in a crowd, asking one specific person directly removes that ambiguity.

Caution: the sample was small and the situation artificial, since subjects never saw anyone and could not see what others did. Later research found that the effect is weaker in clearly dangerous situations, and the newspaper story that inspired the study turned out to be largely inaccurate.
