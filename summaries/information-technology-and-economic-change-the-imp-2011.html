Jeremiah Dittmar's finding is about towns, and the number is large. Between 1500 and 1600, European cities where a printing press had been set up in the 1400s grew 60 percent faster than otherwise similar cities. Those cities had not been growing faster before the press arrived. The gap is not explained by printers simply picking the places that were about to boom. Gutenberg had set up in Mainz around 1450, and the craft moved outward from there. Distance from Mainz predicts which cities got a press early, and it does not predict which cities had been growing in the century before.

The count behind the result is 205 cities that adopted movable type between 1450 and 1500, drawn from the British Library's Incunabula Short Title Catalogue and from the older lists of Febvre and Martin and of Colin Clair. In the plain averages, press cities grew 20 percentage points more than cities without a press. They were already the big places: about 30 percent of the cities with population figures, and about 58 percent of the urban population in 1500. In the regressions, the press lines up with an extra 0.19 log points of growth in a century when European cities grew 0.27 log points on average. The instrumental estimate, using distance from Mainz, is larger, 0.58 log points, about 78 percentage points. Stretched from 1500 to 1800, the raw advantage shrinks to about 7 percentage points. National income series still show no clean break in the late fifteenth century. The press shows up when the unit is the city.

The practical point is about where to look. A technology can rearrange which towns get the people, the shops, and the skilled work, and leave the continental average almost flat. City population is a headcount, a proxy for a place that could draw migrants, and it is the comparable number Dittmar has for hundreds of towns. It is a fact about urban growth over one century.

Later work by Dittmar and Skipper Seabold, circulated in 2019, narrowed the mechanism. They found no robust link between printing in general and city growth from 1500 to 1600. The link that held was with books that taught commercial arithmetic, accounting, and bills of exchange. A press that printed other things did not, on that evidence, do the same job.
