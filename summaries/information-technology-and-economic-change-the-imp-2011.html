A revolution that left no trace in the usual totals

Jeremiah Dittmar’s 2011 paper in the Quarterly Journal of Economics starts from a mismatch that economic historians had learned to live with. The printing press is everyone’s candidate for the decisive information technology of the early modern world, and yet the standard macroeconomic series, productivity, real wages, income per person, do not show a clean break in the late fifteenth century that one could point to and call the effect of print. One respectable conclusion is that print did not matter much for the economy. It transformed a small sector, the making of texts, whose share of output was modest, and the famous cultural consequences, if they were real, were cultural. Dittmar’s paper is an argument that this conclusion comes from looking at the wrong unit. Print was adopted in some European cities and not in others, and its economic footprint, if it had one, would be local before it was national. A national average can bury a technology that rearranged which towns grew.

The paper therefore moves the question down a level. Between 1500 and 1600, did cities where printing presses had been established in the fifteenth century grow faster than otherwise similar cities that did not have them? The answer he reports is that they did. Cities that adopted printing in the fourteen-hundreds grew about sixty per cent faster over that century than comparable cities that did not. They had not been growing faster before adoption. And the association does not look like a story in which printers simply chose the towns that were about to boom anyway. The design is built to make that alternative as difficult as possible, and the headline number is what survives it.

How a press arrived, and how he uses that

The historical pattern he exploits is the diffusion of the technology out of Mainz. Movable type of the European sort was a specific craft, carried by specific people, and it spread through the cities of the Holy Roman Empire and then outward in a pattern historians have compared to ripples. Distance from Mainz predicts early adoption. That fact would be useless if distance from Mainz also predicted city growth in the centuries before print, or if it stood in for some other advantage, river access, political status, that itself caused later growth. Dittmar’s claim, which is the claim on which the instrumental-variable part of the paper rests, is that distance to Mainz is related to the arrival of the press and is not related to prior growth or to the other observable reasons a city might subsequently expand. A city that got a press because a printer stopped there, rather than because its future was already bright, is as close to an accidental adoption as the fifteenth century is likely to offer.

He does not rely on the instrument alone. The simpler comparisons already matter: adopters against non-adopters, with controls for how large a city was and for how it had been growing. The logic is the same logic as in other historical difference-in-differences designs. If print cities were special, their specialness should show up before 1450 as well as after. His finding is that it does not. The growth advantage appears after the technology arrives. Propensity comparisons, matching cities that looked similarly likely to adopt, are there to answer the remaining worry that adopters were a selected club of places with merchants, universities, or bishops. Selection of that kind is real, printers went where there were buyers, and the paper’s point is that within the set of places one would have expected to do well, having the press still marks out faster subsequent growth. The instrument then asks whether the part of adoption that is explained merely by being nearer Mainz tells the same story. He reports that it does.

What the sixty per cent is, and is not

The number needs to be read in the units he used. It is a comparison of city population growth rates over a century, not a claim that print raised national income by sixty per cent, and not a claim about wages. City population is a proxy for economic success in a world where the best comparable numbers across hundreds of towns are headcounts. A town that grows faster is a town that drew people, and people moved towards wages, safety, and markets. The advantage he finds is concentrated in the first century after diffusion, which fits a technology shock better than it fits some permanent cultural superiority of the adopting places. Over longer horizons the advantage is still there in his estimates and is less dramatic, which is what one would expect if other cities eventually acquired presses and if the early adopters’ lead was partly a lead in time.

The mechanism he favours is the one the technology’s name suggests. Print is an information technology. Cities with presses became places where knowledge-intensive activities, commercial, administrative, and technical, could operate at a lower cost of acquiring and checking texts. He treats this as distinct from the religious upheaval that print also amplified. The Reformation is in the same century, and it is a legitimate rival explanation if Protestant cities grew faster for reasons of literacy, plunder, or institutions, and if Protestant cities were also print cities. The paper’s answer is to take that overlap seriously rather than to pretend the press was an economically pure event. The design’s emphasis on the fifteenth-century adoption, including in cities that did not later become Protestant, is part of separating a technology effect from a confessional one. A reader who wants the separation to be total will be disappointed. Sixteenth-century Europe does not offer a clean experiment in which religion sits still. It offers a pattern in which the press predicts growth beyond what the obvious confounders absorb.

Relation to the historians

Dittmar cites the historical literature, including Elizabeth Eisenstein’s argument that print was an agent of change rather than a passive carrier, as the claim his numbers can finally address in an economic register. Eisenstein had described dissemination, standardisation, and the cumulative correction of knowledge. She had not been in a position to ask whether any of that showed up in the relative size of towns, and the macroeconomic silence had been used as an argument against her. Dittmar’s result is the first widely credited quantitative trace. It supports the proposition that print had economic consequences large enough to see. It does not, by itself, confirm that the mechanism was the one she named. A city can grow because it houses a profitable luxury trade in books, or because printers are a skilled and well-connected migrant population, or because the press really does lower the cost of commercial knowledge. Distinguishing those channels wants evidence on occupations, on book prices, and on the content of what was printed, and some of that evidence has been pursued in later work. The 2011 paper establishes the city-level fact and a credible reason to treat it as causal.

Adrian Johns’s critique of Eisenstein, that fixity was a social achievement and that early print was unstable, sits beside this result without being tested by it. Dittmar does not measure whether the books were accurate. He measures whether the towns that had the shops grew. A technology can be chaotic at the level of the individual title and still be economically powerful at the level of the town. Indeed, Johns’s picture, in which trust, privilege, and the Stationers’ institutions had to be built, is compatible with economic effects that arrive as those institutions settle, and with effects that are larger in some cities than others. The economic paper and the bibliographical critique are not on opposite sides of a single bet. They answer different questions about the same machine.

What can still be objected

The remaining objections are the standard ones for this design, and they are not empty. Population is not welfare. A city can grow by pulling in the poor, by annexing suburbs, or by changing its political definition, and long-run urban growth series depend on reconstructions that are better for large and famous places than for small ones. Distance from Mainz is a clever instrument only for as long as one believes it has no other route into sixteenth-century growth. A sceptic who thinks that proximity to the Rhine economy, or to a particular political geography of the Empire, drove both early presses and later urban success, will not be fully reassured by controls. The paper’s pre-trend evidence is the right reply, and pre-trends are only as good as the earlier population figures. Finally, the result is about a century of urban expansion in Europe. It says nothing directly about whether print caused the Reformation’s institutional outcomes, or about science, or about the formation of national reading publics. Those are the historians’ questions. Dittmar’s is whether the technology left a mark on the urban system. On the evidence he assembles, it did, and the mark is large relative to the other differences he can measure between towns.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on the printing press as an agent of change uses this paper as the quantitative sequel to Eisenstein. The card’s statement is the paper’s own: European cities that adopted printing in the fifteenth century grew substantially faster than similar cities between 1500 and 1600. The sixty per cent figure is the more precise form of that statement, and it is a figure about city growth rates, which is the right level of precision for a card that also has to carry Johns’s warning. Johns says the printed book was not automatically fixed or trusted. Dittmar says the printed city’s economy nonetheless pulled ahead. Eisenstein says the shift from script to print changed the conditions of knowledge and of public argument. The card needs all three because they stop one another from overreaching. Read Dittmar when the cultural story seems to float free of any economic consequence, and read him with the unit in mind. He did not find the press in the national income statistics.
