Economists had looked for the printing press in the numbers that usually register a great invention, and they had not found it. Aggregate productivity and income per person in early modern Europe do not show a clear jump after Johannes Gutenberg's shop in Mainz in the middle of the fifteenth century. Jeremiah Dittmar's answer, in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2011, is that the wrong unit was being measured. Presses stood in cities. The people who bought the books lived in cities. If the press changed the economy, the change should show up in which cities grew.

He matched the places that had a press to the city populations compiled by Paul Bairoch and colleagues, a record of European towns that at some point reached 5,000 people. Between 1500 and 1600, cities where a press had been established in the 1400s grew 60 percent faster than otherwise similar cities. They had no such advantage before 1500. The association is not explained by printers simply picking towns that were already on the way up. Dittmar's check is distance from Mainz. The press spread outward from the city where it began, and that distance predicts which towns adopted it early. It does not predict how those towns had been growing before the press existed. Used as an instrument, distance from Mainz still leaves a large growth gap after 1500.

The second result is the one that makes the first usable. Adoption in the fifteenth century and growth in the sixteenth are separated by decades. Towns that would get a press were not already pulling away in the century before they got one. Whatever printers brought, it was not a label stuck on a boom that was going to happen anyway. Dittmar's reading of the mechanism is practical rather than mystical. Printers turned out schoolbooks, arithmetics, and manuals of commercial practice, and a town with that stock of usable technique had an advantage in skills and in trade that a similar town without it did not. The press was a local information technology. Its trace is a local population.

The finding is a way to cut a sentence like "the printing press changed everything" down to a sentence that can be checked. The version that survives the population figures is narrower. Towns with a press by 1500 grew faster over the next hundred years, and they had not been growing faster before. A national average can hide that, because most places did not have a press, and because the gain sat in city size rather than in a single measure of output per person. When a new communication technology is said to have transformed an economy, the first question is which places actually received it, and whether those places were already winning.
