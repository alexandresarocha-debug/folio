Jeremiah Dittmar's question, in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2011, is why the printing press left no clean mark on the usual European totals. Economists looking at productivity, wages, and income per person had found no break in the late fifteenth century that they could call the effect of print. One respectable conclusion was that the press transformed a small trade, the making of books, and that the famous cultural consequences, if real, were cultural. Dittmar moves the measurement down to the city. Between 1500 and 1600, European cities where a press had been set up in the 1400s grew 60 percent faster than otherwise similar cities. Those cities had not been growing faster before the press arrived. The gap is not, on his tests, a story in which printers simply chose the towns that were about to boom.

The machine itself had a place and a price. Johannes Gutenberg established the press in Mainz around 1450. Between 1450 and 1500, Dittmar reports, the real price of books fell by about two-thirds. The historical lists he combines name 205 cities that had a press by 1500. Distance from Mainz predicts which cities got one early, and it does not predict which cities had been growing in the century before print. He uses that distance as an instrument: the part of adoption explained by being nearer Mainz, rather than by a city's prospects, still lines up with faster growth after 1500. In the raw averages, which are easier to misread as the headline, print cities grew 20 percentage points more than other cities over 1500–1600. They were already large. Thirty percent of the cities with population data had adopted, and those cities held 58 percent of the counted urban population in 1500. Over the longer stretch from 1500 to 1800, the raw advantage falls to about 7 percentage points, which is what a lead in time should do once other towns acquire the same tool.

The number is a difference in city population, which is the comparable record across hundreds of towns. It is not a claim that national income rose by 60 percent, and it is not a wage. A town that grew faster is a town that drew people. Dittmar's reading is that presses made commercial and technical knowledge cheaper to get and to check, and that the gain was largest where cheap water transport could carry the new practices. Port cities, in his estimates, gained from the press beyond the gain of printing alone or of being a port alone.

What to do with the figure is to keep the unit attached to it. A national average can bury a technology that rearranged which towns people moved to. The press can matter enormously in the cities that had it, and still be hard to see in a single series for Europe. His own longer-run table is the caution against treating the sixteenth-century gap as a permanent ranking of lucky towns.
