In a cemetery at Aschheim, near Munich, archaeologists had found hundreds of early medieval graves, including a cluster of double and multiple burials from the sixth century. David Wagner, Jennifer Klunk, Hendrik Poinar, and their colleagues took teeth from two of the dead, labeled A120 and A76, and sequenced the DNA of the plague bacterium Yersinia pestis. Radiocarbon put A120 at about 533, plus or minus 98 years, and A76 at about 504, plus or minus 61 years, ranges that overlap the plague of Justinian, which contemporary writers describe as arriving in Egypt in 541 and in Constantinople in 542. The paper, in The Lancet Infectious Diseases in 2014, compares those two draft genomes with 131 other Y. pestis genomes from later pandemics and from modern rodents.

The family tree put the Aschheim strains on a branch of their own. The branch sits between two groups that still exist in rodent reservoirs, called 0.ANT1 and 0.ANT2, and it sits far from the strains of the Black Death in the fourteenth century and from the strains of the nineteenth- and twentieth-century pandemic. Every relevant joint in that placement had full support in their bootstrap test. The branch has no known modern descendant in the set of genomes they searched. Their conclusion is that the Justinianic plague and the Black Death were separate crossings from rodents into people, about eight hundred years apart, and that the first lineage then died out or hides in some animal population nobody has sampled.

Two teeth from one Bavarian graveyard are a thin basis for a Mediterranean pandemic, and the radiocarbon windows are decades wide. What the genomes do establish is the identity of the microbe in those two people, and its place on the tree. A reader can hold that result without deciding how many millions died. The bacterium in the Aschheim graves is plague, and it is not the plague that killed medieval London.

The map was filled in later, and the tree held. In 2019 Marcel Keller and colleagues published eight further first-pandemic genomes, from Britain, France, Germany, and Spain, including a burial at Edix Hill near Cambridge. They fall on the same extinct branch. The disease reached more of Europe than two Bavarian skeletons could show. The separation from the Black Death, which was Wagner's main result, stayed intact.
