Background

The plague that reached the eastern Roman empire in 541, and that historians call the Plague of Justinian, was described by contemporaries in frightening detail. Procopius, writing in Constantinople, recorded swelling in the groin and elsewhere, fever, and death on a scale that disorganised the city. For centuries the diagnosis rested on those descriptions. They look like bubonic plague to a modern reader. They are not a laboratory result. Symptoms in old texts are ambiguous, and several diseases can produce fever and death in a crowded port. The Black Death of the fourteenth century had, by the time of this paper, been tied to the bacterium Yersinia pestis by ancient DNA. The sixth-century pandemic had not yet been given the same kind of proof at genomic scale. A detection had been reported from a Bavarian cemetery. What was still needed was a genome, and a place for that genome on the evolutionary tree of the bacterium.

The question

David Wagner and his colleagues asked two questions that a historian cannot answer from Procopius alone. Was the disease in sixth-century victims actually Yersinia pestis? And if it was, were those strains the ancestors of the later medieval plague, a separate branch, or something else entirely? The first question identifies the pandemic. The second question says what kind of biological event the first plague pandemic was. A single continuous infection running from Justinian to the Black Death would be a different history from a sixth-century outbreak whose lineage later died out.

How the study was built

The material came from teeth of people buried in a sixth-century cemetery at Aschheim in Bavaria. Teeth are useful in this work because the pulp can preserve fragments of the blood-borne pathogens that were present when the person died, and because the cemetery can be dated by its archaeology to the period of the pandemic. The team recovered Yersinia pestis DNA and reconstructed draft genomes, then compared those genomes with modern strains and with what was known of later medieval plague. The methods of ancient DNA are designed to tell ancient bacterial fragments from the contamination that surrounds any excavation and any laboratory. Phylogeny, the family tree of the strains, is the analytical point. Where a genome sits on that tree is the historical fact the paper can establish.

The geography is indirect and has to be stated honestly. Aschheim is not Constantinople, and it is not the Egyptian port where the literary sources place the arrival of the plague. The link from this cemetery to the pandemic of 541 is a link of date, of the wider archaeological context, and of the historical record of a disease moving through Europe in those years. The paper identifies the pathogen in these people. It infers that they belong to the pandemic the texts describe.

What was found

The DNA is Yersinia pestis. The sixth-century genomes fall on an early branch of the species, separate from the branch associated with the Black Death. The lineage looks like a line that ended, rather than the trunk from which all later plague grew. That is a more precise result than a simple confirmation. The Justinianic pandemic and the medieval pandemic were both caused by the same species of bacterium. They were not the same epidemic, and the first does not appear, on this evidence, to have been the direct parent of the second. The bacterium was present in a community far from the imperial capital, which fits a disease that moved along the routes of the sixth century rather than a disaster confined to one city's literature.

What the authors make of it

They read the genomes as the biological identification of the first plague pandemic. Historians who had treated the Justinianic outbreak as bubonic plague were, on the organism itself, right. Historians who had left the question open, because symptoms can mislead, now have a reason to close it for the people in this cemetery and, by the historical connection, for the wider outbreak. The extinct-looking branch matters for a second reason. It warns against telling plague as one continuous story from the sixth century to the fourteenth. The species recurs. Particular lineages can disappear. Later genomic work has added further genomes from the first pandemic and has kept the central identification: Yersinia pestis, on its own early branch.

Limits and later criticism

A genome is not a death toll. Nothing in the phylogeny says what share of the population died, whether the eastern empire's tax receipts collapsed, or whether Justinian's wars failed because of the disease. Those are historical questions, and they need coins, documents, burials in the aggregate, and a reading of the political record. The sample is a small number of individuals from one cemetery. A different region might add diversity to the tree, and ancient DNA is always a fight against contamination and against partial sequences. Dating a cemetery to the sixth century is not the same as dating a death to the year 542.

The paper has been used, fairly, as the confirmation that lets historians stop arguing about the name of the bacterium and start arguing about its consequences. It has also been asked, unfairly, to carry those consequences by itself. Critics of a maximalist reading of the Justinianic plague do not, in general, deny the genomes. They deny the jump from a real pathogen in a Bavarian grave to an empire-wide demographic break. That jump is an interpretation. The finding that these people died with Yersinia pestis in their blood is the result. Keeping the two apart is what makes the result usable.

Why it matters here

The Folio card on climate and plague says that the Plague of Justinian, from 541, is now confirmed by ancient DNA as Yersinia pestis, the bacterium of the Black Death. This paper is that confirmation, and it is more specific than the card's sentence has room to be. The bacterium is the same species. The strain is not the Black Death's strain. The first pandemic has a biological identity of its own, established from sixth-century teeth rather than from a resemblance of symptoms. Harper's The Fate of Rome depends on that identity when it makes the sixth-century disease a central actor in the end of the ancient world.

The card also says the size of the death toll remains debated, and it points to the critics who think the pandemic's effects have been overstated. The genomes do not settle that debate, and they were never going to. They settle the prior question without which the debate is only a quarrel about adjectives in Procopius. A reader of the card can now see the division of labour. Wagner and his colleagues show what the pathogen was, and where its lineage sits. The argument about whether that pathogen changed the course of the empire is a different kind of evidence, and the card is right to keep it open.
