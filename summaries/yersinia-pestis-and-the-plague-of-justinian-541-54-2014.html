In the early medieval cemetery at Aschheim, near Munich, David M. Wagner, Hendrik N. Poinar, and their colleagues took teeth from two people, labeled A120 and A76, and searched for plague. Radiocarbon dated A120 to about 533, plus or minus 98 years, and A76 to about 504, plus or minus 61 years, which puts both burials in the broad window of the pandemic that reached the Mediterranean in 541. The teeth held enough bacterial DNA to reconstruct draft genomes of Yersinia pestis, the organism of bubonic plague. The paper, in The Lancet Infectious Diseases in 2014, is the first time that genome had been recovered from the Justinianic pandemic.

They compared the two genomes with 131 strains from the Black Death and from the plague outbreaks of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The Justinianic pair sits on a branch of its own. That branch has no known living descendant. It falls between two groups of strains that still circulate in rodent reservoirs, called 0.ANT1 and 0.ANT2, and it is distant from the lineage tied to the Black Death of the 1340s. The conclusion is that the sixth-century pandemic and the medieval one were separate jumps from rodents into people, about eight hundred years apart. The first lineage burned out, or it survives somewhere still unsampled. It is not the ancestor of the second.

The genomes close one argument and leave the larger one open. Before this DNA, readers had to decide from symptoms in Procopius and other writers whether the disease of 541 was bubonic plague at all. Aschheim says the bacterium was Yersinia pestis. It does not say how many people died, or whether the deaths changed the course of the eastern Roman empire. A later tally by Lee Mordechai and colleagues, in 2019, put the published late-antique plague genomes at about 45 individuals. That is enough to show a pathogen's presence across a wide map, including places far from Constantinople. It is not a body count, and it cannot by itself support a claim that a quarter or a half of the Mediterranean population died.

Source. David M. Wagner, Jennifer Klunk, Hendrik N. Poinar, and colleagues, "Yersinia pestis and the Plague of Justinian 541–543 AD: a genomic analysis," The Lancet Infectious Diseases (2014). https://doi.org/10.1016/S1473-3099(13)70323-2
