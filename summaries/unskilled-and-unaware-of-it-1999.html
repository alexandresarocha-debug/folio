Justin Kruger and David Dunning asked Cornell students how funny they were at spotting a joke, and then checked them against professionals. Sixty-five undergraduates rated 30 jokes, from Woody Allen to a book of pet jokes. Eight working comedians had rated the same jokes; one comedian's ratings did not agree with the others and were dropped. On average the students put their ability to recognize what is funny at the 66th percentile. The people who had matched the comedians least well did not know it. Across the four studies in the paper, students in the bottom quarter of the scores landed around the 12th percentile and estimated themselves around the 62nd. In the humor study alone, that bottom quarter overestimated its standing by 46 percentile points.

The gap was just as plain on a grammar test, where taste is not an excuse. Eighty-four Cornell students took a 20-item test of standard written English. The 17 people in the bottom quarter scored at the 10th percentile on average. They thought their grammar ability was at the 67th percentile, and they thought their score on that test was at the 61st. At the top of the room the error ran the other way, and it was smaller. On a 20-item logic test given to 45 students, the best quarter estimated their reasoning ability at only the 74th percentile. In a further study with 140 students, a short lesson on the logic problems led the low scorers to rate their own performance more harshly. The skill they had lacked was also the skill they needed in order to see the miss.

That matters anywhere a person decides whether to practice, ask for help, or take on a job. The people who most need the feedback are the ones a self-rating will not warn. A scored comparison, a worked example, or a teacher who marks the paper will do what the feeling of "I did fine" does not. The chart that circulates under this paper's name, with confidence highest at the first contact with a subject, is not a figure in the article. What the article plots is estimated percentile against actual quartile: a large overestimate at the bottom, and a milder underestimate at the top.

How much of that pattern is a special blindness, and how much is arithmetic, is still argued. People who call themselves above average, and whose self-ratings are noisy, will look too confident at the bottom and too modest at the top even if insight is fine. Gignac and Zajenkowski tested the claim in 2020 with 929 people, a self-rating of intelligence, and Raven's matrices. People did overrate themselves: a self-rated IQ of about 124 against a tested IQ of about 102. The link between the two scores was essentially a straight line. Once the analysis left the quartile plot behind, a special blindness at the bottom did not appear. Their test was general intelligence, not jokes or grammar.
