Background

In 1999 Justin Kruger and David Dunning published Unskilled and Unaware of It in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Cornell undergraduates took tests, then said how well they thought they had done relative to their peers. The people who had done worst thought they had done fine. The people who had done best were a little too modest about their rank. Kruger and Dunning offered a double burden as the explanation. The skills that let a person perform well on a test are, in large part, the skills that let her recognise a good performance, including her own. Someone who lacks the skills misses the problems and also misses the fact that the problems were missed. Folio's card on the Dunning–Kruger effect is a reading of this paper, and of the meme that later outgrew it.

The meme is part of the paper's public life and not part of its text. A curve in which confidence soars at the first contact with a subject, collapses into a valley, and then climbs again with genuine expertise has been drawn on a thousand slides. That curve is not in the 1999 paper. The card says so, and a walkthrough that skipped the saying would be describing a different object. The paper is a set of studies about ranked performance and ranked self-estimates. The valley is a later cartoon.

The question

Can people who are unskilled at a task see that they are unskilled? If the answer were a simple yes, low scorers would place themselves low, and high scorers would place themselves high, with whatever noise a self-estimate always carries. Kruger and Dunning thought the answer was no, for a particular reason. Judging a performance uses the same knowledge as producing one. A student who cannot tell a valid argument from an invalid one cannot use that telling to mark her own paper, so her estimate of her percentile has to come from somewhere else: a general sense of being a competent person, a memory of having finished the test, a comparison with an imagined average student who is probably worse. The question is whether the estimates, plotted against actual rank, show that pattern. The further question is whether giving people the missing skill then improves the estimates, which is what the double-burden story requires.

How the studies were set up

The tests covered three domains in which a performance can be scored and a person can have a view of herself: humour, grammar, and logical reasoning. Participants completed the test, and they estimated how their performance compared with the other people in the study, typically as a percentile rank. The design needs both numbers. A raw feeling of confidence, with no measure of achievement, cannot show a metacognitive error. A test score, with no estimate, cannot show whether the error was visible to the person who made it. Putting the two together, and looking at them by quartile of actual performance, is the paper's basic picture.

A further study went after the mechanism rather than the snapshot. If the trouble is the absence of the skill, then teaching the skill should do more than raise the next score. It should change what people recognise as a good answer, and it should change how they rate the attempt they have already made. The logical domain lent itself to that test, because the relevant principles can be taught in a short session and then used to reassess. The paper's claim about a double burden stands or falls on whether the reassessment moves, not only on whether the original estimates were flattering.

What they found

The pattern Folio's card reports is the pattern the studies produced. Those in the bottom quarter of actual performance, whose scores sat around the 12th percentile, estimated that they had landed around the 62nd. They were not claiming genius. They were claiming to be comfortably above average, from a position near the bottom of the room. Top performers showed a smaller error in the other direction. They underestimated their relative standing, which is what one would expect if they assumed other people's answers looked as obvious as their own. Across humour, grammar and logic, the unskilled were not the people with the gloomiest view of their work. They were among the people least able to see the trouble they were in.

The training study did what the mechanism predicts it should. After instruction in how to solve the logical problems, people became better at recognising which answers were the competent ones, and the people who had done badly revised their estimate of their own performance downward. The skill and the judgement of the skill moved together. Kruger and Dunning took that as evidence that the original overestimate was not a generic boast, and not a trick of the way percentiles are reported. It was a consequence of not knowing what a good performance looked like, cured in the same motion as the not knowing.

How the authors read the results

The double burden is the reading they want. Incompetence does two kinds of damage. It produces errors, and it deprives the person of the equipment that would detect the errors. The second damage is why feedback from inside is so unreliable at the bottom of a skill, and why the same person can be both wrong and comfortable. The authors are careful, within the paper, about the reach of the claim. The domains are domains with a scorable right answer and with knowledge that serves both production and evaluation. A skill in which doing and judging come apart, or a domain in which there is no fact of the matter about quality, is outside the design. The paper is about unskilled performance on tasks that have a standard. It is not about every kind of confidence in every kind of life.

They also note the modesty at the top, and they explain it without romance. High performers do not fail to recognise good work. They fail to realise how unusual their own good work is, because the reasoning that is easy for them looks as if it must be easy for everyone. The asymmetry is part of the result. The unskilled overestimate by a lot. The skilled underestimate by a little. A story in which everyone is simply vain does not fit the top of the distribution, and the paper is better than the meme at remembering that there is a top.

Limits

The participants are undergraduates, the tests are short, and the estimates are estimates of rank in a room rather than predictions of a life. Nothing in the design measures a stable trait called insight, portable from grammar to friendship to professional judgement. A person can be lost on a logic test and accurate about her driving, or the reverse. The paper shows a metacognitive pattern on the tests it gave. The cartoon in which a novice at any subject is the most confident person in the building is a generalisation the studies do not attempt, and would not support if they had.

There is a statistical limit that later critics put at the centre, and that the card insists on. If people tend to place themselves above average, and if their self-estimates are noisy, then the lowest scorers will look too confident and the highest scorers will look too modest even when nobody suffers a special deficit of insight. Regression to the mean produces a picture of that shape whenever an imperfect estimate is plotted against the thing it estimates. Part of the classic pattern can be manufactured from noise. Kruger and Dunning's training study is their best answer, because a purely statistical artefact would not move just because someone was taught the logic. How much of the original gap survives a proper correction for the artefact is the argument the card reviews, and it is not an argument the 1999 paper could have finished before it was raised.

Replications and critiques

The paper became famous, and then it became a case study in how a famous picture can leave its data behind. The valley-shaped curve, confidence against knowledge, with a peak of foolish certainty at the start, is the picture most people mean by the effect. It is not a figure in the article. What the article shows is a relationship between objective quartile and estimated percentile, in particular an overestimate at the bottom and a milder underestimate at the top. Reanalyses have asked how much of that relationship is the double burden and how much is the arithmetic of noisy self-placement. The card's summary of that later work is deliberately modest. Random data can draw a graph that looks like the famous one. More appropriate tests, on some skills, find only a weak residue. Dunning and others have answered that a real metacognitive gap remains once the artefact is taken seriously.

The fair summary is the one the card already gives, and a walkthrough should not inflate it. People are imperfect judges of their own competence. The imperfection is largest where the skill being judged is the skill one lacks. The meme, with its mountain of stupidity, overstates the original findings and invents a shape the original findings do not have. Holding both sentences at once is the point of putting this paper on a path about famous results re-examined.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Dunning–Kruger: what the famous graph actually shows, is a correction aimed at this paper's public afterlife. The studies are real. Bottom-quartile performers, scoring around the 12th percentile, really did place themselves around the 62nd, and top performers really were a little too modest, and a short course of instruction really did improve people's ability to see what they had got wrong. The graph that circulates in their name is not theirs. Read the 1999 paper for the double burden and for the size of the claim they actually made. Read the later statistical critiques, which the card names, as arguments about how much of the pattern a noisy estimate would have drawn anyway. The paper is the start of that argument. It is not the cartoon at the end of it.
