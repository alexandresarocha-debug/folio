In 1967 Martin E. P. Seligman and Steven F. Maier published the experiment that named learned helplessness. They started with thirty mongrel dogs and ended with twenty-four, eight in each group, after discards for size, illness, and procedure. One group, strapped in a hammock, learned to stop a 6-milliamp shock to the hind feet by pressing a panel with the head. A yoked group got the same shocks, sixty-four of them, totaling 226 seconds, and panel presses did nothing. A third group was not shocked. Twenty-four hours later each dog was put in a shuttle box. A shoulder-high barrier divided it. If the dog jumped, a 4.5-milliamp shock through the floor stopped. If it did not jump within 60 seconds, the trial ended. There were ten trials.

The dogs who had been able to turn the shock off in the hammock escaped in the box about as well as the dogs who had never been shocked. The yoked dogs did not. They failed to escape on 7.25 of the 10 trials, on average. Six of the eight failed on nine or more trials. They sat while the shock ran its minute. A second experiment, with nine dogs to a group, asked whether an earlier success could block that collapse. Dogs who first learned to jump the barrier, and only then received inescapable shock in the hammock, did not go passive when they were returned to the box. Dogs whose first experience of shock was inescapable did. Seligman and Maier read the pattern as learning: the yoked dog had learned that shock ended whether it acted or not, and so it stopped trying.

The idea left the lab quickly. A person who has spent months unable to affect a boss, a creditor, or a course of illness can look, from the outside, like the yoked dog: capable of the move that would help, and no longer making it. The practical hope the 1967 paper offered was the second experiment. A prior experience of control, even a small one, made later uncontrollable shock less damaging. Teaching someone that a specific action works, before the situation turns hopeless, was the intervention the dogs responded to.

The behavior replicated. The explanation did not survive the authors’ own later work. In 2016 Maier and Seligman wrote that the passivity is the default reaction to a long aversive event, driven by serotonin cells in the dorsal raphe, and that what the brain learns is control, which turns that reaction off. The six dogs who would not jump are still in the data. The claim that they had learned to be helpless is the part the same two authors withdrew.
