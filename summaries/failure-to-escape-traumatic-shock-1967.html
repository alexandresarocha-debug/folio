The pattern the name was invented for

Failure to escape traumatic shock, published by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier in 1967, is the paper that put learned helplessness into the literature. Dogs that had been given inescapable shocks later failed to escape shocks they could have avoided by jumping a low barrier. Dogs that had been given shocks they could escape learned the jump quickly. The difference was not in the pain of the first session alone, because both groups had been shocked. The difference was in whether any action of the animal had been allowed to end the shock. Seligman and Maier proposed that the inescapable group had learned that its actions did not matter, and that this learning transferred to a new box in which action would have mattered a great deal. Passivity, on that proposal, was knowledge.

The paper is an animal experiment of a kind that a modern reader has to approach with the ethics in view from the first paragraph. The card on Folio says the original experiments raise concerns that would make them hard to approve today, and that sentence belongs at the start. Traumatic shock, inescapable by design, inflicted in order to produce a later failure, is a procedure whose scientific yield has to be weighed against a harm that later standards of animal research would be very reluctant to license. The 1967 paper does not read as if that weighing were its subject. A current walkthrough has to supply the weighing the paper omits, and still describe, accurately, what the authors thought they had shown.

Folio's card, Learned helplessness, turned upside down, uses this paper as the behavioural origin and the 2016 review by the same authors as the revision of its meaning. The walkthrough here stays with the origin: the comparison, the proposed learning, and the limits of what a single dramatic transfer can prove. The revision is acknowledged so that a reader does not leave thinking the 1967 interpretation is still the authors' interpretation.

Escape, no escape, and the barrier that came later

The logic of the design is a comparison between animals for whom shock could be switched off by their own response and animals for whom it could not. The second group is the one the theory is about. During the first phase, struggle and movement made no difference to the duration of the shock. In the later test, a low barrier gave a simple way out, a jump that other dogs learned readily. The previously helpless dogs often did not make the jump, or made it late and poorly. They endured a shock that, in the new apparatus, was no longer the kind of shock they had learned about. Transfer of the wrong lesson is the result that gave the paper its force. An animal that fails in the same box where it was hurt is an animal that may simply be afraid of that box.

Seligman and Maier stated the expectation in cognitive language that was bold for an animal paper of that period. The dog had learned that shock was independent of its responses. Independence is a relation, and learning a relation is more than learning a fear of a stimulus. The claim predicts that the deficit should appear wherever the animal has a chance to act and does not take it, and that the deficit should be preventable by prior experience in which action does control shock. The escapable group is that prevention. Those animals, having learned that a response works, did not show the later passivity. Control in the first phase was protective. The paper's title names the failure. The design's other half names the protection, and the protection is what Maier and Seligman would, decades later, decide was the real learning.

The transfer is the detail a summary has to keep, because without it the result collapses into a simpler and less interesting claim, that badly treated animals do badly. The 1967 argument is that a particular kind of bad treatment, uncontrollable bad treatment, produces a particular deficit, a failure to use control when it becomes available. That specificity is what made the finding feel like a model of depression rather than only like a model of fear. Depressed people, the analogy ran, sometimes fail to leave situations they could leave, and the failure looks like a conviction that leaving will not help. The analogy was always an analogy. Dogs jumping or not jumping a barrier are evidence about dogs in that procedure. The human story was an interpretation laid on top, immensely influential, and already a step beyond the data.

What the authors later decided the pattern meant

In 2016 Maier and Seligman published a review whose subtitle promises insights from neuroscience and whose substance withdraws the 1967 slogan. Passivity in the face of prolonged aversive events, they now argue, is the default mammalian response, mediated by serotonin neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus. It does not have to be learned. What is learned, when an animal has control, is that control, detected by a circuit involving the medial prefrontal cortex, which then suppresses the default passivity. The dogs who had escaped in the first phase had learned controllability, and the learning inhibited a brainstem response that would otherwise have kept them from jumping. The dogs who had not escaped had not acquired the inhibitor. Their failure in the second phase looked like a learned expectation of helplessness. On the revised account it was the unbraked default.

The revision changes the sentence a teacher should write on the board, and it does not change the need to describe the procedure. Students still have to know that one group could turn the shock off and one group could not, and that the second group later failed at an easy escape. That is the phenomenon. Learned helplessness remains a reasonable name for the phenomenon as it appears to an observer. It is, in the authors' present view, a misleading name for the mechanism. Folio's card is built to hold both facts. Anyone reading the 1967 paper from the card is reading a classic whose interpretation has been replaced by the people who wrote it, which is a stronger form of correction than a criticism from outside.

There is a temptation, once the mechanism is reversed, to lose interest in the original report. The temptation should be resisted, because the report is what makes the reversal intelligible. A reader who has not pictured the barrier, the two histories, and the claim about independence of action will not see what it means to say that control, rather than helplessness, was the content of the learning. The 2016 paper is written for readers who already carry that picture. The 1967 paper is the picture. They are a pair, and the pair is the card.

Limits that were present from the start

Even under the original interpretation, the paper was a study of a procedure, not a theory of every human passivity. The shocks were traumatic, the subjects were dogs, the escape was a single trained response, and the analogy to depression was an invitation to research rather than a completed clinical argument. Later human work on explanatory style, and Seligman's 1990 book Learned Optimism, took the invitation and built a popular and applied psychology on it, including programmes for young people and, more controversially, resilience training connected with the US Army. Those developments inherit the 1967 phenomenon and the 1967 story about what the phenomenon meant. After 2016 they inherit a phenomenon whose story has changed. A programme justified by the claim that people learn helplessness, and must be talked out of it, is a different programme from one justified by the claim that people learn control, and need experiences in which control is real.

The ethical limit does not shrink because the theory became famous or because the theory was later revised. A finding can be important and still be a finding one would not now seek by the same means. The card's note that the experiments would be hard to approve today is the right companion to any admiration of the design's logic. Design logic is about what comparison isolates controllability. It is silent on whether the isolation was worth the suffering used to achieve it. A walkthrough that praises the comparison and skips the suffering has taken the paper's own emphasis and mistaken it for a complete response. The complete response includes the phenomenon, the withdrawn interpretation, the neural revision, and the fact that the phenomenon was produced by harm.

Replication, in the sense Folio's replication cards worry about, is a slightly different question here. The basic difference between animals with and without prior control was repeated enough, across the years of this research programme, to become a standard preparation rather than a one-time surprise. What failed to stay still was the meaning. That is a reminder that a robust behavioural effect can carry a mistaken theory for a long time, especially when the theory is vivid and the alternative requires a circuit most readers cannot see. The 1967 paper offered a vivid theory. The 2016 paper asks readers to keep the effect and change the theory. Both requests belong in any honest account of the first report.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Learned helplessness, turned upside down, begins with this experiment. Inescapable shock was followed by a failure to escape when escape was easy. Escapable shock was followed by quick learning. The authors' first explanation, that the passive animals had learned the futility of action, is the explanation they themselves set aside in 2016, in favour of a default passivity and a learned control that suppresses it. Read the 1967 paper for the comparison that created the field, and read it with the ethical reservation the card insists on. Read Learned Optimism for the human and popular development of the first explanation. Read the 2016 review for the reversal that gives the card its title. The old paper remains the place where the barrier, and the two histories on either side of it, are set out as a finding.
