Background

Simmons and Simonsohn had concluded, in 2017, that the published evidence for power posing's behavioural and hormonal effects lacked evidential value. A p-curve of those results did not have the shape a true effect tends to leave behind it. Amy Cuddy, S. Jack Schultz and Nathan Fosse replied the next year, in the same journal, with a paper whose title is the reply. P-Curving a More Comprehensive Body of Research on Postural Feedback Reveals Clear Evidential Value for Power-Posing Effects. The move is in the adjective. More comprehensive means a different set of studies from the set that had just been curved, a wider tradition of postural feedback rather than only the papers that looked like the original hormone-and-risk bundle. Folio's card presents the pair as the two sides a reader should actually read, and it records the reduced claim the reply is in a position to defend. An effect on self-reported feelings of power may be real. The original physiological story is not what this defence restores.

The question

If the literature is defined more broadly, as studies of postural feedback rather than as direct copies of one famous experiment, do the significant results then show evidential value? Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse are not rerunning Ranehill's study, and they are not denying that a p-curve can accuse a literature of being a selected set of flukes. They are arguing that Simmons and Simonsohn drew the boundary of the literature in a way that omitted much of the relevant evidence, and that a boundary drawn around postural feedback as a whole yields a curve with the shape of a real effect. The question is technical and political at once. Technical, because evidential value is a property of a specified set of p-values. Political, because the reputation of a claim depends on which set the field agrees to call the claim.

How they redrew the set

The method is the same method, aimed at a larger collection. Studies in which a posture was manipulated, and in which an outcome relevant to power, feeling, behaviour or physiology was measured, are assembled under the heading of postural feedback. From the significant results in that collection a p-curve is drawn, and the curve is compared with the flat or near-flat shape that selective reporting of noise would produce. If the new curve rises towards the small p-values, the authors can say that this broader literature has evidential value. If it does not, the reply fails on its own terms. The honesty of the exercise depends on the rule of inclusion being a rule about posture and outcome, fixed so that it gathers the supportive studies and the awkward ones for a reason one could state in advance.

Comprehensive is a word that has to earn itself. A set can be larger without being fairer, if the additions are loosely related findings chosen because they point the right way, and if the failures remain outside because they were never published or because they used a slightly different label. Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse's case is that postural feedback is a genuine research tradition, older and wider than the 2010 paper, and that judging power posing only against its most famous and most ambitious version throws away evidence about the postural piece. The reader does not have to grant the case to see what the manoeuvre is. It changes the estimand. The thing being given evidential value is no longer exactly the thing Ranehill failed to replicate.

What they concluded

The conclusion announced in the title is that the broader body of research shows clear evidential value for power-posing effects. On their curve, the significant results are distributed in a way that selective publication of pure noise would be unlikely to produce. Something in the postural manipulations, they argue, is being detected often enough, and strongly enough, that the literature as they define it is not a mirage of p-values pressed against the threshold. The card's gloss is more specific than the title, and it is the gloss a careful reader should keep. The effect for which the continuing defence is most plausible is an effect on self-reported feelings of power. People who adopt expansive postures report feeling more powerful than people who adopt contracted ones, and that contrast, across studies, is what the revised claim rests on.

The gloss matters because evidential value is not a resurrection. A curve can support the existence of some effect in a broad set and say nothing about testosterone, nothing about cortisol, and nothing about whether a two-minute pose changes a risky choice. Those were the effects that made the original paper a public event. They were the effects Ranehill's sample did not find. They were the effects whose literature Simmons and Simonsohn had judged to lack evidential value. A reply that establishes a feeling of power, and is then described as a reply that re-establishes power posing, has slid from one claim to another. Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse are entitled to the claim their set supports. The card's entitlement is the smaller one. Some evidence supports a subjective effect. The strong story does not come back.

How to read a reply that changes the question

Changing the question can be a correction or a retreat, and the difference is whether the new question is the one the evidence always justified. There is a good version of this reply. The original paper overreached. The right scientific object was never a hormonal transformation in two minutes. It was a modest embodied influence on subjective power, visible across many manipulations of posture, and easy to over-interpret. On that version, Simmons and Simonsohn curved the overreach, Ranehill buried it, and Cuddy and colleagues returned the field to the claim it should have made. There is a less good version. The famous claim lost, and the boundary of the literature was widened until a different claim, sharing a word, could be said to have evidential value. A reader can hold the good version as a possibility and still require the reply to say, in its own voice, which outcomes the curve is about.

The paper does make a case that feelings of power are the robust piece, and that case is why the card does not score the episode as a simple demolition. A small, real shift in a self-report is a legitimate scientific finding. It is a poor foundation for advice about job interviews, and a poor foundation for a story about testosterone. The dignity of the reply is in the reduction. Its risk is that the reduction will be blurred in the retelling, so that evidential value for a feeling is heard as vindication of the pose as originally sold.

Limits

The limits of any p-curve travel with the reply. The curve sees published significant results. It does not see the file drawer, except by inference from the shape of what was published. A broader set includes more outcomes, some of them softer than a hormone assay, and softer outcomes are easier to move with expectation. Participants know what an expansive pose is supposed to mean. Asked how powerful they feel, they may report the meaning. Evidential value for that report is not evidence that a pose changed a person in any way a camera, a gamble or a saliva sample would have noticed. The 2018 paper can be right about its curve and still leave the mechanism of the feeling unsolved.

There is a social limit the card is right to mention. The exchange around power posing was not only a exchange of curves. It became a public argument, with an audience that does not read p-curves and a cost, borne unevenly, by the researchers who were turned into characters. A reply written in that weather has a job the method does not describe, which is to defend a person's scientific standing as well as a distribution. Readers can recognise that job and still grade the distribution on the distribution's terms. The terms are: which studies, which outcomes, which shape, and which claim is licensed when the shape is kind.

What remains of the effect

What remains, on the card's account, is a possible small effect on feeling powerful, and a clear failure of the effects that were supposed to sit underneath it. Hormones do not reliably change. Risk-taking does not reliably change. The original first author does not stand by the original claim. A comprehensive curve can still be right that postural studies, as a crowd, are detecting something in the realm of felt power. That something is the residue. It is worth knowing about if one studies embodiment, because a residue is a clue. It is not worth prescribing as a technique for changing one's chemistry before a meeting. The distance between a clue and a technique is the distance this whole card is trying to keep open.

The reply's service is to stop the residue from being denied out of momentum. A controversy can swing from credulity to a refusal to admit any effect at all, including the effect the better evidence actually supports. Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse are the correction to that swing, if they are read at the size of their evidence. Read larger than that, they become a way of saying the critics lost, which is not what a curve about feelings is able to say.

Why it matters here

Folio's card ends the power-posing case by asking the reader to hold two papers at once. Simmons and Simonsohn p-curve the evidence for the strong effects and find it wanting. Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse p-curve a wider postural literature and find evidential value, concentrated, in the reading the card trusts, on self-reported feelings of power. Neither paper is a posing session. Both are arguments about what the published record is evidence of. Read them after Ranehill, not instead of Ranehill, because the large sample is what broke the hormonal and behavioural claims in a single stroke. Read this reply for the best version of what a defender can still say, and for practice in noticing when a comprehensive literature is a smaller claim wearing a larger name.
