Amy Cuddy, Jack Schultz, and Nathan Fosse answered the 2017 p-curve with a larger one. Joseph Simmons and Uri Simonsohn had judged an earlier set of power-posing studies and found no evidential value for effects beyond the feeling of power. Cuddy, Schultz, and Fosse describe that earlier set as 34 studies. A systematic search added 21 more, for 55 studies that met their rules. On that broader collection, they reported, the p-curve bent the way a real effect bends: more very small p-values than p-values perched just under .05. They ran the test on the main selection of statistics and again on a robustness selection, and they reported evidential value both times. The curve was not left-skewed, which would have been the signature of p-hacking severe enough to show up in the shape.

The sharpest result was a single outcome, feelings of power. For that curve the right skew was strong, and the estimated average power of the included studies was 58 percent, higher than the estimate for the mixture of all effects. They also reported evidential value for a wider band of feelings: emotion, mood, and evaluations of the self. Their claim is that once the search is wider and more recent than the list Simmons and Simonsohn inherited, postural feedback has a signal, and the signal is clearest when people are asked how they feel.

The argument is about which outcome counts. Simmons and Simonsohn had treated feelings of power as a manipulation check and had judged the downstream claims, behavior and hormones, to lack evidential value. The strongest curve in the 2018 reply is the feelings curve. That split matches what Eva Ranehill and colleagues found in a blinded experiment with 200 people: self-reported power moved, and testosterone, cortisol, and risk-taking did not. A p-curve can show that a literature contains some real signal and still leave the famous claim, two minutes of posture changes your hormones and your gambles, without a defense.

If the decision is whether to coach a team to stand in a victory pose, this paper is evidence about self-report. People who adopt an expansive posture often say they feel more powerful, and that is the pattern these authors could defend. Feeling powerful is a different claim from being more accurate, more persuasive, or hormonally changed. The smaller claim is the one their curve secured.
