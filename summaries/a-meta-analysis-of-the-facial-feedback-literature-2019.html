After the failed replication, Nicholas Coles, Jeff Larsen and Heather Lench asked what the whole body of research says. The last full meta-analysis of facial feedback had been done three decades earlier. They gathered studies that manipulated facial feedback in any way, from posed expressions to hidden techniques like the pen, and collected people's own reports of emotion.

Their final sample included 98 reports containing 138 studies and 286 effect sizes. Notably, 20% of the effect sizes came from unpublished sources such as dissertations and unpublished data, which helps reduce the bias towards positive results.

Overall, facial feedback had a significant but small effect on self-reported emotion: d = 0.20, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.14 to 0.26. The effects also varied. Of 12 possible moderators, three made a difference. Facial feedback affected judgments of a stimulus, such as how funny a cartoon is, more strongly than reports of one's own feelings. Effects on feelings were larger when there was no emotional stimulus at all, and, when there was one, larger for some kinds of stimuli, such as emotional sentences, than for others, such as pictures.

Publication bias told a split story. Three detection methods found no evidence of bias in studies of emotional experience, but all three found evidence of bias in studies of affective judgments, the very kind of measure used in the pen study.

What this means in real life: changing your expression probably does nudge your feelings a little, but it is a small push, not a reliable mood switch.

Caution: meta-analyses combine studies of uneven quality, and the effects were heterogeneous. Pooling different methods may hide which ones work and which do not; Coles went on to lead the Many Smiles Collaboration, a large joint test designed to settle that.
