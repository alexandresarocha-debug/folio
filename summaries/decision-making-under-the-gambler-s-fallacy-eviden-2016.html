Most studies of the gambler's fallacy look at bets. Daniel Chen, Tobias Moskowitz and Kelly Shue asked whether experienced professionals, making serious decisions one case after another, also expect streaks to reverse. If they do, a person's fate can depend on whether the case before theirs went one way or the other.

They looked at three settings where cases arrive in a fairly random order. In US asylum courts, judges were up to 3.3 percentage points more likely to reject the current case if they had approved the previous one. The authors estimate that about 2% of decisions were reversed purely by the order of earlier decisions. The effect was stronger after a longer run of decisions in the same direction, and among judges with moderate overall approval rates.

In loan files reviewed by officers in a field experiment run by other researchers, decisions showed the same negative pattern, strongest after two decisions in the same direction. Education, age, experience and spending longer on the file reduced it. It was stronger when the incentives for accuracy were weaker.

In Major League Baseball, where tracking data record exactly where each pitch crossed the plate, umpires were 1.5 percentage points less likely to call a strike if they had called the previous pitch a strike, holding the location fixed. The effect more than doubled for pitches close to the edge of the strike zone.

What this means in real life: anyone who judges a queue of cases, such as job applications, exam scripts or grant proposals, can be nudged by the last few decisions. Mixing the order or judging in batches can help.

Caution: the effects are small on average, and the authors cannot always tell whether a given decision was right. They test and argue against other explanations, such as quotas or mood, but the gambler's fallacy remains their interpretation of a pattern in the data.

Source. Chen, D. L., Moskowitz, T. J., and Shue, K. (2016). Decision making under the gambler's fallacy: evidence from asylum judges, loan officers, and baseball umpires. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 131(3), 1181-1242. doi:10.1093/qje/qjw017. Working paper: https://www.nber.org/papers/w22026
