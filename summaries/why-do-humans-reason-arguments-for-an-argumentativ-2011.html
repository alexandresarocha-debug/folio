Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber argue that reasoning evolved for argument. The number they lean on comes from a card problem people almost always miss alone. In the Wason selection task, you see four cards and a rule such as "if there is a vowel on one side, there is an even number on the other," and you must turn the cards that could show the rule is false. Alone, people get this right about 10 percent of the time. The study they cite, by David Moshman and Molly Geil in 1998, gave the problem to 143 undergraduates: 32 working alone, and 20 groups of five or six. Of the people working alone, 9.4 percent chose the two right cards. Seven of the ten groups who only worked together got it right. Eight of the ten groups whose members had first tried alone got it right. The transcripts did not look like the clever person announcing the answer. People changed their minds after they had been argued out of the card they wanted to turn first.

A second result in the same paper shows the bias turning around when someone has a reason to attack the rule. Mercier and Sperber describe a 2002 study in which the rule was a stereotype about the participant's own group. People who had a motive to show that the rule was false picked the right cards more than half the time. In the other conditions, fewer than one person in five did. The mind that will not falsify its own hunch will work hard to falsify someone else's.

That is a practical fact about meetings, hiring, and medical hunches. A reason you invent in order to defend a conclusion you already like is a weak test of that conclusion. The same person is stricter about a reason that arrives from outside. A decision gets a real check when someone who does not already agree has to answer it, and when the answer is an argument rather than a show of hands. A room of people who started in the same place can produce a very confident mistake. A short disagreement, with the other side free to say why a reason fails, is where the weak reason gets caught.

Source. Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber, 2011, "Why Do Humans Reason? Arguments for an Argumentative Theory," Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 34(2), 57–74. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X10000968
