Background

By 2011 the catalogue of reasoning's failures was long and slightly embarrassing. People confirm their hypotheses, neglect base rates, attack the other side's evidence more keenly than their own, and solve classic logic problems badly when they sit alone. The standard moral was that reasoning is a flawed instrument for discovering truth, in need of debiasing. Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber asked a prior question, in a target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences. Why would an instrument like that have evolved? Their answer is the argumentative theory of reasoning. Reasoning looks broken as a solitary path to truth because that was never its main job. Its job is to produce arguments that will persuade other people, and to evaluate the arguments other people produce.

The article is the scholarly original of the view Folio's confirmation-bias card borrows from their later book. Confirmation bias, on this account, is not an embarrassing leak in a truth-seeking machine. It is what a machine for advocacy does when it is switched on in private. The article's form matters as much as its thesis. A target article in that journal is written to be shot at, and the commentaries are part of the event.

The question

What is reasoning for? Mercier and Sperber distinguish reasoning from inference. Inference, as they use the word, is the broad production of new representations from old ones, most of it unconscious, fast and good enough to keep a person alive. Reasoning is the narrower activity of attending to reasons, of finding a consideration that supports a conclusion and of inspecting a consideration someone else has offered. If that narrower activity evolved to improve solitary judgement, its well-documented biases are a puzzle. If it evolved for social argument, the same biases are a prediction. A good advocate finds reasons for the side she is on. A good audience finds the holes in a case someone else is trying to sell. The article asks whether the evidence lines up with that division of labour.

How the argument is set up

The method is a reorganisation of existing findings, not a new experiment. Mercier and Sperber assemble the biases that make solitary reasoning look incompetent, with confirmation bias and myside bias at the front. A person asked to generate reasons generates reasons for the opinion already held. A person asked to test a hypothesis proposes cases the hypothesis survives, which is Wason's lesson in one of its forms. The same person, handed someone else's argument, becomes suddenly acute. Flaws that were invisible in one's own case are obvious in a neighbour's. The theory treats this asymmetry as functional. Persuasion rewards the production of supportive reasons. Protection against deception rewards the evaluation of other people's reasons. Neither function rewards a private, even-handed search for the best view, conducted for an audience of one.

They then turn the prediction around. If reasoning is for argument, it should work better in argument. Groups that disagree, and that share a stake in getting the answer right, should correct errors that individuals miss, because each member is motivated to strike at the others' weak reasons and to defend her own only as far as they will withstand the strike. The article gathers evidence that discussion does improve performance on problems individuals botch, including logical problems of the sort associated with Wason's selection task. The card's deep dive records that prediction and the evidence offered for it. The 2011 paper is where the prediction is stated as a theory rather than as a moral about committees.

What the theory claims

The claim is functional and psychological together. Functionally, reasoning evolved in a social species that already had communication, and that therefore already had the problem of whom to believe. A capacity specialised for producing and assessing reasons fits that problem better than a capacity specialised for private philosophical hygiene. Psychologically, the claim is that the normal input to reasoning is a conclusion the person is already inclined to draw, or a reason somebody else has put on the table. Reasoning does not typically open the enquiry. Intuitive inference opens it. Reasoning arrives to justify, to attack, or to yield.

Confirmation bias is thereby relocated. In solitary thought it produces the familiar one-sided case, and it can entrench error, because nobody is paid to answer back. In a genuine argument it is half of a good arrangement, provided the other half shows up. Each side makes the best case it can. The evaluation is outsourced to people who do not share the case. The theory's practical sentence, which the card adopts, is that reasoning works best in groups that argue in good faith. Good faith is doing real work in that sentence. An argument with no shared interest in the truth, or with no permission to contradict, is not the environment the theory says reasoning was built for. It is a theatre of reasons, and the theory does not promise that theatre will be wise.

How to read a target article

A target article is an invitation, and this one was taken up. Dozens of commentaries pressed on the evolutionary story, on the distinction between inference and reasoning, and on whether the group results really show a specialisation for argument rather than a more familiar benefit of extra time and extra minds. Mercier and Sperber's reply is part of the publication. A reader who wants the thesis at full strength should read the criticism beside it, because the thesis is large. It offers to explain the purpose of a faculty. Large offers are supposed to attract precise objections, and the value of the 2011 paper includes the objections it was built to receive.

The right expectation is not a proof of an evolutionary history. Nobody observed the selection. The expectation is a fit between a proposed function and a pattern of performance: poor where the function is unsupported, better where the function has its partner. Where that fit is loose, the commentaries say so. Where the fit is tight, solitary bias plus social acuity, the theory earns the attention the card gives it.

Limits

The theory can be too hospitable. Once every success of a group is attributed to argument and every failure of an individual to the absence of argument, the account explains the literature it was built from and risks explaining the next finding in advance. A group can also amplify a shared bias, pool ignorance, or follow its most confident member. Mercier and Sperber know that bad groups exist. Their claim is that the mechanism of reasoning predicts improvement under conditions of genuine conflict and a common goal, not that every meeting is such a condition. A reader should keep the conditions attached to the optimism.

There is a limit of evidence as well. The article is an argument about other people's experiments. It inherits their ambiguities. A discussion effect on a logic puzzle may be argumentative specialisation, or it may be the ordinary advantage of hearing an answer one did not think of. The theory prefers the first reading and offers reasons. It does not, by the nature of a review, run the decisive separation. The book that followed is where the same authors slow down and answer at the length a target article cannot afford.

What the article leaves in play

Two questions stay open, and they are the ones a careful reader should leave with. First, whether reasoning has only the social function, or also a private one that the theory underweights. A person alone can sometimes change her mind by thinking, and a theory of argument has to say what that episode is, if it is not a leftover use of a social tool. Mercier and Sperber's answer is that private reasoning is mostly the production of reasons for a conclusion already reached, which fits confirmation bias and fits less well the occasions of real solitary revision. Second, whether the evolutionary claim is necessary. The psychological pattern, myside production and other-side evaluation, can be true and useful even if the history that produced it is told differently. The card needs the psychological pattern. The history is the article's ambition, and it is the part the commentaries contested most freely.

Why it matters here

The confirmation-bias card is not only a warning. It is an argument about what the warning is for, and this paper is that argument in its first full form. Wason showed a person failing to eliminate a narrow hypothesis about 2, 4, 6. Nickerson mapped the failure across the institutions that were supposed to be exempt. Mercier and Sperber say the failure is the individual use of a social capacity. The practical implication the card draws, that reasoning works best when people argue in good faith with someone who is allowed to answer, is an implication of this theory rather than a slogan laid on top of it. Read the 2011 article for the claim and for the shape of the debate it started. Read The Enigma of Reason for the version written as a book, where the same idea has room to answer its critics.
