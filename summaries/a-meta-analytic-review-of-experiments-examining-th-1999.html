A review with a specific question about rewards

Edward Deci, Richard Koestner and Richard Ryan published their meta-analytic review of experiments on extrinsic rewards and intrinsic motivation in 1999. The question is narrow enough to be answered and broad enough to matter. When people are already interested in an activity, what do rewards do to that interest? The folk answer is that rewards add a reason and therefore add motivation. A long experimental tradition, beginning with studies such as Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's work with preschool drawings, had found that some rewards seem to subtract interest instead. Children who expected a certificate for drawing later chose to draw less in free time. Deci, Koestner and Ryan gather the experiments that followed, one hundred and twenty-eight of them, and ask which rewards do this, on which measures, and how reliably.

Folio's card states their conclusion in the form a reader needs. Expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards reliably undermined intrinsic motivation. Verbal praise tended to enhance it. The rest of the review is the work of showing that this sentence is a summary of a pattern, not a slogan about money. Rewards differ. Measures differ. A meta-analysis is the right instrument because the literature had become a pile of single studies, each with its own task and its own way of paying, and because the dispute about the pile had become sharp. Eisenberger and Cameron had argued that the negative effects of rewards were limited and avoidable. The 1999 review is the self-determination tradition's systematic reply.

What counts as a reward, and what counts as interest

The review's power is in its distinctions. A reward can be tangible, a certificate, a dollar, a prize, or it can be verbal, a word of praise. It can be expected, announced before the activity, or unexpected, arriving as a surprise after the person has already acted from interest. It can be contingent on merely doing the task, on finishing it, or on doing it to a stated standard. These are different messages. An expected prize for agreeing to draw tells the child that drawing is the sort of thing one does for a prize. An unexpected comment that the drawing is good tells the child something about competence, after the drawing has already been done for its own sake. Collapsing both into the word reward is how the debate became confused, and the meta-analysis refuses the collapse.

Intrinsic motivation is measured, in this literature, in more than one way, and the review keeps the ways separate. The classic behavioural measure is free choice: when the reward is no longer on offer and the person is left with the activity among other options, do they return to it? The other family of measures is self-reported interest. A reward could change what people say and leave what they do alone, or the reverse. Deci, Koestner and Ryan's conclusion about undermining is tied most securely to the free-choice behaviour that follows expected tangible rewards contingent on the task. That is the result the card compresses into the word reliably. Praise is the contrasting case, more often positive, and more naturally read as information about competence than as a controlling payment. The theory's claim is that the meaning of the reward, controlling versus informing, is what the later interest follows.

The pattern, and the argument it was written to settle

Across the 128 experiments, the undermining effect of expected tangible task-contingent rewards is the finding that survives the averaging. Studies differ in size, in the age of the participants, and in the charm of the task, and an average is a way of asking whether the effect is a quirk of the famous preschool study or a feature of the design those studies share. The review's answer is that the feature is shared. When people expect a tangible reward for doing an interesting task, their later intrinsic motivation is lower than that of people who did the task without that expectation. The preschool certificate is a memorable instance. It is, on this review, an instance with a literature behind it.

Verbal praise tends the other way. Being told that one has done well, in the studies that looked, is more often followed by maintained or increased interest. Self-determination theory reads the contrast as a contrast in needs. Praise can support competence, the sense of being effective, without relocating the cause of the action outside the person. An expected tangible reward can relocate the cause, which frustrates autonomy, the sense that one endorses the action. Ryan and Deci's 2000 paper, the short introduction Folio's card recommends, puts this reading at the centre of the wider theory. The meta-analysis is the reason the reading has numbers behind it, and the reason a critic has to argue with a distribution of experiments rather than with a single clever afternoon in a nursery.

Eisenberger and Cameron's critique is the argument the review is answering, and an honest summary keeps the answer from sounding like a silencing. They held that rewards' negative effects were narrower than the self-determination tradition claimed, and that many practical rewards could be used without damage if they were tied to standards and delivered as information. Some of the distinctions in the 1999 review actually give that critique a place to stand. Unexpected rewards, and rewards that are not the controlling kind, are not the villains of the analysis. The reply is that once the villains are correctly named, expected tangible task-contingent rewards, the undermining is reliable rather than fragile. A practitioner who needs a rule can take the rule at that resolution. A practitioner who has only heard that rewards kill creativity has heard a blur of the review, and the blur is less useful than the distinctions.

What a meta-analysis of this era can and cannot lock down

One hundred and twenty-eight experiments is a large pile, and a pile of published experiments has the habits of its time. Studies that found a clear effect were easier to publish. Tasks were often short and interesting, chosen so that intrinsic motivation had somewhere to fall from. Participants were often students or children, for whom a certificate or a small payment is a large slice of the reason they might draw or puzzle. Generalising to wages, to jobs people do not find interesting in the first place, and to rewards that are the only way a household pays rent, is a further step. The review is at its strongest as a statement about interesting activities and about the free-choice period after an expected tangible reward. Employment contracts lie beyond that statement. Ryan and Deci have always been clearer about this boundary than the popular versions of their theory, which sometimes speak as if the nursery result had abolished pay.

The measures have their own limits. Free choice in a laboratory is a brief window, and a child who draws less for a few minutes has not revealed a permanent loss of love for art. Self-report can move because the person is explaining their behaviour in the way the reward made salient, which is a real change in the experience and a small one. The review's claim is about a reliable directional effect on the measures the literature used. Reliable and directional is the right size of claim. It supports the card's warning about expected tangible rewards. It does not license a prophecy about every bonus in every firm. Verbal praise, likewise, enhances interest in the studies that tested the kinds of praise on offer, which were often informational. Praise that is controlling, comparative, or attached to a threat is a different message, and the theory itself predicts that the message matters.

Later decades have made every meta-analysis of this vintage a candidate for a second look, and the candidate status should be named. Analytic flexibility, selective publication, and small studies are the worries Ioannidis, and then Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn, pressed on the field. A review that finds a coherent pattern across many experiments is more informative than any one of those experiments, and it is still a review of the published record those worries are about. The undermining effect has been cited for decades because the pattern was consistent enough to survive its critics in 1999. Consistency in a literature is evidence of one kind. A preregistered confirmation is evidence of another. Folio's card reports the 1999 conclusion as the literature's considered pattern, and it notes that the effect was contested. Both halves are the right way to carry the review into the present.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness, uses this meta-analysis as the backing for its most practical warning. Expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards undermined intrinsic motivation across a large set of experiments, one hundred and twenty-eight of them. Verbal praise tended to enhance it. The 1973 drawing study is the story form of the first half. Ryan and Deci's 2000 article is the theory that says why the two halves differ: one message controls, the other informs, and the needs for autonomy and competence are what those messages touch. Read the review when you want the distinctions among rewards, because the distinctions are the result. A single word, reward, hides the pattern. The pattern is what a parent, a teacher, or a manager can actually use, and it is narrower, and more dependable, than the slogan that payment and interest cannot live in the same room.
