In 1999 Edward Deci, Richard Koestner, and Richard Ryan pulled together 128 experiments on a question parents, teachers, and managers keep asking. If you pay someone, or give them a prize, for an activity they already find interesting, do they want it less once the prize stops? The experiments measured intrinsic motivation in two ways. One was free-choice behavior: how long a person returned to the task when nobody was watching and nothing was offered. The other was a self-report of interest and enjoyment.

Tangible rewards that people had been led to expect reduced free-choice time. Rewards for merely taking part had an effect of d = -0.40. Rewards for finishing had d = -0.36. Rewards for doing well had d = -0.28. The first two also lowered reported interest, by smaller amounts, d = -0.15 and d = -0.17. Rewards for doing well did not clearly lower reported interest. Words went the other way. Positive feedback raised free-choice behavior, d = 0.33, and raised reported interest, d = 0.31. Tangible rewards tended to do more damage among children than among college students. Verbal rewards tended to help college students more than they helped children. The studies came from 94 published articles and 19 dissertations. One hundred one of them included a free-choice measure, and 84 included a self-report of interest.

The cut that matters in ordinary life is the one those averages force. A promised prize for doing an activity a person already likes is the arrangement most likely to shrink the time they spend on it later. A specific comment that the work was good tended, in this literature, to increase that time. A sales contest, a reading chart that ends in a toy, or a completion bonus on a hobby sit on the first side. "You got that problem, and here is what worked" sits on the second. The meta-analysis is also a warning about averaging every reward into one pile. That pile had been hiding a real drop from expected tangible rewards and a real gain from praise.
