The 1984 Lancet study showed that bacteria and gastritis went together. It could not show which came first. A germ can be found in sick people simply because sick tissue is a good place for it to live. The classic way to test cause is a set of rules named after the German physician Robert Koch, known as Koch's postulates: find the germ in the sick, grow it on its own, give it to a healthy host and see whether the disease follows. For a human-only bacterium, there was no animal to use.

The paper that tried to do this appeared in the Medical Journal of Australia in April 1985, with Marshall as first author and three co-authors, Armstrong, McGechie and Clancy. Its title says attempt, and it describes one volunteer with histologically normal stomach lining, which means that a biopsy showed no gastritis to begin with. He swallowed the bacteria. The volunteer was Marshall himself, who drank a culture of the organism.

The paper's summary is short. A mild illness developed and lasted 14 days. A biopsy on the tenth day after swallowing the bacteria showed gastritis. By the fourteenth day it had largely resolved. The authors described what they called the syndrome of acute pyloric campylobacter gastritis, and proposed that it may progress to a chronic infection which predisposes to peptic ulceration.

It was a bold and risky experiment on one man, and it should be read with that in mind. A single volunteer cannot prove a general claim. What the test did show was that the organism could cause inflammation in a stomach that had none before, which the 1984 data alone could not establish.

What this means in real life: the claim that a germ causes a disease needs more than a link. It needs evidence that the germ comes first and that removing it helps. The Nobel committee later wrote that the case for H. pylori rests on human volunteers, antibiotic treatment studies, and epidemiological studies together. Today, ulcers caused by it are treated with a short course of antibiotics along with acid-reducing drugs.

Caution: self-experiment is not a model to copy. It is ethically fraught and scientifically limited, and the paper's authors present it as an attempt, not as a final proof.
