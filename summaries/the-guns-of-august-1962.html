Barbara Tuchman opens The Guns of August (1962) on 20 May 1910, at the funeral of Edward VII, with nine kings riding in the procession. Four years later those courts were at war, and plans their staffs had written in peacetime were running the governments that had ordered them. The book is the first month of fighting: the invasion, the August battles, and the check to the German advance at the Marne in early September, when a short war became a long one. It won the Pulitzer Prize for general nonfiction in 1963.

Germany's design for a two-front war required a fast sweep through Belgium and a decision in France before Russia could fully arrive. German troops entered Belgium on 4 August 1914 and expected the forts at Liège to fall in days. The forts held into the middle of the month. Heavy siege guns were brought up. On 15 August the magazine at Fort Loncin exploded, and the Belgian commander, General Gérard Leman, was pulled from the ruins. Liège was a delay, not a victory that stopped the invasion. A plan built on speed had already slipped at the first obstacle. The violation of a guaranteed neutral also gave a divided British Cabinet a cause it could take to Parliament.

France's Plan XVII sent armies east under a doctrine that treated attacking spirit as a match for modern rifles. The retreat, and the turn at the Marne, are the book's climax. General Joseph Gallieni moved troops out of Paris in requisitioned taxis, and the taxis became a national legend out of proportion to the few thousand soldiers they carried. What checked the advance was an overstretched German wheel and a French army that had been kept intact. In the east the same month produced Tannenberg, where a Russian army that had attacked early, for the sake of its French ally, was destroyed.

On the evening of 26 October 1962, during the Cuban Missile Crisis, John F. Kennedy told his brother he would not follow a course that let anyone write a comparable book about that week and call it The Missiles of October. He had read Tuchman as an account of timetables eating the room in which politicians might still stop. August 1914 is the month that shows what a mobilization schedule does once it is treated as the policy.

In 1999 Terence Zuber argued, from German staff papers, that the single Schlieffen Plan Tuchman puts at the center, a 1905 design to wheel through Belgium and around Paris, was not the order Helmuth von Moltke issued in 1914. Other historians answered that the army which crossed the Belgian border was still committed to a fast right-wing sweep on a railway schedule. The dispute is about how binding the plan was, not about whether Liège was attacked.
