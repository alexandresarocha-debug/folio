Barbara Tuchman opens The Guns of August (1962) on 20 May 1910, at the funeral of Edward VII, with nine kings riding in the procession. She wants the reader to see a world of dynasties and uniforms that was about to hand the tempo of politics to railway timetables. The German design for a two-front war, Alfred von Schlieffen's memorandum as Helmuth von Moltke the Younger had adapted it, sent the main armies through Belgium so that France could be beaten before the Russian masses were fully in the field. France's Plan XVII sent its armies the other way, toward the lost provinces of Alsace and Lorraine, on a doctrine of the offensive that treated spirit as a substitute for the firepower modern weapons had given the defense. The British Expeditionary Force was small, professional, and committed by staff talks more than by a public treaty to take its place on the French left.

The campaign those plans produced is the book. On 22 August 1914, in the Battle of the Frontiers, 27,000 French soldiers were killed, the worst single day in French military history. The German wheel through Belgium was checked at the Marne from 6 to 12 September. The detail everyone remembers from that battle is the taxis. On the night of 6 September, General Joseph Gallieni sent Paris cabs toward the fighting. They carried about 5,000 soldiers, in a battle fought by more than a million men, and most of those men were held in reserve. The taxis became the picture of the city saving itself. They are a bad measure of why the German advance stopped. What stopped it was a large army that had retreated without breaking, turned around, and struck a wheel that had stretched itself and lost contact with its own commander. After the Marne both sides reached for the sea, and the short war the plans had promised was visibly over. Tuchman stops as that stalemate becomes imaginable.

The book won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction in 1963, and it had a political afterlife inside a year of publication. On the evening of 26 October 1962, during the Cuban Missile Crisis, John F. Kennedy told his brother he was not going to follow a course which would allow anyone to write a comparable book about that time, and he named the book he did not want written: The Missiles of October. General Maxwell Taylor later recalled that what Kennedy took from Tuchman was the rigidity of the 1914 mobilization plans, which left politicians choosing between doing nothing and accepting a timetable. That is a fair use of her month. It is a use of August, after the political decisions of July had already been taken.

Later work has put more of the cause back into those July decisions. Christopher Clark's The Sleepwalkers (2012) treats the handover to the generals as itself a series of political choices. Tuchman's sequence remains the clearest account of what the plans did once they were loose: the violation of Belgium that brought Britain in, a French offensive that met modern rifles, a Russian advance into East Prussia that ended at Tannenberg, and a September battle that saved France from a short German victory. Her portraits of Moltke, Joffre, and Sir John French are why the month is still seen. She works from what those men published about themselves, and she will give a general a mood the archives may not record.
