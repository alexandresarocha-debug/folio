Barbara Tuchman opens The Guns of August (1962) on 20 May 1910, at the funeral of Edward VII, with nine kings riding in the procession. The splendor is the point, and so is the date. Four years later the same courts were at war, and the plans their general staffs had written in peacetime were running the governments that had ordered them. The book is not a history of the July crisis and not a history of the trenches. It is the first month: invasion, the battles of August, and the check to the German advance at the Marne in early September, the moment a short war became a long one. It won the Pulitzer Prize for general nonfiction in 1963.

The campaign she tells in the west begins with a timetable. Germany's design for a two-front war required a fast sweep through Belgium, a holding action in the east, and a decision in France before Russia could fully arrive. Belgium was in the way. German troops entered the country on 4 August 1914 and expected the forts at Liège to fall in short order. The forts held on until the middle of the month. Heavy siege guns were brought up. On 15 August the magazine at Fort Loncin exploded, and the Belgian commander, General Gérard Leman, was taken out of the ruins. Liège was a delay of days, not a victory that stopped the invasion. Tuchman's point is what days were worth. A plan built on speed had already slipped at the first obstacle, and the political fact traveled with the military one. The violation of a neutral country, guaranteed on paper, gave a divided British Cabinet a cause it could take to Parliament.

The month's other face is the French offensive. Plan XVII sent armies east into the lost provinces and into German fire, under a doctrine that treated attacking spirit as a substitute for the advantage modern weapons had given the defense. The retreat that followed, and the turn at the Marne, are the book's climax. General Joseph Gallieni moved troops out of Paris in requisitioned taxis, and the taxis became a national legend. They carried a few thousand soldiers. The battle was fought by armies numbered in the hundreds of thousands. What checked the advance was a tired, overstretched German wheel and a French army that had been kept in being through the retreat. After the Marne both sides raced toward the sea, and the trench line became imaginable. In the east the same logic produced Tannenberg, at the end of August, where a Russian army that had attacked early for the sake of its French ally was destroyed.

The part of the book that entered policy was the warning about plans. On the evening of 26 October 1962, during the Cuban Missile Crisis, John F. Kennedy told his brother he would not follow a course that let anyone write a comparable book about that week and call it The Missiles of October. He had read Tuchman as an account of timetables eating the room in which politicians might still stop. August 1914 is a bad month to study if you want the full argument about who willed the war. It is the month to study if you want to see what a mobilization schedule does once it is treated as the policy.

In 1999 Terence Zuber argued, from the German staff's own papers, that the single Schlieffen Plan Tuchman puts at the center, a 1905 design to wheel through Belgium and around Paris, was not the order Helmuth von Moltke issued in 1914. Other military historians answered that the army which crossed the Belgian border was still committed to a fast sweep on the right, on a railway schedule. The dispute is about how binding the plan was. It is not about whether Liège was attacked.
