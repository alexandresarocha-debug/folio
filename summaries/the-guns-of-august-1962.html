Barbara Tuchman opens on 20 May 1910, at the funeral of Edward VII in London, with nine kings riding in the procession. She is not investigating why the alliances existed. She is watching what happened once the armies started to move in August 1914, and how plans written in peacetime captured the governments that had ordered them. Germany's design for a two-front war required a fast sweep through Belgium, before the Russian army could come fully into play. France's Plan XVII sent the army forward into the lost provinces on the doctrine that attacking spirit would carry the day. The British Expeditionary Force was small, professional, and already committed by staff talks to the French left. On 4 August, Germany invaded Belgium. The violation of a neutral country that Britain had guaranteed gave a divided London cabinet a cause it could take to Parliament.

The month turned on two battles she tells in sequence. At the end of August, at Tannenberg, the Russian Second Army, thrown into East Prussia early for the sake of the French ally, was destroyed, and General Alexander Samsonov shot himself. In the west, the German wheel ran toward Paris and then past it. The Battle of the Marne was fought from 6 to 12 September. On the night of 6 September, General Joseph Gallieni, military governor of Paris, sent about 600 taxis carrying men of the 103rd and 104th infantry regiments toward Nanteuil-le-Haudouin and Silly-le-Long. The French defense ministry's later account puts the number moved at about 5,000. The taxis became the picture of a city saving itself. They were a few thousand soldiers in a battle of more than a million, and a large share of the men they carried were held in reserve. The German advance stopped because the attack had stretched, contact between the German commanders had frayed, and a French army that had been retreating was turned around.

The political lesson she handed to readers is about timetables. Once mobilization was treated as the policy, hesitation looked like sabotage of a schedule, and the schedule produced a war none of the separate plans had described: Britain in because of Belgium, France still in the field after a disastrous August, Russia wrecked in East Prussia, and a trench line instead of a short campaign. During the Cuban missile crisis, in October 1962, John F. Kennedy told his brother he was not going to follow a course that would let anyone write a comparable book about that month and call it The Missiles of October. The book had won the Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction in 1963. Kennedy was using it as a warning that a military plan, once it is allowed to set the clock, eats the political choice.
