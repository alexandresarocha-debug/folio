A history of the first month

Barbara Tuchman’s The Guns of August, published in 1962, covers a narrower slice of the First World War than its reputation sometimes suggests. It is not a history of the causes in the manner of a diplomatic survey, and it is not a history of the trenches. It is a narrative of the war’s opening, from the atmosphere of the last pre-war years through the battles of August 1914 and on to the point, in early September, when the German advance in the west was checked at the Marne and the short war everyone had planned for was visibly over. The book won the Pulitzer Prize. It has remained, for more than sixty years, the account through which more general readers have met the opening campaigns than any other. That popularity is part of what a serious reading has to assess. Tuchman wrote narrative history of a high literary order, from published sources, memoirs, and official histories, and she wrote it with a thesis about miscalculation that later historians have both used and corrected.

The thesis is visible in the construction. She begins not in Sarajevo but at the funeral of Edward VII in 1910, nine kings riding in a cortege that looked, in retrospect, like the last assembly of an older Europe. The anachronism is deliberate. She wants the reader to feel a political world of dynasties, uniforms, and certainties that was about to hand itself over to railway timetables and machine guns. The causes of the war, in the deep sense, are assumed rather than investigated. What she investigates is the conversion of a political crisis into a military one, and the way in which plans written in peacetime captured the governments that had commissioned them. Once mobilisation began, the argument runs, the room for political choice collapsed towards zero, and the generals executed designs that produced, with a kind of horrible efficiency, a war none of the designs had actually envisaged.

The plans

The early chapters are portraits of plans and of the men who believed in them. Germany’s design for a two-front war, the Schlieffen plan as modified by Moltke the younger, required a vast and rapid sweep through Belgium and northern France, a holding action against Russia, and a decision in the west before the Russian masses could fully arrive. The plan treated Belgian neutrality as an obstacle to be kicked aside and treated speed as the condition of survival. French planning, Plan XVII, was an offensive into the lost provinces and beyond, animated by a doctrine that Tuchman renders with open distaste: the cult of the attack, of élan, of the offensive spirit as a substitute for the firepower that modern weapons had given the defence. The British Expeditionary Force was small, professional, and committed, by quiet staff conversations more than by a public treaty, to taking its place on the French left. Russia’s contribution was mass and the promise of an early attack into East Prussia, a promise made for the sake of the French ally and kept at enormous cost.

Tuchman’s gift is the individual inside the plan. Moltke is hesitation wearing a schedule. Joffre is phlegm raised to the level of a strategic asset, a commander whose refusal to be flustered became, in August, as important as any French manoeuvre. Sir John French, the British commander, is a study in the friction between an ally’s expectations and a small army’s caution. The Kaiser flickers between martial poses and late doubts. These portraits are why the book is remembered, and they are also where professional historians have been least comfortable. Tuchman works from what the principals wrote about themselves and from the published record of what they did. She does not work from the full archives that were still opening in 1962, and she has a novelist’s willingness to attribute a mood. The moods are often well chosen. They are not the same thing as a documented deliberation. A reader can trust the sequence of movements more confidently than the inner life of any one general.

Belgium, the frontiers, and the Marne

The campaign narrative begins with the German irruption into Belgium, the resistance at Liège, and the political consequence Tuchman underlines: the violation of a guaranteed neutral country handed the British government, which had been divided, a cause it could take to Parliament and to the country. She does not reduce Britain’s entry to a scrap of paper. She does insist that the scrap mattered, because it converted a balance-of-power calculation into a public injury. On the French frontier she follows the Battles of the Frontiers, the disastrous offensives in which Plan XVII met German armies and modern rifles, and the retreat that followed. The British fight at Mons and the long withdrawal southward are told from the point of view of a small army discovering the scale of the thing it had joined. Joffre’s achievement, in her telling, was to keep a retreating army in being, to dismiss generals who could not manage the retreat, and to be ready to strike when the German wheel presented a flank.

The Battle of the Marne, in early September, is the book’s climax, and Tuchman tells it as a genuine reversal rather than as a legend. The German advance had stretched itself, Moltke had lost touch with his commanders, and the French, reinforced and turned about, struck into the gap that the wheel had opened. She includes the famous taxis of Paris, the civilian cars that carried a portion of a division to the battle, and a careful reader should keep the proportion in mind. The taxis became a symbol of national mobilisation out of all relation to the number of soldiers they moved. The symbol is part of the history of how France understood the Marne. It is a poor guide to the logistics. What decided the battle was the recovery of a large army and the exhaustion and misdirection of the attacker. After the Marne, both sides extended their lines towards the sea in the race that would produce the trench front. Tuchman’s subject ends as that stalemate becomes imaginable. The short war was over. The long war had not yet been understood.

The other front, and the thesis about inevitability

Interleaved with the western story is the catastrophe in East Prussia. Russia’s early offensive, undertaken before her armies were ready, ended at Tannenberg in a defeat that destroyed an army and made the reputations of Hindenburg and Ludendorff. Tuchman tells it as the eastern proof of the same western moral. A plan made for the sake of an ally, executed with insufficient preparation, produced a disaster that the alliance logic had not included in its price. The two campaigns together are her picture of August. Everywhere, governments had surrendered the tempo to general staffs. Everywhere, the staffs executed brilliant and brittle designs. Everywhere, the designs generated consequences, Belgian resistance, British entry, a French recovery, a Russian disaster, that the designers had noted as risks and then declined to treat as reasons to stop.

The thesis has a political afterlife, which is part of why the book is on a card about 1914 rather than only on a shelf of military narratives. During the Cuban Missile Crisis, John F. Kennedy is widely reported to have had Tuchman’s account in mind, and to have wanted to avoid the kind of automatic escalation in which timetables, rather than politicians, drove the next step. The anecdote is second-hand in the way of all such anecdotes, and it has been repeated because it flatters both the book and the president. It is still a fair reading of what Tuchman offered a statesman. She offered a warning that military plans, once treated as the policy, consume the policy. Later historians of 1914, Christopher Clark among them, have pushed the analysis of decision back into July, and have shown that the handover to the timetables was itself a series of political choices, not a cliff the politicians fell off. Tuchman starts nearer the cliff. Her account of the fall remains the most vivid there is, with the limits of vividness. National stereotypes do some of her explanatory work. The German is rigid, the French is recovery and dash, the Russian is brave and unready. The stereotypes are older than she is, and they flatten a war that was more bureaucratic, and more similar across uniforms, than a morality play can afford to admit.

How to read it now

The right use of The Guns of August is as a campaign narrative with a political moral, not as the current state of the archival argument about war guilt. Operational histories have corrected movements, timings, and the relative contribution of particular corps at the Marne. Cultural histories have done more justice to the home fronts she barely enters. Nothing in that subsequent work has made the core sequence dull: a plan to violate Belgium, a plan to attack in the east too soon, a doctrine of the offensive meeting a defence that could kill it, and a September battle that saved France without saving Europe from four years of the consequence. Tuchman’s prose is the reason the sequence is still seen. The price of the prose is a confidence about character and causation that the sources she used cannot always bear. Read her for the shape of the month. Check her when she tells you what a general felt, and when a nationality seems to be doing the work of an explanation.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on The Sleepwalkers places Tuchman as the book about the first month of the war, a Pulitzer-winning narrative that reportedly weighed on Kennedy in 1962, when the lesson he wanted was the lesson about miscalculation. That placement is exact if the distinction of timescales is kept. Clark’s sleepwalkers are the politicians of July, edging into a war by misreading one another. Tuchman’s subject begins when the edging is over and the plans are loose. MacMillan’s The War That Ended Peace covers the long peace whose destruction Tuchman narrates at the moment of impact.
