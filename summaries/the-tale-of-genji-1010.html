A court tale of the eleventh century

The Tale of Genji was written in the early eleventh century by Murasaki Shikibu, a woman at the Heian court in Japan, during the years when she served in the household of the empress. The date that attaches to it in modern lists, around 1010, is a convenience. The tale grew over years, in a court where prose fiction circulated among readers who already shared a deep training in poetry. It is long, in fifty-four chapters in the form readers now use, and it follows several generations. People call it a novel when they want a word for its scale and its attention to inner life. The word it grew under is monogatari, a tale. Keeping both words in mind is the honest way to read it beside a theory like Ian Watt's. It is a masterpiece of prose fiction. It is also a court work, made of seasonal ritual, rank, and the exchange of poems, and those conditions are part of its art.

Murasaki wrote in the vernacular, in the phonetic script associated with women's writing, at a time when learned men were expected to compose in Chinese. The choice of language is already a choice of world. The tale can follow gossip, grief, music, dress, and the small signals by which people of the court harm and comfort one another. It can also carry a large architecture. Hikaru Genji, the Shining Prince, is the son of an emperor by a consort of lower rank, Kiritsubo. She dies young. The boy is too beautiful and too loved to sit easily in the succession, and he is made a commoner, given the name Genji, and set loose in a life of privilege without the highest office. The tale is the record of what such a man does with beauty, talent, and a standing that is slightly off to the side of power.

Genji's life, and the women who give it form

The early chapters establish a pattern the rest of the book will deepen. Genji loves where he should not. His attachment to Fujitsubo, his father's consort, repeats his longing for his dead mother and breaks a law of the court. The secret of that love, a son who will one day sit on the throne with the wrong father publicly assigned to him, is one of the tale's lasting shadows. Alongside the secret runs a different project. Genji finds a child, Murasaki, who resembles the woman he cannot openly have, and he brings her up to be his companion and wife. The tenderness of that raising and the offence of it are both in the prose. The tale does not possess a modern vocabulary of condemnation, and it does not look away. A reader watches an imbalance of power become a marriage that is also, for long stretches, a real intimacy.

Politics answers the private offences. Genji is exiled to Suma, across the water from the capital, and the exile chapters are among the great passages of the book: rain, music, letters, the company of a few loyal men, the sensation of a life interrupted. At Akashi he meets a lady of a provincial house, and their daughter will matter to the realm in a way his more glamorous loves do not. Return to the capital brings a different kind of splendour. He builds a great establishment, gathers women in separate households around it, and tries to make a world that will hold all his attachments in something like harmony. The harmony frays. Illness, jealousy, spirit possession, and the ordinary cruelty of rank keep interrupting it. The Rokujo lady, alive and then dead, is the figure through whom the tale lets jealousy become a force that can sicken a rival. Whether a modern reader calls that force psychological or supernatural, the tale treats it as real in its effects on bodies and on reputations.

After the shining prince

Genji's later years darken. Murasaki's death takes the centre out of the house he has arranged. His own death is handled with a reticence that still startles. Tradition leaves a blank where a chapter of his dying might have stood, a title without a text, so that the brightest life in the book passes out of it between chapters. The tale then continues, and the continuation is a different temperature. In the chapters set at Uji, two friends, Kaoru and Niou, circle a household of sisters. Kaoru carries a doubt about his birth. Niou carries an easier and more dangerous charm, a smaller echo of Genji's radiance without Genji's long education in consequence. The youngest sister, Ukifune, is loved by both and can survive in neither arrangement. Her flight, her attempted drowning, and her later life among nuns give the end of the tale a starkness the early chapters, for all their sorrow, had kept inside the capital's beauty.

Scholars differ about how much of the Uji sequence is Murasaki Shikibu's own hand and how much may be continuation. The difference of tone is unmistakable either way. The early book glows with Genji's gift for making a room more alive by entering it. The late book is full of people who miss one another, who write the wrong letter, who arrive at a house and find that the person has chosen absence. Mono no aware, the pathos of things, is the later critical name for a feeling the tale practises on almost every page: beauty and attachment made sharper by the knowledge that they are already passing. Seasonal flowers, a robe's colour, a poem sent at dawn, are units of that feeling.

Poems, rank, and the inner life

Conversation in the tale often becomes poetry. A remark in prose calls up a poem, and the poem answers, revises, or evades. Readers of English meet this texture through translators who have made very different books: Arthur Waley's free and novelistic version, Edward Seidensticker's, Royall Tyler's annotated rendering, Dennis Washburn's. The poems are part of the action. Characters reveal taste, resentment, and education in the way they handle an allusion. Rank is equally part of the action. Who may see whom, who sits behind which screen, whose daughter can be offered at court: the plot runs on these rules. Interiority in Genji is interiority inside a system. People feel most deeply the distinctions the system makes available. That is one reason the book can seem, to a reader trained on the English novel, both strangely modern and deeply unlike a novel of the Watt type. The particular person is here. The clock, the money, and the middle-class public are absent. In their place are ceremony, kinship, and a prose that can stay with a hesitation for as long as a letter can.

Murasaki Shikibu's diary, a separate work, shows pieces of the same court from the outside of the tale: festivals, the empress's household, the author's own reserve. It is useful background, and it is the wrong substitute for the fiction. The tale's scale is its thinking. It follows the consequences of charm across decades, and it is willing to let charm curdle. Genji is radiant and repeatedly at fault. The women are constrained and repeatedly more perceptive than the arrangements that contain them. By the time Ukifune chooses a life in which she will be hard to find, the book has earned a scepticism about the shining prince that it never converts into a simple rejection of beauty.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the rise of the novel tells an English eighteenth-century story through Watt, and then points to Genji so that the story cannot masquerade as the origin of serious prose fiction. This tale is the point. Long before Defoe, a writer in another language built a vast narrative about particular people, memory, sexual power, and the sadness of time, and did it in the vernacular prose of her own court. Calling Genji the first novel settles a question the book does not ask. Reading it as a court tale that does the kinds of work later readers ask novels to do is enough to change the map. Watt's formal realism remains a sharp description of Crusoe and Pamela. It is a description of one rise, in one culture. Genji is the evidence that inner life in long prose has more than one history.
