Early in the eleventh century, Murasaki Shikibu, a lady-in-waiting to the empress, wrote a story about a boy who cannot be emperor. His father, the emperor, loves a low-ranking consort, Kiritsubo. Rival ladies at court make her life impossible, and she dies while the child is small. The boy is too brilliant, and too weakly backed, to be left in the succession, so he is given the surname Minamoto and called Genji. Grown, he begins an affair with Fujitsubo, his father's consort, who looks like the dead mother. The son born of that affair is raised as the emperor's own child and in time becomes emperor, while the court goes on treating the official story as the true one.

The book then does something novels much later still hesitate to do. After Murasaki, the woman Genji loves most, dies, he is given a chapter of grief, and then a chapter that is only a title: "Kumogakure," "Vanished into the Clouds." No text comes down with it. Years pass. The tale picks up with a younger generation, moves to Uji, and follows Ukifune, a woman pulled between two men. The last chapter, "The Floating Bridge of Dreams," stops without a wedding, a punishment, or a death scene that would close the plot. Old commentaries already disagreed about whether the blank chapter was never written or was lost.

What a reader meets is a working court: ranks, clothes, and the private knowledge that a child's parentage is a lie everyone lives with. A poem does the work a speech would do in a later novel. Desire, apology, and status go out in a few lines, often at dawn, and the answer comes back as another poem. That is a novel's material, particular people in a particular house, set down centuries before Defoe. Anyone writing fiction now still has the same two choices the book makes. Stay inside one person's shame and desire, and let the life end the way a life does, by disappearance rather than by a moral.

Source. Murasaki Shikibu, The Tale of Genji (Genji monogatari), early eleventh century. A standard modern English text is Royall Tyler, trans., The Tale of Genji (New York: Viking, 2001). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Tale_of_Genji
