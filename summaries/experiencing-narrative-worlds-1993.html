Richard J. Gerrig's case for what a story does to a reader is someone who knows the ending and still feels suspense. In experiments he reports in Experiencing Narrative Worlds, people read short narratives aimed at facts they already knew. One of those facts was that Charles Lindbergh was the first pilot to fly the Atlantic alone. When the narrative put argument and obstacles in the way of the flight, readers were slower to confirm the fact. For a moment the known outcome was less available than it had been a minute before.

He describes the same state as travel. The reader leaves the chair, some of what is true in the room becomes hard to reach, and the return is not quite to the same person. The distance may be years and another country, or only the fact that you cannot step onto the page and warn the person in the scene. Knowing that the pages are fiction does not, on his account, keep the trip from happening. You also do not need to be the character. A film can show a victim who has no idea anyone is behind the door, and the viewer is the one who tenses. Gerrig uses that gap to argue that suspense is the reader's, produced by what the story makes likely, even when the figure on screen feels nothing.

You can know who wins a game, a trial, or a war and still go quiet in the retelling. Rereading a novel whose last page you remember produces the same anomalous suspense: for a stretch of sentences the hoped-for outcome feels open again. That delay is why a story you have already heard can change what feels likely for a few minutes after you stand up. Melanie Green and Timothy Brock later turned the trip into a questionnaire and tied higher scores to changes in belief. Gerrig's Lindbergh result is smaller and sharper than a claim about conversion. It is a measurable hesitation in a fact the reader never actually forgot.
