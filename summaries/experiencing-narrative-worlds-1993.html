The reader leaves and comes back

Richard J. Gerrig's Experiencing Narrative Worlds, published by Yale University Press in 1993, is the book Green and Brock later cite when they turn a metaphor into a scale. Gerrig is a cognitive psychologist, and the metaphor is travel. A reader is transported. She leaves the world of origin, travels in a world the narrative proposes, and returns. The return is the part easy to forget when criticism talks only about immersion. Gerrig thinks the traveller comes back changed, even if the change is as small as a new trace in memory or a belief that has lost its source tag. The book is an argument, with experiments behind it, about what that journey consists of: how we build a story world, how we participate in it, how we believe and doubt, and what we carry home.

He sets himself against a comfortable slogan. The slogan says that audiences willingly suspend disbelief, as if the natural state were doubt and the story had to be granted a special pardon. Gerrig argues the opposite direction of effort. Readers construct the narrative world as they go. They take on its facts in order to understand the sentences. Disbelieving, tagging a claim as false, rejecting a premise, is additional work, done when something triggers it. The slogan about suspension flatters the audience's control. His account asks us to notice how much has to be accepted simply to follow a paragraph, and how late the sceptical act can arrive. This is a claim about processing, and it has an obvious moral for persuasion. If rejection is the extra step, then a fluent story has a head start.

Suspense you have no right to feel

One of the book's best-known lines of evidence concerns anomalous suspense. You know how a story ends. You may have known for years that a historical figure dies, or that a pair of lovers in a famous play do not walk away. You can still feel suspense when the scene approaches. Knowledge that ought to make the outcome inert fails to switch the feeling off. Gerrig treats this as a clue to the way narrative processing is built. The system that follows a scene tracks the local uncertainty of the characters and the immediate next moment. It does not calmly consult the archive where the ending is already stored. The result is a kind of double life. You know, and you fear, and the fear is a real response to the telling in front of you.

He connects this to anomalous replotting, the habit of wanting events to come out otherwise even when you know they will not. Participatory responses, in his term, are the private asides a reader or viewer produces along the way: do not open the door, do not trust him, leave now. These responses treat the audience as present at the events in a peculiar, powerless way. You are not the protagonist. Gerrig calls the position side-participation. You act, mentally, from beside the scene, with hopes that the plot will not oblige. Children listening to a story show the social version of the same impulse when they shout at the teller. Adults keep the shout inward. The inward shout is evidence that transportation is active, a stance of someone inside a situation.

Memory, source, and the return

The return journey is where Gerrig's account becomes a theory of influence. Information taken from a story is stored. Later, the information may be used without a clear memory of where it came from. A fact about a city, a profession, or a kind of person, picked up inside a novel or a film, can surface as something you simply know. He discusses the experimental literature on this kind of source confusion and the conditions that make it more likely, including the fluency of the original telling and the delay before you need the information again. The point for narrative worlds is sharp. The traveller's change is often invisible to the traveller. You remember the gist and forget the ticket. Fiction is therefore a teacher even when you never sat down to be taught, and the teaching is hard to audit afterward.

Belief, in this framework, is the outcome of construction plus failed or skipped rejection. While you are inside the world, you perform its premises. When you come home, some of the premises have nowhere tidy to go, and they settle among your other premises. Gerrig does not think this makes audiences helpless in every case. Cues can trigger rejection. Inconsistency can throw a reader out. A framing that keeps the source salient can make the return cleaner. His insistence is that these are achievements, things the mind does under certain conditions, against a background in which following the story is the path of less resistance. Experiencing a narrative world is the ordinary case. Policing it is the special effort.

A psychology that critics can use

The book sits between laboratory psychology and the ordinary experience of readers, and it is written so that both can follow. Gerrig explains situation models, the representations readers build of who is doing what and why, without requiring a seminar in jargon. He uses well-known stories as intuition pumps and then shows where an experiment has pressed on the intuition. The method matters. Anomalous suspense could have remained a charming remark about rereading. Tied to a processing claim, it becomes a reason to distrust any theory that pictures the audience as a cool archivist consulting known endings. Side-participation could have remained a metaphor. Tied to the things people want to shout, it becomes a description of the social stance stories create: we are there, we are powerless, we are not neutral.

Writers and teachers can take several working ideas away. Fluency is a moral fact, because fluent false worlds are easy to enter and easy to keep. Rereading still moves us, so familiarity is a poor explanation of aesthetic death. The end of a story is a return, worth attending to, the moment when a reader might notice what she is bringing back. Gerrig's limits are the limits of the cognitive tradition he works in. He is stronger on the individual mind in a single encounter than on institutions, markets, and the politics of whose stories get fluent production. The 1993 book does not need to be those other books. It needs to be the careful account of the journey, so that later scales and meta-analyses have something exact to mean by the word they share with it.

Why it matters here

The card on narrative transportation borrows Green and Brock's scale and van Laer's wide confirmation, and it points back to this book for the metaphor those papers turned into measures. Gerrig is the prior account of what the measures are measuring. Transportation is leaving, travelling, and returning changed. The change can be a belief you did not mean to adopt, because disbelief was the extra work and you were busy following the scene. Anomalous suspense and the urge to warn the characters are the signs that the journey is happening even to people who know the ending. Read him when the later research starts to sound like a technique for persuaders alone. The technique works, where it works, because this is what ordinary reading already is.
