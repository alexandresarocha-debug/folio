Why progress did not stall

Joel Mokyr's A Culture of Growth, published in 2016, asks a question one step behind the industrial revolution. Britain in the eighteenth century produced a famous cluster of machines. Mokyr wants to know why the cluster was not another of history's temporary booms, a burst of ingenuity that ran into a ceiling and stopped, as earlier episodes of commercial and technical vitality had stopped. His opening claim is cultural, in a sense he is careful to specify. What changed in early modern Europe was not the intelligence of individuals and not a single legal reform. It was the emergence of a set of beliefs and of a social organisation for beliefs. Nature came to be seen as knowable. Knowledge came to be seen as something that ought to be useful, public, and cumulative. And a competitive market for ideas, the Republic of Letters, grew up across a politically fragmented continent, so that no one prince and no one church could shut the argument down. Sustained modern growth, in which technological change is a normal expectation rather than a lucky run, rests on that culture. The machines are a consequence.

Useful knowledge and the Republic of Letters

Mokyr's unit of analysis is useful knowledge, a term he had developed in earlier work. It includes propositional knowledge, the beliefs a society holds about how the natural world is put together, and prescriptive knowledge, the techniques it knows for making and doing. The two feed each other. A better account of the vacuum improves the steam engine. A working engine raises questions that the account of the vacuum has to answer. A culture of growth, in his sense, is one in which this feedback is expected, organised, and honoured. The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, he argues, are when that expectation became a programme. Francis Bacon is less an inventor than a propagandist for the programme. The point of knowledge is power over nature in the service of human welfare, and the method is systematic inquiry rather than the preservation of ancient authorities. Newton and the mechanical philosophy gave the programme prestige. What made it a culture rather than a slogan was the network that carried it.

The Republic of Letters is that network. Scholars, artisans who read, patrons, academies, journals, and a very large correspondence connected people who often never met and who did not share a sovereign. Priority, reputation, and the pleasure of being right were the incentives. The republic was not democratic, and it was not free of feuds, plagiarism, and status. It was open in a more limited and more important way. A finding published in one city could be checked in another. A scholar in trouble with one court could move. The audience for a discovery was European rather than local, which raised the return to making the discovery public instead of burying it in a guild secret. Mokyr's claim is that this social technology, a competitive, transnational market for ideas, was the institutional face of the cultural change. Beliefs about nature would have remained the opinions of isolated geniuses without a republic to reward them and to store what they found.

Fragmentation as an advantage

The political argument is the part that most clearly distinguishes a European story from a story about a general rise of the human mind. Early modern Europe was a set of competing states. Competition was often violent and fiscally crushing. Mokyr's point is that the same fragmentation which produced the wars also produced an exit option for knowledge. A ruler who suppressed a line of inquiry lost the inquirers to a rival, and a ruler who patronised them gained prestige and, sometimes, a technical edge. Censorship existed and was sometimes savage. It could not be continental. The Republic of Letters exploited the gaps between jurisdictions in the way merchants exploited the gaps between customs zones. A unified empire with a single orthodoxy and a single examination system would have been, on this logic, a more dangerous environment for a disruptive intellectual programme, even if that empire was rich, literate, and administratively impressive.

China is the comparison he uses to make the logic sharp, and it is the comparison that has drawn the most fire. Mokyr does not deny Chinese technological achievement. He argues that in the centuries that matter for his question, a large and relatively unified political and cultural space did not sustain the same open, adversarial, cumulative market for propositional knowledge, and that the connection between scholars and artisans was weaker. The examination culture channelled talent into a classical canon. The state did not face a peer state next door ready to welcome a dissident natural philosopher. Specialists on Chinese science have replied that this understates the practical learning, the philological rigour, and the real debates that existed, and that it measures China against a European programme which Europeans themselves only partly lived up to. The comparison is most useful as a statement of Mokyr's mechanism and least useful as a verdict on a civilisation. Fragmentation, in his theory, is an advantage for the growth of knowledge even when it is a disadvantage for peace and for the coordination of policy.

From culture to the factory, and what he does not claim

The conclusion connects the culture to the economy without pretending that a journal article is a spinning machine. Mokyr distinguishes the Industrial Revolution, a British episode of macro-inventions in textile, iron, and power, from modern economic growth, the self-sustaining process in which innovation continues and productivity keeps rising. Culture, he argues, explains the second more directly than the first. Allen's prices can explain why Britain was the first place where a set of labour-saving machines was worth building. Those machines could have remained a local plateau, a high-wage society's clever speciality, if the wider intellectual culture had not been generating the tools, the instruments, the chemical knowledge, and the habit of improvement that turned a plateau into a slope. The culture of growth is a theory of why the slope continued. It is not a theory of the wage bill in Lancashire in a particular decade, and Mokyr does not try to make it one.

The evidence is intellectual history read by an economic historian. He follows the advocates of useful knowledge, the institutions they built, the correspondence networks that have been mapped by historians of science, and the resistance they met. He uses the economic concept of a market, with entry, competition, and returns, as the model for what the republic was doing. The model is an analogy. Reputation is not a price in the same way a wage is a price, and the analogy can be pressed too far. Its strength is that it generates a prediction. Where the market for ideas was closed, by monopoly of patronage or by a successful continental censorship, the culture of growth should have been weaker. The European fragmentation prediction is the one he thinks the record supports.

What the cultural thesis cannot settle by itself

The standing objection to cultural explanations is that they are easy to assert and hard to falsify. Any successful economy can be given a flattering description of its beliefs after the fact, and any unsuccessful one can be given an unflattering one. Mokyr's defence is specificity. He names the beliefs, the date at which they became a programme, and the institution, the Republic of Letters, through which they acted. He also names the thing he is not explaining, which leaves him open to a different objection from the other side. If prices explain the British machines and culture explains the European continuation, a full account needs both, and the book should not be cited as if it had replaced coal, wages, and colonies. Pomeranz's ecological constraints and Allen's factor prices remain necessary for the question of where the first industrialisation happened. Mokyr becomes necessary for the question of why technological change, once it started, became the normal condition of economic life rather than a passing efflorescence.

A second limit is the distance from the workshop. The people in the Republic of Letters and the people who built the first machines were not the same people, and the paths between a mechanical philosophy and a working mule are longer and more contested than a smooth story of useful knowledge suggests. Artisans had their own knowledge, often tacit and sometimes secret, and historians of technology have insisted that a theory centred on scholars can miss where the improvements actually came from. Mokyr has room for artisans in the culture he describes. The emphasis of the book is still on the learned end of the feedback. A balanced reading keeps his market for ideas and adds the workshop as a place where prescriptive knowledge lived and changed under the pressure of prices. Culture, in the sense that survives this reading, is not a mist over the economy. It is a set of institutions that made some kinds of knowledge cheaper to share and harder to kill.

Why it matters here

The card pomeranz-divergence sets three explanations of the industrial divergence next to each other. Pomeranz gives the late break, the coal, and the ghost acres. Allen gives the high wages and the cheap energy that biased British invention towards labour-saving machines. Mokyr, whom the card names for the cultural alternative, gives the reason a European society could keep improving after any particular set of prices had been exploited. A Culture of Growth is that alternative at book length. The Republic of Letters, the Baconian programme of useful knowledge, and the advantage of political fragmentation are the mechanisms the card is pointing to when it says that one major position in the debate is cultural. Folio uses the book to stop the path from collapsing into a choice between resources and wages. Resources and wages can start an industrial revolution. Mokyr's claim is that only a culture organised for cumulative, public, and contestable knowledge turns the start into modern growth. The China comparison should be read as his sharpest statement of the mechanism, and as a claim that specialists continue to dispute, not as a settled measurement of two civilisations.
