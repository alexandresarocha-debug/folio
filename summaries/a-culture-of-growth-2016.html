Joel Mokyr asks why the Industrial Revolution began in Europe when China, on many technical measures, was not behind. His answer is a change in beliefs among educated Europeans between about 1500 and 1700. He calls the setting a market for ideas. People with a new claim had to persuade others to accept it, and the reward was reputation, which could turn into a patron, a post, or a printing. The judges of that market stopped treating ancient authors as the last word. Mokyr names the new standards: mathematics where it applied, experiment where an experiment could be run, and careful collection and classification of facts where neither of those would do.

Two people, in his account, did the most to change what counted as a good idea. Francis Bacon argued that knowledge was supposed to be useful, and that understanding nature was worthwhile because it could be turned to material improvement. Isaac Newton then showed, in public, that the moderns could outdo the ancients. Around them stood the Republic of Letters, a cross-border network of scholars who circulated findings, argued in print, and judged one another. Europe's political fragmentation made the network hard to shut. A thinker in trouble in one country could cross a border and keep publishing. China's intellectual life, Mokyr argues, was at least as sophisticated, and it remained under the control of a single ruling elite. There was no neighboring court to flee to. Heterodox work could be stopped everywhere at once.

The practical claim is that a technology is downstream of a culture that expects knowledge to be tested and then used. A society can have skilled artisans, markets, and a large population and still not build a shared, cumulative body of useful knowledge, if new results stay secret, stay local, or stay subordinate to authority. Competition among patrons and among states is, on this view, a piece of industrial policy. It is what keeps one ruler from being able to end the argument. The printing press lowered the cost of joining that argument. It did not decide who won it.

The timing is the vulnerable point. Kenneth Pomeranz, in The Great Divergence (2000), argues from consumption, wages, and farm output that Britain and the lower Yangzi were still economically close in the eighteenth century, and that Britain pulled away around 1800 because of coal and American land, not because of a cultural head start laid down two centuries earlier. If living standards were still similar that late, a European market for ideas may have been real without being the thing that broke the tie. Mokyr's evidence is about what scholars believed and how they argued. It is thinner as a measurement of why a particular machine appeared in a particular decade.
