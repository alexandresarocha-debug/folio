Among the Tiv of central Nigeria, a man who gathered too much power did not get a state. He got accused of witchcraft. Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson take that practice, described by the anthropologist Paul Bohannan in 1958, as a working political system. The Tiv distrusted anyone who rose above the lineage. Movements such as Nyambua cut such men back down, so that an egalitarian order could continue and no chief could turn a temporary advantage into a government. Acemoglu and Robinson call the result an Absent Leviathan. Society is strong enough to stop a state from forming. People are also left without a power that can keep the peace, provide public goods, or stop one neighbor from dominating another. Liberty, in their definition, is not the absence of a state. It is freedom from violence and from living at someone else's mercy.

Athens is their case of the opposite movement, and it was not calm. Solon's reforms opened politics to more people and loosened the social rules that had kept most of them out. Later, tyrants pulled power back toward the state. In 508 BC a popular uprising brought Cleisthenes in. His reforms strengthened the state's capacity to tax and to act, and at the same time strengthened the public's ability to restrain the people who held office. Acemoglu and Robinson call the ongoing contest the Red Queen effect, after the queen in Lewis Carroll who tells Alice that it takes all the running you can do to stay in the same place. State and society both have to keep getting stronger. If the state pulls ahead, the result is despotism, as in their account of China. If society refuses power altogether, the result is the Tiv situation. The corridor between them is narrow, and it is a process, not a constitution you can copy.

The everyday version is a government that can do something, and that can be made to stop. Courts and police strong enough to restrain a boss, a militia, or a rich neighbor are useful. The same courts and police become the threat if nobody can fire the people who run them, sue them, or demonstrate against them. A state that has a flag, a capital, and a stack of laws, and still cannot keep people safe or deliver a service, is what they call a Paper Leviathan. Argentina and Liberia are among the cases they put in that group. The paper exists. The protection does not.
