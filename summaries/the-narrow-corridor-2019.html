Liberty needs a strong state and a strong society

The Narrow Corridor, published in 2019 by Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, is the sequel to the question they had answered, in Why Nations Fail, largely in terms of prosperity. That earlier book sorted institutions into inclusive and extractive and argued that prosperity follows the inclusive kind. The later book asks a further question. How does a society get a state that is strong enough to protect people, enforce rules, and provide public goods, without that state becoming the instrument of domination? Their opening claim is that liberty survives only inside a narrow corridor. On one side of the corridor is the absent state, a society in which people are at the mercy of local violence, kinship, and custom because no authority can restrain the powerful among them. On the other side is the despotic state, strong enough to act and unconstrained by any organised society that could answer back. Inside the corridor, a capable state and a capable society hold each other in balance. The balance is not a constitution written once. It is a continuous race. They call it the Red Queen effect, after the character who had to run in order to stay in the same place. If the state pulls ahead, the society tips towards despotism. If society pulls ahead and the state withers, the society tips towards the cage of private power and customary constraint. Liberty is the condition of remaining in the race.

Three Leviathans

Thomas Hobbes's Leviathan is their starting image and their target. Hobbes thought that a sovereign strong enough to overawe everyone was the condition of peace, and that life without such a sovereign was miserable. Acemoglu and Robinson accept the misery of stateless violence and reject the conclusion that an unconstrained sovereign is the remedy. They divide the possibilities into three. The absent Leviathan is the state that does not exist or cannot reach beyond a court. People then depend on norms, clans, and local bosses. The authors are willing to say that some of these orders are sophisticated and long-lived, and also that they can be cages. Custom can fix a hierarchy as firmly as a police force, and can make exit from the hierarchy unthinkable. The despotic Leviathan is the state that has broken or absorbed the social forces which might have shackled it. It can build, tax, and repress. It does not have to bargain with an independent society, because it has organised society out of independent existence. The shackled Leviathan is the one inside the corridor. It is powerful. It is also answerable, not because the rulers are milder by nature, but because organised subjects can resist, sue, leave, publish, and replace.

The shackled case is their account of how western European societies, unevenly and late, became liberal in a structural sense. They emphasise a historical combination they think was unusual. A tradition of assemblies and of local corporate life, which they trace in part to the fragmented politics of medieval Europe, met a state that was trying to get stronger. The church, as a rival organisation with its own law and its own property, kept lay rulers from becoming the only source of authority. Cities and parliaments bargained over tax. The result, where it worked, was not a weak state. Britain and, later, other European states became very strong administratively and militarily. They became strong while being watched. The authors' point against a certain libertarian instinct is that liberty was not the absence of government. It was the presence of a government that society had learned to tie down without disabling.

The corridor's historical cases

Athens is one of their early exhibits of the race. Democratic institutions and a powerful civic identity grew together with a state that could tax, fight, and punish. The democracy was narrow by any modern standard, and the authors do not pretend otherwise. They use it to show that shackling is old, and that it can be lost. China is their recurring case of the despotic Leviathan. A long history of bureaucratic state capacity, from the Qin unification onward, appears in their account as a state that repeatedly prevented the formation of an independent social power capable of constraining it. Periods of commercial energy and technological invention occur inside that history and do not, on their reading, produce a durable corridor. The argument is bound to be controversial with historians of China, because it gathers very different dynasties into one political type. The authors would say the type is the point. Capacity without a shackled relationship to society is a different trajectory from the European one, not a delayed version of it.

India is the case they use for the cage of norms. They argue that the caste order and the local power of customary law constrained the state and constrained individuals, so that neither a fully despotic Leviathan nor a shackled liberal state emerged from a very old civilisation. This chapter has drawn some of the sharpest specialist criticism, because a single corridor metaphor is a crude instrument for the variety of Indian polities, and because caste is being asked to explain a very long macroeconomic and political history. The Islamic world and parts of Latin America and Africa are discussed through related failures of the balance. In some places a state claims authority it cannot exercise, a paper Leviathan, despotic in form and absent in practice, so that people suffer both official predation and private insecurity. In others a strongman builds a despotic capacity out of a prior fragmentation. The typology is doing a lot of sorting. Its value is that it distinguishes a weak state from a shackled one, a distinction that cruder measures of governance often miss, since both can look less than authoritarian in a table.

What has to stay true for the corridor to be a theory

The conclusion they want is diagnostic. A society that wishes to be both prosperous and free needs state capacity and social mobilisation at the same time, and it needs them to keep pace. Reforms that build a tax authority, a court, or an army without building the social power to oversee them are a road out of the corridor on the despotic side. Reforms that weaken the state in the name of liberty, in a place where the state was already the only defence against private domination, are a road out of the corridor on the other side. The policy implication is awkward for programmes that have only one instrument. State-building and society-building are complements. The Red Queen is a claim that there is no stable liberal constitution which can be installed and then left to run without the race.

Limits and the arguments it has drawn

The corridor is a metaphor, and metaphors stretch. Once any balanced polity is inside and any unpleasant polity is outside, the theory describes outcomes rather than predicting them. A usable version has to say in advance what counts as a shackle and what counts as a society, and it has to allow for the possibility that a society shackles a state into stagnation, or that a state without their kind of social constraint remains law-bound for reasons of bureaucracy, ideology, or elite self-discipline. Historians of China and of India have argued that the chapters on those civilisations flatten scholarship that does not fit the type. Historians of Europe have argued that the European corridor is told as a more purposeful march than the record of monarchy, empire, and exclusion justifies. The book is aware, from the authors' own earlier work, that inclusive institutions for some people have coexisted with extraction of others. A corridor that describes the freedom of citizens and ignores the people those citizens dominated is incomplete, and the authors' treatment of slavery and empire is one of the places a critical reader should press.

The relation to Why Nations Fail is the cleanest internal limit. The earlier book could treat a strong inclusive state as the solution. The later book says that the strength and the inclusiveness are a pair of moving targets, and that the political problem never finishes. That is a deepening rather than a retraction. It also means that advice drawn from the first book, build inclusive institutions, is not yet an instruction. The instruction is to build them in a race with the social capacity to keep them inclusive, and the book is more eloquent about the race than about how a society currently outside the corridor is supposed to enter it. Entry is historically rare in their own account. A theory of liberty that makes liberty the product of a long, contingent, easily lost balance should be modest about recipes. The Narrow Corridor is more modest than its range suggests, and less modest than the difficulty of the cases requires.

Why it matters here

The card acemoglu-institutions is the inclusive-against-extractive argument and the research programme around it. The Narrow Corridor is the card's next step, the book in which Acemoglu and Robinson stop treating the right institutions as a stock of rules and start treating them as a balance between state and society that has to be run at continuously. The absent Leviathan, the despotic Leviathan, the shackled Leviathan, and the Red Queen are the vocabulary the card is pointing at when it sends the reader onward from Why Nations Fail. Folio uses the book to keep the institutional theory from hardening into a binary of good rules and bad rules. The rules matter because of the power that stands behind them, and the book's claim is that liberty exists only where that power is both real and answered. The card's standing caution, that definitions in this research programme can be stretched, applies here with particular force, because a corridor drawn after the fact can always be made to fit. The use of the book is the distinction itself, state capacity against social constraint, not a licence to sort every nation into a diagram.
