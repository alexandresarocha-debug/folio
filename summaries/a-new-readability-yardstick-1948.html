In 1943 Rudolf Flesch developed a statistical formula for measuring readability, based on average sentence length, the number of affixes such as prefixes and suffixes, and the number of references to people. It was taken up for newspaper reports, advertising, government publications and leaflets for farmers. But users found counting affixes tedious, and the formula had odd results: it consistently rated the popular Reader's Digest more readable than the New Yorker, which many educated readers found far livelier.

This 1948 paper offered a revision in two parts. The reading-ease formula uses just two measures: average sentence length in words and average word length in syllables per 100 words. Fitted to the McCall-Crabbs standard test lessons in reading, it is RE = 206.835 - 0.846 wl - 1.015 sl, with a multiple correlation of 0.70 with the criterion. Scores run from 0 to 100 for almost all ordinary prose; 100 means a child who has finished fourth grade could answer three-quarters of the test questions. A separate human-interest formula counts personal words and personal sentences.

Flesch also warned against misuse. Because sentence length was the easiest element to measure, it was often overemphasised; he cited directives to staff writers at the Associated Press and the New York Times recommending shorter sentences in news leads.

In a footnote he scored his own paper. The main body had a reading-ease score of 30 and a human-interest score of 0, while the section with directions for users scored 79 for reading ease.

What this means in real life: the Flesch reading-ease score and later variants of it are still built into common word processors, so writers can check their own drafts in seconds.

Caution: the formula predicts difficulty from surface features only. It cannot tell whether a text is accurate, organised or interesting, and writers who chop sentences or swap words purely to raise the score can make a text worse.
