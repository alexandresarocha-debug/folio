Many things we call environment look like pure nurture: how much money a family has, whether a baby was breastfed, how much television a child watches. Eva Krapohl, Robert Plomin and colleagues at King's College London asked whether those measures carry a genetic signal.

They used 6,710 unrelated young people from the Twins Early Development Study, which recruited over 11,000 twin pairs born in England and Wales in 1994, 1995 and 1996. For each child they built polygenic scores: one number per person that adds up thousands of small DNA differences linked to a trait such as years of education, from large earlier gene studies.

The child's education score was linked to many parts of the child's early environment. It explained 6.5% of the differences in mother's education, 3.4% in television watching, 3.2% in household income, 2.1% in breastfeeding, 1% in parental smacking and 0.8% in smoking during pregnancy. The child's genetic risk score for schizophrenia was linked to the father's age, but only weakly, at 0.2%. The team controlled for overall genetic relatedness, so the links were not just a sign of ancestry differences between groups.

Many of these exposures were outside the child's control. Parents' age and education at the child's birth cannot be caused by the child, so those links most likely reflect the passive kind: parents pass on genes and also create the home. Other measures, such as smacking, could partly be evoked by the child.

What this means in real life: when a study says breastfeeding or income predicts a child's school results, part of that link may run through genes shared by parent and child.

Caution: the effects are small, mostly a few percent. Without the parents' DNA, the study could not separate the passive kind from the evocative kind. And the sample comes from one country and one three-year birth cohort.
