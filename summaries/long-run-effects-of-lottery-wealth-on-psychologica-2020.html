Erik Lindqvist of the Stockholm School of Economics, Robert Östling and David Cesarini set out to measure, as precisely as possible, what lottery wealth does to well-being years later. They surveyed a large sample of Swedish lottery players and analysed the results following procedures registered in advance in a public analysis plan.

The design used the rules of the lotteries themselves. Within certain groups of players, the size of the prize was randomly assigned, so the researchers could compare players in the same group who happened to win different amounts. Their sample of 3,362 players had won prizes totalling $277 million, which made the estimates far more precise than earlier work.

Large-prize winners showed sustained increases in overall life satisfaction that persisted for more than a decade, with no evidence of fading over time. According to their estimate, an after-tax prize of $100,000 improves life satisfaction by 0.037 standard deviations. The effects on happiness and mental health were significantly smaller.

The authors read this as a difference between evaluative well-being, how people judge their lives as a whole, and affective well-being, how they feel day to day. Wealth had larger long-run effects on the first. Follow-up questions pointed to improved financial circumstances as the key reason for the rise in life satisfaction.

What this means in real life: money seems to change how people rate their lives more than how they feel on an ordinary day.

Caution: the effect per dollar is modest, the players were Swedish, and survey questions about life satisfaction and happiness measure different things. The study cannot say much about the very largest jackpots or about people who never play.

Source. Lindqvist, E., Östling, R., and Cesarini, D. (2020). Long-run effects of lottery wealth on psychological well-being. Review of Economic Studies, 87(6), 2703-2726. doi:10.1093/restud/rdaa006. Working paper: NBER Working Paper 24667 (2018), https://www.nber.org/papers/w24667
