In 260 the Sasanian king Shapur I captured the Roman emperor Valerian near Edessa. He is the only Roman emperor taken alive in the field by a foreign king. In the same years the empire split from the inside. Postumus set up a Gallic empire on the Rhine in 260. Zenobia of Palmyra held Egypt and much of the east until the emperor Aurelian defeated her in 272. Aurelian had the pieces back together by 274, and his own officers killed him in 275. Adrian Goldsworthy's How Rome Fell (Yale University Press, 2009) treats this stretch as the pattern of the age. From the murder of Severus Alexander by his troops in 235 to Diocletian's accession in 284, emperors and would-be emperors followed one another in quick succession, and the usual death was at Roman hands.

He follows the habit past the third-century recovery. Emperors pulled frontier armies inward to fight other Romans, then purged the friends of the loser. Constantine's victory at the Milvian Bridge on 28 October 312, the battle after which he favored the Christian God, was a war against Maxentius, a rival emperor sitting in Rome. In 454 the western emperor Valentinian III killed his general Aetius, the man who had held the western coalitions together through the Hunnic war. In 455 Valentinian was murdered in turn. Goldsworthy's short moral is that the competition for the throne consumed the army the empire needed against everyone else. Outside forces took the openings that competition left.

The eastern empire is the test. Constantinople had usurpers and civil wars of its own, and Roman government there lasted until the Ottoman conquest of 1453. The same political habit cannot, on its own, explain why one half ended in the fifth century. Goldsworthy's answer is that the east was richer, had a shorter land frontier, and held a capital that was very hard to take, so the same disease did less damage. Peter Heather's history of 2005 puts the weight on a different dated event, the loss of African tax revenue after the Vandals took Carthage in 439. Both descriptions fit particular years. Goldsworthy's question is how many of the west's later defeats were fought with an army that had just been used up against another Roman.
