A superpower that spent itself on its own wars

Adrian Goldsworthy's How Rome Fell: Death of a Superpower, published in 2009, is a political and military narrative from the late second century to the end of the western empire. The title's answer is plainer than many recent accounts. Rome fell because it tore itself apart. External enemies were real. Persia under the Sasanians was a serious rival. Germanic groups raided and, later, settled. Goldsworthy does not think those enemies were strong enough, on their own, to destroy a united empire. What made them decisive was that Roman armies spent so much of the third century, and so much of the fifth, fighting other Romans for the throne. Civil war is the mechanism. Everything else is what civil war left undefended.

He is impatient with single exotic causes. Christianity, lead in the water, moral decadence, and a mysterious loss of nerve do not, in his view, explain a state that could still win battles in its last century. The late Roman army was a professional force, often well led, and capable of beating Persian and Germanic opponents when it was allowed to face them as its main task. The tragedy, as he tells it, is how rarely that was the main task. A general with an army on a frontier could imagine the purple. The system gave him reasons to try and very few institutional ways to be stopped without another war.

From the Severans to the third-century break

The narrative begins where the habit of civil war becomes normal. After the Antonines, Commodus and then the Severan dynasty govern a state in which the army already knows its price. Septimius Severus wins power by war against other Romans. The murder of Severus Alexander in 235 opens the half-century in which emperors are made by camps and die by the sword. Goldsworthy counts the reigns not as a curiosity but as a structural failure. A state that cannot keep a ruler for long cannot keep a strategy. Troops are pulled off the Rhine, the Danube, and the eastern frontier to fight usurpations. The frontiers then break, and the next usurpation is justified by the break.

He treats the foreign dangers of these decades as genuine and as secondary. Sasanian Persia captures the emperor Valerian. Germanic raiding reaches deep into the provinces. For a time the empire splits, with a separate regime in the west and Palmyra dominant in much of the east. The coinage is ruined. Plague passes through. Goldsworthy does not deny the pile of disasters. He organises the pile. The empire's own contests for power are what turn a difficult strategic situation into a near-disintegration. A united empire had survived defeats before. A divided one could not man its borders while its field armies watched each other.

The recovery, and the state it produced

The recovery is part of his argument, because a recovery shows what was still possible. Aurelian reunites the provinces. Diocletian, from 284, rebuilds the administration, multiplies provinces, separates civil and military careers, enlarges the army, and tries to solve succession by sharing the throne in a college of emperors. Constantine continues the reconstruction, founds a new capital, and attaches the monarchy to the Christian church. Goldsworthy respects the achievement and describes its cost. The late empire is more bureaucratic, more heavily taxed, and more openly autocratic than the principate. It works. It works by consuming a larger share of what the provinces produce, and by trusting a larger set of officials. The reform that saves the system also makes it expensive and rigid, which will matter when the next round of civil wars arrives.

He does not think the fourth-century empire was a fake. It fought major wars and held a huge territory. He thinks its political culture was never repaired. Shared rule and designated heirs were attempts to stop usurpation. They did not remove the basic fact that a successful general was a plausible emperor, and that the soldiers' loyalty was personal and immediate. The machinery Diocletian built could run well under a dominant ruler. It could also be seized. Each seizure was paid for by the frontier that the seized army had left.

The fifth century as the crisis that was not reversed

Goldsworthy's fifth-century west is the third-century crisis without a Diocletian at the end of it. Usurpations and the fear of usurpation distort strategy. Large barbarian groups, some of them admitted and then mismanaged, some of them forcing entry, take advantage of a government that is fighting itself. The loss of provinces reduces the tax base. A smaller tax base supports a smaller army. Commanders in the west become the real patrons of the remaining field forces, and emperors become their candidates. He is sceptical of the idea that these commanders were the agents of foreign nations with long-prepared plans of conquest. They were players in a Roman political game that had consumed the state the game was meant to control.

The deposition of the last western emperor in 476 is, for him, the end of a superpower's western half, not the end of every Roman institution. Law, language, the church, and local elites continue. What ends is the capacity to act as a single military state in the west. The eastern empire, which he keeps in view, shows that Roman government as such had not become impossible. It had become impossible to sustain in a half of the empire that had spent its armies and its revenue on internal war. Goldsworthy closes on that institutional point rather than on a moral one. Superpowers can survive formidable enemies. They do not survive treating the contest for their own leadership as the permanent war.

Why it matters here

The Folio card on the third-century crisis takes this book as its guide through the years from the murder of Severus Alexander in 235 to Diocletian's accession in 284, and through the reboot that followed. The card's list is the book's list: a crowd of emperors who died violently, Valerian captured by Persia, raids across the rivers, a split into three political zones, a ruined silver coinage, plague, then Aurelian's reunion of the empire and Diocletian's new order. Goldsworthy is the narrative that keeps that list from reading like bad luck. The connecting fact is civil war. The empire almost disintegrated because Roman soldiers were the enemy Rome could not stop producing.

The card draws a further lesson that the book supports and that a list of reforms can obscure. The state survived by reinventing itself, and the reinvention made it more militarised, more taxed, and more rigid. Diocletian's college, Constantine's capital, and the heavier administration were answers to the third century. They were also the expensive machine the later empire had to keep feeding. Goldsworthy's longer story, past the card's fifty years and on to 476, is what shows the limit of the reboot. The fourth-century repair held while it held. The political flaw it could not remove, the use of the army to make emperors, returned in the fifth century, and the west did not get a second recovery of the same kind. The card's claim that a reform can save a system and burden it at the same time is the claim this whole narrative is built to make.
