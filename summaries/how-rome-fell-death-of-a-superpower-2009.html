Adrian Goldsworthy's count, in How Rome Fell: Death of a Superpower (Yale University Press, 2009), is a count of Roman wars against Romans. From the death of Caracalla in 217 until the western empire disappeared, he finds only a handful of decades without a civil war. His sharper claim is that, across those centuries, Roman soldiers were more likely to die in battle against other Romans than against foreign enemies. In 312, before he fought the rival emperor Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge, Constantine ordered his men to paint the chi-rho, the monogram of Christ, on their shields. The enemy on the other side of the bridge was a Roman army. No foreign king had the power, for a long time, to abolish the empire. A Roman usurper only had to kill the man wearing the purple.

The consequence shows up in the early fifth century, when the losses came in a rush the government did not reverse. Britain, Spain, North Africa, and parts of Gaul passed out of imperial control within a short span, and the western court never assembled the resources to take them back. Goldsworthy treats that sudden failure as what happens when a state has spent its armies, its officers, and its attention on internal survival. Emperors abandoned foreign wars to deal with challengers at home. After each failed coup came executions of anyone tied to the loser. The machine still looked, from outside, like a superpower without a serious rival. It had become a machine for keeping the current ruler alive.

The lesson he draws is about time. A government that spends its force on short-term personal survival can go on for a surprisingly long time, because the people it fights are trying to capture it, not to replace it with something else. The break, when it comes, is abrupt: several provinces gone, and no spare army that has not already been used against another Roman. The question for anyone telling the story of the fifth century is how much of the damage had already been done by Roman swords before a Vandal fleet or a Gothic army arrived.

The obvious pressure on the argument is the east. Constantinople had civil wars too, and a Roman emperor was still there when Ottoman forces took the city on 29 May 1453. Peter Heather's history of the same western collapse puts the decisive loss on the Vandal capture of Carthage in 439, which removed the African tax base, rather than on a rot that had been running since 217. Civil war is unmistakable in the record. Whether it is the cause, or the way a state already under outside pressure behaved, is where the two accounts split.
