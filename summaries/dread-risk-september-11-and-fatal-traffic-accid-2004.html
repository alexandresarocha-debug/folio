Gerd Gigerenzer looked at what Americans did with travel after the attacks of September 11, 2001. He calls a dread risk a low-probability event in which many people can be killed at one moment. The four hijacked flights were that kind of event. Two hundred and sixty-six passengers and crew died on those planes, and many more people died at the sites. His question was whether fear of a repeat pushed people out of the air and onto the road, where the ordinary risk is higher.

Air travel did fall. Compared with the same months in 2000, national revenue passenger miles were down 20 percent in October 2001, 17 percent in November, and 12 percent in December. Miles driven tell a smaller story, and not a direct one. There is no list of people who cancelled a flight and took the car. What the highway figures show is a shift in the total. In 2001, monthly miles driven were on average 0.9 percent higher than in 2000 before September, and 2.9 percent higher in October, November, and December. The biggest rise was on rural interstate highways, 5.3 percent, which is where a long drive would show up.

Fatal crashes before the attacks were boringly regular. From January through August 2001, the count sat within the range of 1996 through 2000, off by an average of 9 crashes a month, about 0.3 percent. October through December broke that pattern. Against a baseline that allowed for those extra 9 crashes a month, Gigerenzer counted a surplus of 317 fatal crashes: 67, 163, and 87. The comparison was statistically significant, chi-square of 12.0 with 3 degrees of freedom, N of 10,157, p of .008. In 2001 there were 42,116 traffic deaths and 37,795 fatal crashes, so the extra crashes come to about 353 deaths. He calls it about 350 lives, more than the 266 on the four planes, and only for those three months.

The everyday version is familiar. After a crash, a fire, or a scare in the news, people switch to the option they can picture as safe, even when the quiet option kills more people one at a time. A patient who has just heard a rare disaster story may refuse a treatment whose ordinary risk is larger. The feeling is about the kind of death, not the count.

The 353 is an estimate, not a list of named drivers. Gigerenzer could not see who had been going to fly. Oliver Kuss pointed out the gap: national totals cannot tell a former flyer from a stressed driver, or from someone driving to see family. The figure of about 1,500 extra road deaths is not this paper. It is Gigerenzer's later estimate for the twelve months after the attacks. Peter Ayton, Samantha Murray, and James Hampton, writing in 2019, report that Su and colleagues found no significant national rise in miles driven compared with 1999 and 2000, and no significant national rise in traffic deaths. The increase they did find was in the Northeast, along with more alcohol and drug citations, which fits fear that worsens driving rather than a countrywide decision to drive instead of fly.
