Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman were then at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. A footnote says the ordering of authors is random. Its targets were not students but their own colleagues: research psychologists.

The paper opens with a question from a questionnaire handed out at meetings of the Mathematical Psychology Group and the American Psychological Association. You have run an experiment on 20 subjects and found a significant result. You now run another 10 subjects. What is the probability that this new group, on its own, will also give a significant result? The typical answer was about .85. Depending on how you calculate it, a more reasonable value is about .47 or .48, and only 9 of 84 respondents gave answers between .40 and .60.

The authors argued that researchers hold a belief in the law of small numbers: they expect a small sample to be highly representative of the population, as if the law of large numbers applied to small samples too. That belief leads to studies that are too small, to too much confidence in early results, and to explaining away failures to replicate instead of blaming chance.

Other questions showed the same pattern. Respondents expected results to replicate in samples that had little chance of detecting the effect, and they offered causal stories for differences that sampling variation could easily explain.

What this means in real life: one small study, or a few early customers, rarely settles a question. A result needs a sample large enough to be likely to show it again.

Caution: the questionnaire samples were small and informal, and the paper is an argument as much as an experiment. Its warning proved prescient: decades later, low statistical power became a central explanation for psychology's replication problems.
