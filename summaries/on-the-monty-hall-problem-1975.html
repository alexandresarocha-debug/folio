Fifteen years before the Parade column, Steve Selvin, a statistician at the School of Public Health of the University of California, Berkeley, sent a letter to The American Statistician. Published in February 1975 under the title A Problem in Probability, it set the puzzle on Monty Hall's television game show, with three boxes, one of them holding car keys.

Several readers wrote to say his answer was wrong. In a second letter, published in August 1975 under the title On the Monty Hall Problem, he replied. The basis of his solution, he explained, is that Monty Hall knows which box holds the keys and, when he can open either of two boxes without revealing them, chooses between them at random. Under those conditions he showed with Bayes's rule that the contestant's box still has a probability of one in three. The contestant who trades it for the other unopened box wins with probability two in three.

He also reported other letters. D. L. Ferguson generalised the problem to n boxes with the host opening p of them. Benjamin King stressed that the answer depends on assumptions about the host's behaviour, not just the starting odds. And Monty Hall himself wrote. He said he was not a student of statistics problems, and pointed out that on his show a contestant could not in fact trade boxes once one was opened. But he added that after one box is seen to be empty, the contestant's chances are not 50/50: they remain what they were at first, one out of three. It only seems to the contestant that he stands a better chance. Selvin replied that he could not have said it better himself.

What this means in real life: probability answers depend on how the information was produced. The same open door means different things if the host knew, or opened it by chance.
