Background

In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration, a group of 270 researchers organised by Brian Nosek, published Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science in Science. They had tried to replicate 100 studies drawn from three leading psychology journals, following the original procedures closely enough that a repeated success could be credited to the finding and a repeated failure could not be shrugged off as a different experiment. Of the original studies, 97 per cent had reported a statistically significant result. Of the replications, about 36 per cent did. The effects that reappeared were, on average, about half the size of the effects that had been published. Folio's card on the replication crisis takes this paper as the moment the unease became a number the field had to cite.

The number is a verdict on a publishing system. A literature in which nearly every paper succeeds, while a serious repetition succeeds about a third of the time with smaller effects, has been selected and inflated. The paper measures that inflation. The causes and the repairs are the argument the card builds around the measurement.

The question

If a competent team takes a published psychology study and runs it again, how often does the result come back, and how large is it when it comes? Individual replications could not answer it, because a sample composed of findings someone had decided to challenge is already biased. Challenge samples are biased towards results that already look surprising or convenient to doubt. A reproducibility estimate has to begin from the journals, not from the grudges. The Open Science Collaboration asked the question in that form. One hundred studies, three journals, a shared standard for what would count as a replication, and a willingness to report the hits and the misses in one table.

How the project was set up

Teams of replicators took on studies from the target journals and attempted to repeat the essential design: the same kind of participants where that was possible, the same manipulation, the same outcome the original paper had treated as its result. Sample sizes were often larger than the originals, because the point was to estimate an effect, not to give a fragile finding a second chance to be lucky. Each replication was an individual scientific act, with its own materials and its own difficulties of translation. The paper is the aggregate. It scores each replication on whether it produced a significant result in the original direction, and it compares the size of the obtained effect with the size that was published. Those two scores answer different worries. A finding can fail to reach significance and still point the same way. A finding can reach significance and still be a shadow of the effect the textbook is teaching.

The collaboration itself was part of the method. A single laboratory replicating a rival's study is a duel, and duels have reputations at stake. Two hundred and seventy researchers, spread across the studies, make the estimate harder to file as one person's campaign. The rules for inclusion and for scoring were the protection against a different bias, the freedom to decide after the fact which replication had been fair. Not every original study was equally easy to repeat, and the paper does not pretend the hundred were a random draw from all of human psychology. They were a draw from what leading journals had been printing. That is the population the crisis was about.

What they found

The headline contrast is the one the card quotes. Ninety-seven per cent of the original studies reported a statistically significant effect. About 36 per cent of the replications did. The effects that were recovered were real often enough to matter, and they were systematically smaller, on average about half the published size. A reader who remembers only the 36 per cent will think the literature is a coin toss that came up tails. A reader who remembers only the half will think the literature is essentially right and merely enthusiastic. The paper's point is the conjunction. Many published findings did not reappear as significant results when someone ran them again, and the ones that reappeared had been advertised as larger than the second look could support. Both facts are what one would expect if journals prefer significant results, if analyses are flexible enough to coax a result over the line, and if small samples make the results that do cross the line look bigger than they are.

The variation inside the hundred is as important as the average. Some findings replicated cleanly. Some pointed in the same direction weakly. Some did not return at all. The paper is an estimate of a literature's reproducibility, not a stamp on each member. Using the aggregate to sneer at a particular study that was not in the hundred, or to anoint one that was, abuses a project that was built to answer a collective question. The collective answer is the one Folio's card leads with. The published record of those journals, in that period, was not a reliable guide to what a repetition would find.

How the authors read the estimate

The Collaboration read the estimate as a property of practices, not as a discovery that psychologists are unusually careless. The practices had been described before this paper, and the card names the descriptions. John Ioannidis had argued in 2005 that, under small samples, small effects and flexible designs, most published findings should be expected to be false. Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn had shown in 2011 how ordinary analytic choices, trying several outcomes, adding observations until a result appears, setting aside a condition that did not work, could make a false hypothesis look established. Their demonstration that those choices could produce a significant effect of a song on a reported age is the card's memorable proof that flexibility is not a minor bookkeeping issue. Publication bias, the preference for positive results, then freezes the successes into the journal and leaves the failures in a drawer.

The 2015 paper is what those mechanisms predict, measured in the currency of actual replications. It does not need to catch a particular team making a particular choice. The aggregate is the fingerprint. A success rate near 100 per cent in print, and a much lower success rate in repetition, with shrunken effects, is the fingerprint of a system that selects and inflates. The authors' tone is diagnostic. The estimate is painful because the journals were real journals and the studies were real studies. It is useful because a field that can measure its own reproducibility can decide what to change. The changes the card lists, preregistration, registered reports accepted before the results exist, larger collaborative samples, open data and open code, are responses to this estimate rather than decorations on it.

Limits

A replication is a judgement about what the essential procedure was. Reasonable people can disagree about a manipulation, a population, or an outcome, and some of the hundred replications were criticised on those grounds by the original authors. The disagreement is a limit. It is not a general pardon. A project that finds about a third of replications significant will contain some unfair repetitions and some generous ones, and the estimate is unlikely to be an artefact of unfairness alone, because the direction of the shrinkage is so consistent. Effects got smaller. That is what inflation looks like when it is asked to show its work.

The other limit is the one the card's deep dive insists on. Reproducibility is not one number for all of psychology, still less for all of science. Effects differ by method, by sample, and by how much freedom the original analysis enjoyed. Later work found that some classic findings in judgement, anchoring and framing among them, replicate well, and that many striking social-priming results do not. A subsequent effort to replicate experiments published in Nature and Science found a better, though still incomplete, rate of success, and found that researchers' bets could predict which studies would survive. Those differences are why this paper is an estimate for a defined set of journal articles, not a law of the mind. Quoting 36 per cent as the chance that any given psychological claim is true is a misuse of a careful sample.

What the estimate changed

The practical change is a change in what counts as a finished study. A significant result in a small sample, analysed until it appeared, is no longer the end of the conversation in the corners of the field this paper reached. Registration is a way of tying the analyst's hands before the p-value is known. A registered report is a way of getting the method accepted before the outcome can sweeten or doom it. Multi-lab studies are a way of making the sample a property of the question rather than of one supervisor's budget. Open materials are a way of making the next replication a repetition rather than a guess. None of these repairs guarantees truth. They attack the specific leaks the 2015 estimate revealed, the leaks between a result that can be coaxed and a result that can be repeated.

Folio's other cards on famous findings, ego depletion and power posing among them, are case studies a reader can now place against a base rate. They are members of a literature whose reproducibility this project measured, not bizarre exceptions to a record that otherwise held.

Why it matters here

The card called The replication crisis: how psychology checked its own work is, above all, a card about this paper. The 270 researchers, the 100 studies, the three journals, the drop from 97 per cent significant to about 36 per cent, and the effects that came back about half as large, are the check. Ioannidis supplied the warning that the check might fail. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn supplied the mechanism, ordinary flexibility, by which a false result could look true. Later projects supplied the variation, the effects that hold and the effects that do not. Read the 2015 paper for the estimate itself, and for the tone in which a field can publish a disappointing number about its own journals and call the publication progress. The progress is the willingness to count. The count is what the rest of the crisis is a response to.
