In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration reported what happened when 270 researchers repeated 100 psychology studies. Each repeat took one key result from a paper published in 2008 in Psychological Science, the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, or the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition. Teams used the original materials when they could get them, and the plan for each replication was set out in advance. Of the 100 original results, 97 had been statistically significant. Of the 100 replications, 36 percent were.

The effects also shrank. Put on a common scale, the average original result was a correlation of 0.403. The average replication result was 0.197, about half as large. In 47 percent of cases the original effect size still landed inside the 95 percent confidence interval of the replication. Asked to judge their own repeats, the teams said 39 percent had reproduced the original result. If the original and the replication were simply pooled, and if one assumed the original number was unbiased, 68 percent of the combined tests were still significant. What predicted a successful repeat better than the characteristics of the original team or the replication team was the strength of the original evidence. There is no single verdict in the paper. Significance, effect size, the teams' own judgment, and a pooled test all pointed the same way: a large share of the published results did not come back at anything like their published size.

A surprising result in a good journal is a lead. For a manager, a clinician, or a teacher about to change a practice because one experiment found a clever effect, the project is a base rate. A repeat often comes back smaller, and often it is not distinguishable from nothing. The studies that survived tended to be the ones whose original evidence was already stronger, so sample size and effect size are the first questions, ahead of the journal's name. Ask whether anyone has run the study again, under a plan written before the new data arrived, before betting a decision on the first report.

Source. Open Science Collaboration, 2015, "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science," Science, 349(6251), aac4716. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aac4716
