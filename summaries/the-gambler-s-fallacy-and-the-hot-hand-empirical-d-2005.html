Laboratory studies had long shown that people misread random sequences. Rachel Croson, then at the Wharton School, and James Sundali of the University of Nevada, Reno, wanted to know whether the same mistakes appear when real gamblers bet their own money. Gamblers have every reason to learn the game, and they get instant feedback, so the casino floor is a hard test for any bias.

Casino executives in Reno, Nevada, gave them security videotapes of a single roulette table. The data covered 18 hours of play, in which 139 players placed 24,131 bets. Roulette suits the question well: each spin is independent, and the even-money outside bets, red or black, odd or even, low or high, let a player bet with or against a recent streak.

They counted a bet as consistent with the gambler's fallacy if it went against a streak, for example a bet on black after red had won the last four spins. Players were significantly more likely to bet against a streak than with it, and the pattern grew with the streak's length. After streaks of six or more, 85% of bets were placed against the streak.

The same data showed the opposite illusion too. After a win, players placed bets on more numbers on the next spin, which fits the hot hand belief that a winner is on a roll. So people treated the wheel as if it would even out, and themselves as if their luck would run.

What this means in real life: a run of reds tells you nothing about the next spin. The wheel, like a coin, does not keep score.

Caution: the authors call the biases small but significant. The sample came from one casino and its tapes, and a bet against a streak does not prove what each player believed. Some may have been following a betting system rather than a gut feeling.
