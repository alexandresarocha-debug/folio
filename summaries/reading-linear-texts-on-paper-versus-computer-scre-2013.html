Norwegian schools were moving towards PDF texts on computers, including in tests. Anne Mangen and colleagues at the University of Stavanger asked whether that shift affects how well pupils understand what they read.

They tested 72 tenth graders, aged 15 and 16, from two schools. The pupils were randomly divided into two groups. One read two texts of 1,400 to 2,000 words, one narrative and one expository, on paper; the other read the same texts as PDFs on a computer screen. Pretests of vocabulary and word reading showed that the groups started out similar.

Pupils who read on paper scored significantly better on the comprehension test than those who read on screen. The researchers had also expected the expository text to suffer more on screen, but there was no difference between the two genres.

Their explanation centred on navigation. Long texts on screen require scrolling, which the authors argue creates a spatial instability that can weaken the reader's mental picture of the text. On paper, each passage has a fixed place on a page that readers can see and return to.

What this means in real life: when a long text needs to be understood well, such as in a test or a study session, printed copies may help, and digital tests may not measure the same thing as paper ones.

Caution: this was a single, small study in one country, with a fairly homogeneous group of pupils. It used PDFs on desktop computers, not tablets or e-readers, and could not separate scrolling from other differences between the media.

Source. Mangen, A., Walgermo, B. R., and Brønnick, K. (2013). Reading linear texts on paper versus computer screen: effects on reading comprehension. International Journal of Educational Research, 58, 61-68. doi:10.1016/j.ijer.2012.12.002. Link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijer.2012.12.002
