In 1987 Robert Plomin, then at Pennsylvania State University, and Denise Daniels, a postdoctoral researcher at Stanford, asked a question every parent of two children has asked: why are children in the same family so different from one another? Their answer turned a common belief upside down. The environment matters a lot, they argued, but mostly the parts of it that siblings do not share.

The twin data came first. In a large study of high-school twins by John Loehlin and Robert Nichols, personality questionnaires gave correlations of about .50 for identical twins and .30 for non-identical twins. That pattern points to about 40% genetic influence and 60% environment, and more than 80% of the environmental part was non-shared environment plus measurement error. Error alone was about 20% of the total. In over 12,000 adult twin pairs in Sweden, extraversion correlated .51 for identical twins and .21 for non-identical twins.

Adoption gave the most direct test. Adopted children raised together share a family but not genes, so their similarity measures the shared family environment. In four adoption studies of personality, adoptive siblings correlated on average .04, and adoptive parents and children .05. For most psychological traits, the authors wrote, these correlations hover near zero.

IQ looked like the exception. Across 11 studies, adoptive siblings' IQ correlated about .30. But those children mostly still lived at home. A study of adoptees past adolescence found -.03, and a study of 52 adoptive sibling pairs aged 9 to 15 found -.16. The shared home seemed to shape IQ in childhood, then fade.

So what are the non-shared experiences? The authors pointed to parents treating each child differently, relationships between siblings, and life outside the home. Birth order had over 1,000 entries in the research index, yet its links to IQ and personality were weak. They also named a gloomy prospect: that the key events are chance ones. Darwin wrote that his career depended on an uncle offering to drive him thirty miles, and on the shape of his nose, which nearly made the Beagle's captain turn him down.

What this means in real life: two children in one home do not live the same childhood.

Caution: the non-shared share is what is left after genes and shared family, so it includes measurement error. Twin and adoption studies rest on their own assumptions. The 2011 reprint appeared with four commentaries, and later work found that the specific differences people measure explain little.
