Robin Warren, a pathologist at Royal Perth Hospital in Western Australia, had seen small curved bacteria on stomach biopsies and noticed that they went with inflammation of the stomach lining. Barry Marshall, then a young clinical fellow, wanted to know whether they mattered. Their paper appeared in The Lancet on 16 June 1984.

The design was simple and careful. During 12 weeks, 184 patients were seen by the gastroenterology unit. Of the 84 excluded, 5 refused consent, 4 had medical reasons not to take a biopsy and 75, mostly unbooked cases, could not be invited. The remaining 100 were consecutive patients who agreed to gastroscopy. Small pieces of the lining of the lower stomach were taken from healthy-looking areas, away from any ulcer. The pathology, the microbiology and the questionnaires were done separately and the results were not put together until the end.

Spiral or curved bacteria were present in 58 of the 100. Silver staining found them best. Gastritis was present in 55 of the 57 biopsies with bacteria, a correlation the authors called remarkable. The link to ulcers was close: of the 31 patients with a peptic ulcer, 29 had gastritis, and the bacteria were found in nearly all patients with active chronic gastritis, duodenal ulcer or gastric ulcer. The match persisted when the ulcer patients were excluded, which suggested the bacteria were not just growing in an ulcer crater. A second pathologist who graded 40 of the biopsies blind agreed on the presence or absence of gastritis in 36 of them.

The part everyone remembers is the culture. Specimens from 96 patients were grown, and 11 produced the organism. No bacteria grew from the first 34 cases, probably because plates were discarded after 2 days. Then a plate that had been left for 6 days over the Easter holiday grew the first positive result, and after that cultures were kept for 4 days. The organism was gram-negative, flagellate and microaerophilic, and looked like a new species related to Campylobacter. It was later named Helicobacter pylori.

What this means in real life: most stomach ulcers turned out to come from a treatable infection, not from a lifestyle failing. The Nobel committee puts the share at over 90 percent of duodenal ulcers and up to 80 percent of gastric ulcers. The paper also shows how a discovery can hang on a small practical detail, such as how long to keep a dish.

Limits: the authors state that cause and effect cannot be proved in a study of this kind. They believed the bacterium was related to chronic gastritis and probably to ulcers. That had to wait for the self-experiment and treatment trials.
