The 1000 Genomes Project set out to list the common ways human DNA varies. Its final paper, published in Nature in 2015, describes the genomes of 2,504 people from 26 populations across Africa, Europe, East Asia, South Asia and the Americas. In total the team found more than 88 million variant sites: 84.7 million single-letter changes, called SNPs, 3.6 million short insertions and deletions, and 60,000 larger structural variants.

The most useful number for everyday questions is what one person carries. The paper reports that a typical genome differs from the human reference genome at 4.1 million to 5.0 million sites. Set against a genome of about 3 billion letters, that is roughly one site in every 600 to 750, or about 0.15 percent. This is where the familiar line that any two people are about 99.9 percent the same comes from.

That figure counts places, not amounts of DNA. More than 99.9 percent of the variants are SNPs and short indels, but the rare big ones cover more ground. A typical genome holds about 2,100 to 2,500 structural variants, including about 1,000 large deletions, about 160 copy-number variants and about 915 inserted Alu elements, and together they affect about 20 million bases. Once those are counted, the share of DNA that differs rises to well over half a percent.

The paper also shows where the differences fall. A typical genome has only 149 to 182 sites where a variant cuts a protein short, and 10,000 to 12,000 sites that change a protein's sequence. By contrast it has 459,000 to 565,000 variant sites in known regulatory regions, the promoters, enhancers and other switches that control when and how strongly genes are used. So most of the variation that might matter changes the dials on shared genes, rather than giving people different genes.

Two more findings are worth knowing. People of African ancestry carry the most variant sites, as the out-of-Africa model of human origins predicts. And while most common variants are shared across the world, rarer ones are usually confined to closely related populations, so 86 percent of all variants were found in only one continental group.

What this means in real life: the data are a reference panel used in medical genetics, for example to fill in missing genotypes in disease studies. They are also a good answer to anyone who thinks human groups are deeply different at the DNA level.

Caution: the project mostly used low-coverage sequencing, on average about 7 to 8 reads per site. That is good for common variants but misses some rare ones and many hard-to-read structural variants, so the counts are best read as lower bounds.

Source. The 1000 Genomes Project Consortium (2015). A global reference for human genetic variation. Nature, 526(7571), 68-74. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature15393
