One story, told as if it were the story

Joseph Campbell's The Hero with a Thousand Faces, published in 1949, argues that the myths of the world are versions of a single pattern, which he calls the monomyth. The pattern is the adventure of a hero. Campbell did not invent the comparison of myths, and he did not invent the idea that heroic tales share incidents. His claim is larger. The shared incidents are a necessary sequence, the sequence is a map of psychic transformation, and the local gods, beasts and kingdoms are costumes on a common structure. He draws on comparative mythology and on Jungian psychology, and he writes in a style that is closer to a modern myth than to a folklorist's report. The book has been enormously influential and, among specialists, sharply disputed, which follows from the size of the claim.

Campbell's own summary of the nuclear unit is the right place to start, because the rest of the book is an expansion of it. A hero ventures from the ordinary world into a region of supernatural wonder, wins a decisive victory, and comes back with the power to bestow benefits on others. Separation, initiation, return. The formula is his, and it is already an argument. It says that the meaning of the adventure is not the marvels in the middle. The meaning is the circuit, the going out and the coming back changed, with something to give. A hero who only wanders, or who wins and does not return, has not completed the monomyth, however exciting the episodes. The boon for the community is part of the shape, not a moral glued on afterwards.

The stages of the circuit

Within the circuit Campbell describes a sequence of stages, grouped under departure, initiation and return. The hero begins in the ordinary world and receives a call to adventure, often in the form of a herald or a strange event. The call is frequently refused. Refusal is not a failure of the pattern. It is a stage of it, the clinging of the old self to the life that the adventure will end. Supernatural aid then appears, a mentor or a talisman, and the hero crosses a threshold into the unknown, sometimes by way of what Campbell calls the belly of the whale, an image of being swallowed into the new world rather than merely walking into it. The crossing matters because the monomyth is a story about two realms. Without a threshold there is no initiation, only a trip.

Initiation is the long middle. The hero travels a road of trials, meets figures who test and tempt, and moves towards a supreme ordeal. Campbell's names for some of these meetings, the goddess, the temptress, the atonement with the father, are heavily marked by the psychoanalytic tradition he is using, and they show how much interpretation is already in the taxonomy. The ordeal leads to an apotheosis and to the ultimate boon, the thing the quest was for. Return is a sequence of its own, and Campbell is unusual among popular tellers of his theory in refusing to treat the return as automatic. The hero may refuse to come back, may flee with the boon pursued by forces of the other world, may need rescue from outside, and must cross the return threshold. The completion is the master of two worlds, at home in both, and the freedom to live. A story that skips the return has, on this account, left the hero in the special world and the community without the benefit the adventure existed to produce.

The psychological claim underneath the folklore

Campbell would be a classifier of tales if the stages were the whole of the book. They are not. The monomyth is offered as a magnification of rites of passage and as a map of the psyche. The hero's death and rebirth, the meeting with parental figures, the winning of a boon, are read as the psyche's own work, made visible in public narrative. Myths, in a formula associated with him, are public dreams, and dreams are private myths. The claim explains why he is willing to assemble examples from cultures that had no historical contact. If the pattern belongs to the structure of the mind, contact is unnecessary. Diffusion of tales would be one mechanism. An independent invention from a common psychic source would be another, and he is content with both, because either fills his net.

This is also where the argument is most exposed. A psychological universal is a powerful reason to ignore counterexamples, and Campbell's method has been accused, fairly, of selecting and flattening. Tales that fit are exhibited as faces of the one hero. Tales that do not fit are set aside, or their awkward parts are read symbolically until they fit. The male hero is the default person of the adventure, and the roles assigned to women are often functions of his development. Non-Western traditions are cited widely and interpreted quickly, in the service of a pattern that was built from a modern psychological theory as much as from the traditions. Folklorists, whose business is the patient comparison of versions, have generally preferred more empirical approaches. Alan Dundes was a pointed critic of the method. Vladimir Propp's morphology of a defined corpus of Russian tales, published two decades before Campbell's book and widely read in the West only later, is the obvious contrast: a sequence derived from a stated body of evidence, rather than a sequence illustrated by a world of evidence.

What the book changed anyway

The influence of the book does not depend on the universal claim being true. George Lucas has said that Campbell's work influenced Star Wars, and the admission sealed a feedback loop. A theory that presented itself as a description of existing myth became a prescription for new myth, and the new myth then looked like evidence for the theory. In the 1980s Christopher Vogler, working as a story analyst, condensed Campbell into a practical memo for film development, later published as The Writer's Journey. The twelve-stage template that resulted is more orderly than Campbell's book and less interested in metaphysics. It is the form in which most working writers now meet the monomyth. Through that form, the pattern has shaped a great many films and novels, which means that a reader today will find the monomyth confirmed by stories that were built to confirm it. The confirmation is real as a fact about the industry. It is not independent evidence about world mythology.

There have been replies inside the same imaginative territory rather than only from outside it. Maureen Murdock's The Heroine's Journey was written in part as a response to the male centre of Campbell's model, arguing that a woman's passage does not simply repeat the hero's circuit with a different pronoun. Other critics have pointed out that plenty of lasting stories are not returns with a boon. Tragedy, in Aristotle's sense, ends in a fall produced by an error. Freytag's pyramid ends in a catastrophe. A folklore corpus of the kind Propp studied contains heroes who are recognisably Campbell's and also contains patterns Campbell's sequence has to stretch to include. The monomyth describes a family of stories about transformation by ordeal and return. It does not describe the family of all stories, and Campbell's title is less cautious than his evidence.

Using the pattern without swearing the oath

A writer can still take the circuit as a tool. The questions Campbell makes available are good questions. What is the ordinary world, so that departure means something? What is the refusal protecting? Where is the threshold? What is the ordeal that the earlier trials were leading towards? What is the boon, and who is it for? What does the return cost? These questions are older than Campbell, and they are sharper when they are asked as questions rather than ticked as stages. A story that has no mentor has not thereby failed. A story that refuses the return, and means the refusal, may be more honest about its hero than a story that drags him home to satisfy the template. The book is at its best when the sequence is felt as a rhythm of separation and return, and at its worst when the rhythm is offered as a law that every myth obeys and every screenplay should.

The psychological reading can be kept at a similar distance. It is illuminating to notice that adventures externalise an inner change, and that a threshold in a story often corresponds to a point after which the protagonist cannot go back to the original desire. It is a different thing to decide that the correspondence proves a collective unconscious, or that a tale from a culture one knows slightly is the same tale one already believes in. Campbell's book encourages both the illumination and the overreach. They are separable. The illumination is why the book remains worth reading. The overreach is why the reading should be done next to a folklorist, and next to at least one myth the pattern does not want to hear about.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The hero's journey and its critics, is an argument about this book and about what to do with it. The card states the monomyth, the circuit of call, refusal, threshold, mentor, trials, ordeal, reward and transformed return, and the formula in which the hero comes back able to benefit others. It records Lucas, Vogler's memo, and the spread of the structure into films and novels. It also records the critics: selected and flattened examples, neglected counterexamples, a male centre, a superficial treatment of traditions that are not the author's own, and the preference of folklorists for more empirical work. Propp is the card's example of that empirical alternative, and he has a card of his own. The recommendation Folio ends on is the right recommendation for Campbell. Treat the monomyth as one useful pattern among many, not as a universal law of storytelling. The book is the pattern's most influential statement. It is not the last word on the stories it collects.
