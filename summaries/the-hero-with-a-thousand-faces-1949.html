Joseph Campbell, in The Hero with a Thousand Faces (1949), says the usual path of a hero's adventure is an enlargement of the rites of passage: separation, initiation, and return. He calls that the nuclear unit of the monomyth, a word he takes from James Joyce. The hero sets out from "his common-day hut or castle," by his own choice or because he is carried off, and comes to a threshold. A shadow figure guards the passage. He may defeat or conciliate that figure and go alive into the dark, or be slain and descend in death. Past the threshold he meets forces that threaten him and forces that help him. At the bottom of the journey he faces a supreme ordeal and gains a reward: a sacred marriage, recognition by the father, his own divinization, or the theft of the boon he came for. Then he has to get back. If the powers bless him, he returns under their protection. If not, he flees and is pursued. At the return threshold the transcendent powers stay behind. The boon he brings is supposed to restore the world.

Under those three headings he sets out seventeen steps. Five belong to the departure, including the call, the refusal, supernatural aid, the first threshold, and the belly of the whale. Six belong to the initiation, from the road of trials through the meeting with the goddess and atonement with the father to the ultimate boon. Six belong to the return, from the refusal to come back through the magic flight to freedom to live. He draws the steps from a wide mix of stories, among them the Buddha, Odysseus, and fairy tales, and he reads them with Freud and, especially, Jung. The claim is not that every tale contains a dragon and a wedding. It is that the stories people call heroic keep rehearsing a going out, a trial, and a coming back with something for the community.

The shape is a powerful way to notice what a story is doing. A character who only wins, and never crosses into a world with different rules, has not taken this journey. A character who wins the boon and stays in the special world has not finished it. The trouble starts when the seventeen steps are treated as a quota. Folklorist Barre Toelken, in The Dynamics of Folklore (1996), wrote that Campbell could construct a monomyth of the hero only by citing the stories that fit his preconceived mold and leaving out equally valid stories that did not fit the pattern. A tale can be about a trick, a marriage negotiation, or a landscape, and still be a real story, without a departure into supernatural wonder and a boon brought home. Use the three-part path as a question about the story in front of you. Do not use it as a test the story has to pass.
