In 2006 Jerry Burger, at Santa Clara University, ran the closest thing ethics boards would allow to Stanley Milgram’s obedience procedure. He stopped at 150 volts. That is the switch where, in Milgram’s voice-feedback condition, the learner first demands to be let out. Burger chose it because it was the fork in the original data. Of the forty men in that condition, fourteen stopped before 450 volts. Six of those fourteen stopped at 150 volts, and one had stopped earlier. Of the thirty-three who continued past 150 volts, twenty-six, or 79 percent, went all the way to 450. If you know what someone does at 150 volts, you have a decent guess about the rest of the board.

Seventy adults took part, twenty-nine men and forty-one women, ages twenty to eighty-one, with an average age of about forty-three. They were recruited by newspaper ads, an online listing, and flyers, and paid fifty dollars for two short sessions. People who had taken more than two psychology classes were screened out, and a clinical psychologist excluded anyone who seemed likely to be harmed. Participants were told at least three times, twice in writing, that they could leave and keep the money. The sample shock they felt was 15 volts, not the 45 volts Milgram had used. In the base condition, 28 of 40 people, 70 percent, pressed the 150-volt switch and went on, and the experimenter had to stop them. In Milgram’s comparable condition, 33 of 40, 82.5 percent, had continued past that point. The gap was not statistically significant. In a second condition a confederate refused first. Burger expected that model to free people. It did not do so clearly: 19 of 30, 63.3 percent, still continued. Women continued about as often as men.

For anyone managing a team, a ward, or a training, the useful detail is the 150-volt moment. The dangerous pattern is a series of small steps and a first real objection that gets waved aside. Once people have continued past the objection, most of them, in Milgram’s full procedure, did not find a later place to stop. Saying in advance that someone may quit, and paying them either way, did not by itself produce a mass refusal in 2006.

This was a partial rerun. Nobody was allowed onto the upper half of the board, so it cannot show what these seventy people would have done at 300 or 450 volts. It also did not repeat Milgram’s strongest antidote. When two peers in the 1974 series got up and refused, 36 of 40 participants defied the experimenter. One quiet refusal, seen before the participant was deep into the task, did not have that effect.
