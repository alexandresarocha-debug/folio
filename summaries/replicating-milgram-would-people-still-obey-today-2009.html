Background

Jerry Burger's Replicating Milgram: Would people still obey today? appeared in American Psychologist in 2009. It is the paper Folio's card means when it says that a partial replication, stopped at 150 volts for ethical reasons, found a similar willingness to continue. Burger's problem was real. Milgram's full procedure, taken to the point of apparent collapse and silence, would not be approved by a modern ethics board, and should not be. A half-century of argument about whether people would still obey was therefore stuck. Either one repeated a study one should not repeat, or one guessed from old data. Burger looked for a stopping rule inside Milgram's own results that would let a new study ask the question without putting people through the upper end of the board.

The rule he used is simple and, if one accepts it, powerful. In Milgram's data, the decision that mattered came early. Once a participant had continued past the point at which the learner first demanded to be released, that participant usually went on to the maximum. The first clear refusal-point on the script is 150 volts. A study that stops there, Burger argued, captures the obedience decision without the prolonged distress of the original. The 2009 experiment is that study.

The question

The question is whether adults in the United States, decades after Milgram, will still agree to give what they believe is a painful shock to a protesting person when an experimenter asks them to continue. Burger was also asking a second question that the famous condition cannot answer. Does seeing another person refuse make disobedience easier? Milgram had found, in a variation, that defiant peers cut obedience sharply. Burger wanted a version of that comparison inside an ethical design, because an ally is one of the few practical protections the obedience literature suggests. A third, quieter question runs underneath. If the rate at 150 volts looks like Milgram's rate at the same point, the phenomenon is not a relic of 1960s Yale and of people who had never heard of the Second World War as a psychology lesson.

How the study was set up

Burger recruited men and women from the community, not only students, and paid them for taking part. The ethical machinery is part of the method and has to be described as such. People who already knew about Milgram's research were screened out, because a participant who recognises the ruse is not in Milgram's situation. People for whom the procedure might be especially upsetting were screened out in a two-step process. A clinical psychologist was present and had the authority to end a session. Participants were told, more clearly than in the folklore version of Milgram, that they could withdraw. The sample shock used to convince them the generator was real was mild. As soon as the session reached its stopping point, they were told that the shocks had not been real and that the learner was unharmed.

The base condition followed Milgram's script up to 150 volts. The participant was the teacher. The learner was a confederate in another room, audible, making mistakes on a memory task. The generator and the cover story were recognisable descendants of the original. At 150 volts the learner demanded to be let out, and his protests made it clear he wanted the procedure stopped. The experimenter used the same sort of prods Milgram had standardised, asking the teacher to continue because the study required it. If the teacher agreed to go on past that demand, the session ended. Burger did not collect the upper voltages, and he did not claim to have observed them. In a second condition, a second teacher, also a confederate, refused to continue part-way through and pushed away from the table.

What he found

In the base condition, the proportion of participants willing to continue past the learner's demand was similar to the proportion who had gone past the same point in Milgram's study. Burger's conclusion, and the card's, is that the readiness to continue has not obviously shrunk just because the calendar has. Men and women did not differ in any large way. The modelled-refusal condition reduced continuation only modestly. Seeing another person stop was not, in this version, the powerful release that Milgram's peer-rebellion variation had been. Participants still had the experimenter to deal with, the other refuser was a stranger rather than a long ally, and the study ended at the first crisis rather than accumulating pressure. The practical lesson is cautious. An example of disobedience helps less than one might hope, at least when it is a single, brief, scripted refusal in a room that still belongs to the experimenter.

The similarity to Milgram is a similarity at one decision, not a claim that these participants would have gone to 450 volts. Burger is explicit that he used the old data as a bridge: because continuing past 150 volts had strongly predicted going to the end in the original series, a similar rate of continuing past 150 volts is evidence of a similar disposition to obey at the moment the harm becomes unambiguous. A reader who rejects the bridge should reject the headline and keep the narrower finding. At the first serious protest, a majority of Burger's participants did what the experimenter asked. That narrower finding does not need the bridge, and it is already enough to disturb the hope that the original was an antique.

How Burger read it

Burger read the study as a careful yes. People still obey, in the specific sense this design can speak to, at rates that would have surprised the psychiatrists Milgram polled in the 1960s and that do not surprise anyone who has taken the original seriously. He did not read it as proof that nothing has changed in fifty years. His participants were a screened group of adults who did not know the study, sitting in a modern lab with a milder authority figure than the stern man in the famous film, under rules that emphasised the right to leave. If anything, those differences should have made continuation harder. That the rate stayed in Milgram's range is, for Burger, the reason the result is interesting. The phenomenon survived a design that had been softened in every way ethics required.

He also read the modelled-refusal result as a reason to be modest about easy cures. The obedience literature is often raided for the comforting variation in which two peers refuse and almost everyone else refuses too. Burger's weaker effect is a reminder that the comforting variation had a particular shape. The rebels were built into the procedure as fellow teachers from the start, and their refusal was sustained. A single visible no, late and brief, did not hand the participant a new definition of the situation. Changing obedience, if one wanted to change it, would take more than a nearby example. It would take a change in how the authority frames the task, or in how costly refusal feels, or in whether the participant ever accepts the science story in the first place.

Limits

The bridge from 150 volts to the end of the board is the largest inferential step, and it is only as strong as the old regularity. If today's participants who pass 150 volts would in fact stop at 300, Burger's design cannot see them do it, and his claim about full obedience is an extrapolation. The screening cuts both ways. Excluding people who know Milgram makes the test fairer as a replication and less representative of a public that has met the study in school. Excluding people who might be upset makes the procedure ethical and leaves open the possibility that the original's rate was swollen by people a modern study will not enrol. The sample is American and modest in size. One laboratory, one script, and a confederate's performance are a narrow base for a sentence about "people today".

There is an ethical limit that is also a scientific one. Because the debrief comes quickly and the upper shocks are never given, the study cannot observe the long deterioration, the silence, and the teacher's confrontation with what he thinks he has done. Those were part of what made Milgram's sessions so disturbing, and part of the phenomenon. Burger studied the decision to cross the line at which the learner's will is clearly opposed. He did not study what it does to a person to keep crossing it. Anyone who equates the two will over-read the paper. Anyone who dismisses the decision because the rest was not repeated will under-read it.

Why it matters here

On the Milgram card, Burger is the evidence that the famous pattern is not safely in the past. The card's summary says partial replications found similar rates of continuing, and this is the partial replication it names. The walkthrough keeps the similarity attached to its rule. Similar, here, means similar at 150 volts, in a sample screened to be able to bear a stopped procedure, with the right to withdraw stated, and with the full 450-volt outcome inferred rather than observed. That is still a striking result. It is a different result from a claim that two-thirds of modern adults would deliver the maximum shock if asked in the original way.

The card's other cautions stay in force beside this paper and are not answered by it. The 65 per cent figure is one variant. Archival work has found deviations from the script and widespread doubt about the shocks. The prod that sounds most like a direct order was, in the original series, typically refused, which is Haslam and Reicher's reason for talking about engaged followership rather than about blind obedience. Burger shows that the early decision to go along with the experimenter's scientific frame remains easy to obtain. He does not show that a bare order is irresistible, and the card does not say he did. Read him as the ethical, partial, contemporary test, and let the variations in Milgram's book do the work of showing when the rate moves.
