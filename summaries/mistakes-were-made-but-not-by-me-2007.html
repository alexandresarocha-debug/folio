The argument in outline

Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me), by Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson, appeared in 2007 and has been revised since. It is a book about self-justification for a general reader, written by a social psychologist who had spent a career on dissonance and by a writer who had spent a career explaining psychology without letting it off the hook. The claim is that the need to reduce the discomfort of having been wrong, or cruel, or merely inconsistent, leads ordinary people to revise their beliefs, their memories and their portraits of other people, until the self comes out clean. The revisions are sincere. That is the danger. A person who has talked himself into a new past is not lying, and cannot be reached by evidence aimed at a liar. Folio's card points to the book as the readable application of dissonance to politics, law and relationships. The walkthrough follows the book's movement from the mechanism, through memory, through institutions, to the question of how anyone ever admits an error.

Self-justification and the pyramid

Tavris and Aronson begin with Festinger's idea and then add a picture of how small choices become large differences. They call it the pyramid of choice. Two people who are close in their values face a decision that is not yet a matter of identity. One cheats a little, or stays silent, or signs the convenient statement. The other does not. Each then has dissonance to reduce, and each reduces it by becoming more convinced that the choice was the only decent one. The next decision is made by a slightly different person. Over time the two have slid down opposite faces of a pyramid, each looking up at the other with genuine moral horror. The book uses the picture to explain polarisation without assuming that either person set out to become extreme. The extremity is the residue of justifications, and each justification was, at the time, a small repair to a self-image.

The opening chapters insist on a distinction the rest of the book needs. Self-justification is not the same as a deliberate cover-up, though the two can end in the same place. A cover-up knows the truth and hides it. Self-justification changes what the person experiences as the truth, so that there is nothing left to hide. Tavris and Aronson are harsh about the result and careful about the motive. They are not arguing that people are scheming. They are arguing that the scheming theory is too flattering, because it keeps a cool liar in charge. The dissonance process puts the story in the hands of someone who wants to think well of himself and who has the ordinary mental tools to succeed.

Memory as an ally of the present

The memory chapters are the book's bridge from the laboratory to the lives people actually argue about. Tavris and Aronson review research on how recollection is reconstructed, and on how the reconstruction favours the current self. People misremember their past attitudes so that they line up with the attitudes they hold now. They remember their side of a quarrel more charitably than a recording would. They promote their successes into character and demote their failures into circumstances. None of this requires a special pathology. It requires a memory that is a story, rewritten at each telling, under a pressure to keep the teller decent. The practical consequence is that two honest people can produce two incompatible histories of the same marriage, the same meeting, the same war, and each history can feel like playback rather than like advocacy.

They are particularly interested in the cases where a vivid memory is taken as proof. Confidence, detail and emotion are what people use as signs that a memory is accurate, and those are exactly the qualities a reconstructed memory can acquire as it is retold. The book warns against treating the feeling of remembering as evidence. That warning does a great deal of work in the later chapters on therapy and on the law, where a sincere, detailed, emotional account is often the whole of the case. Tavris and Aronson do not conclude that memory is useless. They conclude that memory is a witness with an interest in the verdict, and that institutions which forget the interest will keep consecrating mistakes.

Clinics, courts and the cost of being sure

The institutional chapters apply the same mechanism where the cost of not admitting error is paid by someone else. In the clinic, they examine therapies and recovered-memory practices in which a practitioner's theory tells a patient what must have happened, and the patient's increasingly confident story is then taken as confirmation of the theory. Each step is justified. The therapist believes she is helping. The patient believes she is finally facing the truth. Dissonance makes it costly for either to consider that the procedure produced the memory. The book treats this as a case study in good intentions sliding down the pyramid, and it is written with unusual anger, because the people harmed by a false accusation or by a life rebuilt on a fabricated past do not get the comfort of the practitioner's sincerity.

In the legal chapters the target is the machinery that cannot revisit itself. Interrogations that produce false confessions, prosecutors who treat a developing doubt as a threat to be fought, investigators who notice only the evidence that fits the first suspect: Tavris and Aronson describe these as dissonance processes with badges. Once an institution has accused, punished, or publicly committed itself, new evidence is not neutral information. It is an attack on the competence and decency of everyone who acted. The path of least resistance is to discredit the evidence, the witness, or the person still insisting he did not do it. The book collects cases in which that path was taken and later shown to have been wrong. The point of the collection is the pattern, not the scandal of any one office. A system that rewards certainty and punishes admission will generate self-justification the way a forced-compliance experiment generates attitude change, and for the same structural reason. The act has been done. The justification follows.

Intimacy, politics and the way back

Closer to home, the book looks at couples and at political enmity, where the pyramid is small enough to watch. Each partner in a long conflict keeps a ledger that is accurate about the other's offences and vague about his own. Each ledger reduces dissonance. Together they make reconciliation feel like surrender, because reconciliation would require a revision of the self, not only of the spouse. Political versions of the same ledger let a person hear a flaw in the other side as confirmation and a flaw on his own side as an exception, a media distortion, or a price worth paying. Tavris and Aronson are even-handed about this in a way that is itself a method. They take examples from more than one camp, because a theory of self-justification that only indicts one's opponents is an instance of the thing it describes.

The closing chapters ask what it would mean to stop. Their answer is deliberately unspectacular. Admit a mistake early, while the pyramid is still flat and the admission is cheap. Prefer institutions, including scientific ones, that treat a correction as a success rather than as a disgrace. Listen for the moment when the urge to explain oneself away is strongest, and treat that urge as information about dissonance rather than as information about the facts. They do not pretend this is natural. The whole book has argued that the natural path is the path that keeps the self intact. Getting off it requires a value placed on accuracy that is strong enough to compete with the value placed on being the good person in the story. Some people and some professions manage it some of the time. The book is a brief for making it easier, and a warning about the places, the interrogation room, the closed clinic, the marriage that has become a case, where it has been made nearly impossible.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on cognitive dissonance offers this book as the application of the theory to politics, law and relationships, once the reader has met the 1957 statement, the prophecy study and the one-dollar experiment. Tavris and Aronson do not add a new mechanism. They show the old one at the scale where it does damage: a memory that edits, a prosecution that cannot retreat, a couple who each remember a different decade, a citizen who has slid, choice by choice, into a certainty he could not have predicted of himself. The pyramid is their one original teaching image, and it is worth keeping because it explains how people who started as neighbours end as enemies without either having planned the trip.

The card's caution belongs in the same reading. A 2024 multi-lab replication did not reproduce the classic induced-compliance effect, so a reader should not treat every laboratory demonstration in the dissonance tradition as settled. This book is not that demonstration. It is an argument that the self-justifying pattern, however it is ultimately explained, is common in institutions that punish apology and reward certainty. That argument can survive a change in the laboratory status of one paradigm, and it is the argument the card is pointing to when it calls the book readable and useful. Read Festinger for the theory. Read this for the places the theory hurts.
