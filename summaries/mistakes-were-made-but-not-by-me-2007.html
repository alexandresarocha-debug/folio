Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson open Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me), published in 2007, with the feeling Leon Festinger named cognitive dissonance: the strain of holding two ideas that do not fit. Their smoker believes “this could kill me” and “I smoke two packs a day.” Quitting would end the strain. What people more often do, the authors write, is edit the belief. The evidence is overstated. The cigarettes calm them. Weight gain would be worse. The habit stays, and the story around it gets more ingenious. The book’s subject is that edit, applied to foolish beliefs, bad decisions, and hurtful acts, by people who are sure the mistake was someone else’s.

Their picture of how a small choice hardens is the pyramid. Two young men, alike in honesty and in a middling view of cheating, sit an exam that will decide graduate school. Each hits a blank on a crucial question, and each has an easy look at another student’s answers. One cheats. One does not. The decisions were close. A week later, the one who cheated has decided that everyone does it and that his future required it. The one who refused has decided that cheaters should be expelled. They started near each other. Self-justification, the authors say, slides them to opposite corners of the base, and each man is sure he has always felt that way. The book then follows the same slide through prosecutors faced with a wrongful conviction, therapists who had encouraged “recovered” memories, and couples rewriting a fight so that the other person started it.

At work and in medicine the pyramid is a reason to write the reasons down before the choice, and to separate the person who decides from the person who reviews. A hiring manager who has championed a candidate, a surgeon who has recommended an operation, or a team that has spent a year on a product will find it painful to say the choice was poor. The pain is lower if the review belongs to someone who did not make the choice, and if the first doubts are recorded while the two options still look similar. Once the story has been polished in public, asking for a reversal asks the person to call their earlier self a fool.

The book is an argument from cases, not a new experiment. The mechanism it borrows, that a freely chosen inconsistent act rewrites the attitude, is the piece a 2024 multilab study failed to reproduce: among 4,898 people in 39 labs, high choice and low choice did not differ after a counterattitudinal essay. Self-justification in the wild is easy to see. Producing it on demand, with choice as the lever, has proved harder.
