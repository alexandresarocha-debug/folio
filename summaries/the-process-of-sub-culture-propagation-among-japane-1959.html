Syunzo Kawamura's 1959 paper in Primates is the early English account of how a new feeding habit moves through Japanese macaques. He sets three provisioned troops next to each other. On Koshima, in Miyazaki Prefecture, the new habit is washing sweet potatoes. At Takasakiyama, in Oita Prefecture, it is eating candy. At Minoo, in Osaka Prefecture, it is eating wheat. The shape he describes is the same in each place. A new food habit starts with one animal. It then reaches that animal's close family, especially the mother when the inventor is a juvenile, and the other juveniles who are there at feeding time.

The animals most likely to miss it are the subadult and adult males. As a male grows he spends more time at the edge of the troop and has fewer chances to watch the animals who already do the new thing. Kawamura records an exception at Minoo that shows the rule by breaking it. An adult male there already ate the wheat when it was put out. The group's top-ranking male began to do the same, then the dominant female, then her offspring. Whichever way it travels, Kawamura's point is that the animal who already has the habit is not teaching. There is no lesson. Another animal is close enough to copy.

Kawai, watching Koshima through the 1950s, treats Kawamura as the map of the first years. Kawamura had named the two routes, playmates and kin, and had noted that inside a family the copy often goes from younger to older. He had also recorded, in 1954, that the first potato washing on Koshima was done in a freshwater brook, not in the sea. The sea came later. By 1956, eleven monkeys on the island washed potatoes. That is a slow list, not a crowd.

In ordinary life this is how a small shortcut actually travels. It moves through the people who share a bench, a shift, or a kitchen, and it can start with whoever tried it first, not with the person in charge. The people who no longer sit in that room can miss it completely. Sending a notice is not the same as being watched while you do the thing.

The hundredth-monkey story later flattened this paper into a fairy tale about a sudden leap. Lyall Watson in 1979 and Ken Keyes in 1982 claimed that once enough monkeys knew the trick, the rest knew it at once, including troops the learners had never met. Ron Amundson, in the Skeptical Inquirer in 1985, set that claim against the field counts. The spread Kawamura and Kawai described is copy by copy, along kinship and along play, and it leaves some animals out.
