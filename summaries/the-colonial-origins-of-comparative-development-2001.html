Background

The research programme that runs through Why Nations Fail needed a result that was not a set of well-told stories. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson supplied it in 2001, in the American Economic Review, under the title The Colonial Origins of Comparative Development. The background is the difficulty of proving that institutions cause growth. Everyone can see that rich countries have rules which protect investors and poor countries often do not. The seeing proves nothing. Wealth may buy the rules. Culture may buy both. Geography may make some places productive and also, by a separate route, easier to govern. A persuasive paper has to find a reason why some societies got one kind of institution rather than another, a reason that is not itself a disguised measure of their present income. Colonial history, the authors argue, contains such a reason, because European empires set up very different regimes in different places and because those regimes had a tendency to last.

The question

The question is whether differences in institutions across former colonies cause differences in income per head, once the reverse effect of income on institutions has been removed. They are not asking whether education, trade, or oil can also matter. They are asking whether the institutional bundle, summarised by the security of property against expropriation, has a large causal role of its own. A second question rides with the first. After institutions are given that role, is there anything left for the favourite geographic variables, latitude and the disease environment as it is now, to explain? The paper is designed so that a yes to the first question and a no to the second would move the debate. A correlation, however pretty, would not.

Design

The design is an instrument built from colonial settlement. The authors take, as their measure of institutions today, an index of protection against the risk of expropriation, a commercial assessment of how safe capital was in each country over a run of recent years. That index is endogenous. They need something that pushed the index around without being a product of modern income. The something is the mortality faced by European settlers at the time of colonisation. In colonies where Europeans died at high rates, settlement was thin, and the colonial power built extractive institutions, slave plantations, forced labour, monopoly companies, a state whose job was to remit wealth to a metropole and to a local few. In colonies where Europeans survived, they arrived in numbers, and they reproduced institutions that gave a wider settler public some protection against the executive. The authors take the historical mortality rates, many of them from the medical history of colonial armies, and use them as the instrument. The first stage of the design asks whether high settler mortality predicts worse expropriation protection now. The second stage asks whether the part of the institutional score that is predicted by that mortality also predicts income.

The exclusion restriction is the sentence on which the whole paper depends. Settler mortality of several centuries ago must affect present income only by affecting institutions, not by a direct effect of climate or disease on productivity, and not by the human capital that settlers brought in their own persons. The authors' defence is that the diseases which killed unseasoned Europeans are a poor measure of the disease burden on populations who already lived with them, and that they can control for current health and for geography and still see an institutional effect. They also argue for persistence. A colony's early choice of rules shaped the distribution of power, and the distribution of power reproduced the rules after independence. The instrument is therefore a piece of historical epidemiology standing in for a political choice that nobody wrote down as a single variable.

Results they claim

What they claim, and what a careful reader can grant without memorising a coefficient, is a strong first stage and a strong second stage inside their sample of former colonies. Mortality predicts institutions in the expected direction. Institutions, so predicted, predict income in the expected direction, and the estimated effect is large relative to the income gaps among these countries. Geographic variables that look powerful in a simple regression look much weaker once the institutional channel is included. The authors read this as evidence that the colonial inheritance of rules, rather than the colonial inheritance of latitude, is the operative cause of the pattern they study. They report that the finding is not an artefact of one or two countries, and that it survives controls which are meant to catch the current disease environment, so that the past is not merely a proxy for being unhealthy in the present.

The result is about a bundle and a sample, and both limits are visible in the design even before the later controversy. The bundle is one index. A country can move along it by changing many different rules at once, and the paper does not say which rule inside the bundle does the work. The sample is the ex-colonial world. The estimate does not explain institutional differences among countries that were never colonised, and it does not explain the industrial revolution in the metropoles. It explains variation produced by different colonial strategies. That is already a great deal. It is not a theory of everything a nation does.

Interpretation

The interpretation that feeds their later books is a theory of persistence and of political economy. Extractive colonial rules created elites who gained from extraction and who, after independence, inherited the state. Inclusive colonial rules, inclusive for the settlers if not for everyone the settlers dominated, created a wider group with the power to resist confiscation. Development differences are, on this reading, the long shadow of who was allowed to settle and of what they set up when they did. The instrument is clever because the sorter, mortality, was not chosen by the colonists as a policy. It was suffered. Places did not select extractive institutions because they were already poor in the modern sense. They received them because Europeans could not live there and still wanted the surplus, or they received settler institutions because Europeans could live there and insisted on rights for themselves.

This interpretation is the empirical foundation under the inclusive and extractive vocabulary. Without some result of this kind, that vocabulary is a sorting of anecdotes. With it, the anecdotes in the popular book are illustrations of a relationship that was estimated. The direction of dependence should be kept straight. The paper does not become true because the cases are vivid. The cases are a reason to care whether the paper is true.

Limits and later criticism

David Albouy's comment, published in the same journal in 2012, is the criticism that has to be faced. He argued that a large share of the mortality figures were not measured in the country to which they were assigned, but borrowed from other countries on the authors' judgement of which disease environments matched. He argued that rates for soldiers on campaign, for bishops, and for labourers were treated as comparable when the circumstances of death were different, and that campaign mortality in particular inflates the contrast in a way that helps the hypothesis. When he restricted the sample to rates he regarded as actually observed, and when he accounted for the campaign problem, the first stage weakened and the instrumental estimate became too imprecise to carry the conclusion. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson replied that discarding the assigned rates threw out Latin America and much of Africa, a large part of the sample, on principle rather than on evidence, and that a fair treatment of outliers and of the campaign data left their result standing. The exchange is a model of how an empirical historical claim is actually tested. It turns on ledgers, not on rhetoric.

Other limits are conceptual. Settler colonies also received the settlers' skills, and skill is not an institution. An instrument that moves institutions and human capital together cannot hand the whole effect to the expropriation index. The persistence assumption can fail in both directions. Some extractive colonies built better institutions later, and some settler colonies spent a long time being extractive towards the people they displaced or enslaved. The paper's measure, a late twentieth-century risk rating, folds all of that history into one modern number. Used as the foundation of a grand theory, the estimate has to be restated with these qualifications in the same breath as the result. Used as one study of colonial legacies, it remains one of the most influential designs in the field, precisely because its weakest point, the historical mortality table, is visible and therefore arguable.

Why it matters here

The card acemoglu-institutions names this paper as the empirical foundation under Why Nations Fail. The foundation is the settler-mortality instrument, the first stage from mortality to institutions, the second stage from institutions to income, and the claim that geography drops out once institutions are allowed to work. The card's deeper note says that the mortality data have been challenged, most prominently by David Albouy, and that the authors replied. That exchange is part of the meaning of the paper for Folio, not a footnote to be skipped on the way to the policy moral. The inclusive-against-extractive story is only as strong as this design. Reading the design, including the exclusion restriction and the limits of a colonial sample, is the way the card's confidence can be kept proportionate to the evidence.
