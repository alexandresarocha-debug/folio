Forty years after Darley and Latané, Peter Fischer and colleagues pulled together the research on whether the presence of other people makes an individual less likely to help. They searched databases, contacted researchers through mailing lists for unpublished work, and combed reference lists, coding each study for features such as danger, the presence of a perpetrator, and the kind of cost that helping involved.

Pooling more than 7,700 participants and 105 independent effect sizes, they found an overall bystander effect of g = -0.35 in a fixed-effects model and -0.33 with random effects: a real but small-to-moderate tendency for people to help less when others are present.

The effect was not uniform. It was weaker when situations were perceived as dangerous, when a perpetrator was present, and when the cost of helping was physical rather than, say, time or embarrassment. The authors explain this with an arousal-cost-reward account: dangerous emergencies are recognised more quickly and clearly as real, which raises arousal and prompts action. Other people can also be a source of physical support for someone thinking about stepping in.

They checked for publication bias. By one classic calculation, it would take 2,240 unpublished studies with null results to cancel the overall effect, and they found few unpublished studies to include.

What this means in real life: in a minor incident, such as someone dropping papers or feeling faint in a busy station, the crowd may hold people back. In a clearly dangerous situation, others nearby may make intervention more likely, not less.

Caution: most studies were laboratory or staged field experiments, and the fail-safe calculation is a rough and controversial check on publication bias. The analysis measures individual probability of helping, which is not the same as the chance that a victim gets help from someone.
