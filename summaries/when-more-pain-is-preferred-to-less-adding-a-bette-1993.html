Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber, and Donald Redelmeier asked 32 people to put a hand in cold water twice. In the short trial the water was 14°C for 60 seconds. In the long trial it was 14°C for those same 60 seconds, and then the hand stayed in for 30 seconds more while the water was warmed, on average from 14.1°C to 15.2°C. The extra half-minute was still unpleasant. It was only less so. Discomfort ratings at the 60-second mark were nearly identical, 8.44 in the short trial and 8.34 in the long one. During the warmer ending, discomfort fell by an average of 2.65 points. People were then told they would have to repeat one of the trials, and they chose which.

Twenty-two of the 32, 69%, chose the longer trial. The long trial contained every cold second of the short one, and then some more. Among the 21 people whose discomfort fell by two points or more at the end, 17 chose the long trial. The 11 people who felt little or no relief split almost evenly. After the choice, most people also said the long trial had caused less discomfort overall. The minute-by-minute record says otherwise. One participant, having noticed which trial lasted longer, was heard to say, "The choice I made doesn't seem to make much sense."

The memory that guided the choice gave heavy weight to the worst moment and the final moment, and little weight to duration. That matters wherever a person decides whether to repeat an experience from memory. A medical test, a flight, a shift on a hard job, or a customer-service call is later remembered as its peak and its ending. Making the last minutes milder can make the whole episode more acceptable in retrospect, even when the total discomfort went up. The study is a hand in a bucket for a minute and a half. It is a direct case in which the ledger of minutes and the ledger of memory ranked the same two options in opposite orders.
