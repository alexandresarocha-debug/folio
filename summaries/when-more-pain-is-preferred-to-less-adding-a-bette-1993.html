The question the experiment is built to ask

When more pain is preferred to less, published in 1993 by Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber and Donald Redelmeier, is a short paper with a rude implication. If people kept a running total of what an experience felt like while it was happening, a longer spell of pain would be worse than a shorter one, and a spell that added extra unpleasant minutes would be worse still. The authors suspected that people do not keep that total. They remember an episode by a few of its moments, and they choose the next episode on the basis of the memory. The paper asks whether that way of remembering can make someone prefer, quite sincerely, to repeat the option that contained more pain.

The distinction they need is between two ways of evaluating the same stretch of time. One evaluation is built from the moment-by-moment record: how bad it was at each point, added up. The other is the retrospective evaluation, the single judgement a person offers afterwards when asked how bad the episode was, or which episode they would rather go through again. Economics and medicine had both tended to treat those as the same number, or at least as numbers that must agree about which option is worse. The experiment is designed so that they come apart. If the retrospective judgement and the choice follow the added-up record, people will reject the longer pain. If they follow something else, the longer pain can win.

What the two trials actually did

The painful experience was a cold-water immersion, a standard laboratory discomfort that is nasty, safe, and easy to time. In one trial a participant held a hand in water at 14 degrees Celsius for 60 seconds. In the other trial the hand stayed in for those same 60 seconds at 14 degrees, and then for 30 seconds more while the water was warmed slightly, to about 15 degrees. The extra half-minute was still unpleasant. It was only less unpleasant than what had come before. On any adding-up account the second trial is strictly worse: it contains everything the first trial contains, plus a further interval of pain. Nothing in the second trial compensates the person during the experience itself. The compensation, if there is one, exists only in the shape of the ending.

Participants were not asked to theorise about this. They reported discomfort as the trials went on, so the experimenters had a real-time trace and not only a story told later. Those traces are what make the result interpretable. They show that the longer trial really did extend the pain, and that its final moments really were easier than its worst moments. The retrospective question could then be compared with a record the person had already supplied, rather than with the experimenter's assumption about what cold water feels like. When people were asked which trial they would rather repeat, most chose the longer one.

The peak-end rule, and the neglect of duration

The pattern has a name in this paper, and Folio's card is built on it. Retrospective evaluation followed the peak-end rule. The judgement of the episode was dominated by the most intense moment and by the way the episode ended. Duration was largely neglected. The longer trial had a similar peak to the shorter one, because both passed through the same very cold minute, and it had a better end, because it finished on the slightly warmer water. The extra duration, which is the whole of the difference in total pain, dropped out of the comparison. People were not confused about which trial lasted longer. They were using a summary that does not contain duration as a term.

That summary is simple, and the simplicity is the point. A person who stored every moment and then averaged them would have preferred the short trial. A person who stored the worst moment and the last moment, and who weighted those heavily, would prefer the long trial. The choice data line up with the second person. Kahneman and his colleagues treated this as a claim about the remembering of episodes, not as a claim that people enjoy suffering or that they fail to notice clocks. The real-time ratings show an experiencing subject who is tracking the pain while it happens. The choice shows a remembering subject who consults a compressed version afterwards. The two disagree about a question that looks as if it had only one rational answer.

The paper is also careful about what kind of extension produces the effect. The added time has to improve the ending. Adding a worse ending would be expected, on the same rule, to make the memory worse, and adding time that merely copies the peak would not help. The preference for more pain is therefore not a preference for length. It is a preference for a better last moment, purchased by accepting more total discomfort. The title states the resulting paradox from the point of view of the adding-up standard. From the point of view of the memory rule, there is no paradox at all. The longer trial is the better memory, and people choose memories when they choose whether to repeat an experience.

What the authors take the result to mean

The argument does not stop at a curiosity about cold water. If choices among experiences are governed by remembered summaries, then anything that changes the peak or the end can change a decision even when it changes the total in the opposite direction. A procedure, a film, a queue, or a medical test can be made more unpleasant in the integral sense and more acceptable in the memory that later guides behaviour. The authors present this as a problem for any theory that identifies the utility of an episode with the sum of its momentary utilities. The sum is a coherent measure. It is not the measure people consult when they say which episode they are willing to have again.

They are not claiming that duration never matters, or that every experience in life is remembered by the same two moments. The claim is narrower and, for that reason, more useful. In this setting, with a single aversive episode judged shortly afterwards, peak and end dominate, and length is under-used. That is enough to break the assumption that more pain must be preferred less. It is also enough to suggest a practical rule that later papers in the same programme test directly: if you can do nothing about the worst moment, you may still change the memory by changing the finish. The cold-water study is the clean demonstration, because the added finish is still bad, the total is unambiguously worse, and the choice goes the other way anyway.

What this paper does not settle

A laboratory immersion is not a life. The trials are brief, the discomfort is chosen, and the choice is about repeating a known episode rather than about advising a stranger or setting a policy. People might weight duration more heavily for experiences that last hours, or for pleasures rather than pains, or when someone explicitly asks them to think about how long it went on. Later reviews of the peak-end pattern, including Fredrickson's own, have treated it as a good description of some classes of episode and a poor description of others. Duration neglect is a tendency in the way episodes are stored, not a law that forbids people from caring how long something takes when length is made the question.

The paper also does not, by itself, show that clinicians should lengthen real procedures. It shows why that idea is no longer absurd. A better end can improve the memory of a worse total. Whether that trade is worth making in a clinic depends on how large the memory effect is, how the added time feels in a genuinely invasive procedure, and what the memory is being asked to predict. Those are the questions the colonoscopy studies take up. Reading the 1993 paper as if it were already a medical recommendation flattens it. Its job is to separate two ledgers, the ledger of moments and the ledger of memory, and to show a case in which they rank the same options in opposite orders.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The peak-end rule: memory edits your experiences, takes this experiment as the founding demonstration. The 14 degrees, the extra half-minute at about 15 degrees, the greater total pain, and the preference for the longer trial are the card's facts because they are this paper's facts. The card's language of an experiencing self and a remembering self is the later, fuller statement of a contrast the 1993 results already force. The 1996 study of patients and the 2003 randomised colonoscopy trial are intelligible only as the next steps: first, does peak-end describe memories of real treatment, and second, can a deliberately better ending change those memories and the willingness to come back. Read this paper for the logic that makes more pain preferable to less. Read the clinical papers for the test of whether the logic survives contact with medicine.
