A comedy that keeps its promises

Anton Chekhov's The Seagull, written in 1895 and first performed in 1896, is the play people cite when they want an example of his famous economy, and it is also the play that shows how little a mechanical reading of that economy will catch. A young writer, Konstantin Treplev, lives on his uncle Sorin's country estate in the shadow of his mother, the famous actress Irina Arkadina. He loves Nina Zarechnaya, the daughter of a neighbouring landowner, who wants to be an actress. Arkadina's lover, the successful novelist Boris Trigorin, is the kind of writer Konstantin despises and Nina adores. Around them is a small society of mismatched loves. Masha, the steward's daughter, dresses in black and loves Konstantin. The poor schoolteacher Medvedenko loves Masha. Polina loves the doctor Dorn. Almost nobody is loved by the person they want. The play watches this system for two years, and it ends with a gunshot that the company in the drawing room is encouraged not to understand.

The gun is real, and it is the reason the play is recruited as a illustration of the principle later called Chekhov's gun. Konstantin tries to shoot himself and fails, and the attempt is not a rumour. He appears afterwards with a bandage, and his mother, who cannot bear illness or need, has to confront the bandage anyway. At the end of the play he shoots himself again, offstage, while the others play lotto and talk. Dorn hears the shot, tells the room that something harmless in his medical bag has burst, and then, privately, tells Trigorin to get Arkadina away, because Konstantin is dead. A weapon introduced by the failed attempt has been discharged by the successful one. The emphasis was not idle. Anyone looking only for that click, however, will miss what the play thinks emphasis is for. The gun is one promise among several, and the richest promise is not the weapon. It is the seagull, and the disastrous little play in the garden, and the argument about what art is allowed to do to the people who serve it.

The bird, the play, and the writer who takes notes

In the first act Konstantin stages an experimental play outdoors, with Nina as the voice of a world-soul speaking over a lake. He wants new forms, and he wants his mother to be struck by them. Arkadina mocks the performance, the rustic audience is uncomfortable, and the play collapses. The failure is a promise about the rest of the evening and about the rest of Konstantin's life. His demand for new forms will be tested against the old theatre, which his mother embodies and which knows how to hold a room, and against his own incapacity to live with indifference. Nina's taste of performance, even in a fiasco, is also a promise. She has been on a stage, someone has listened, and the hunger that follows is the hunger that will take her to Moscow.

The seagull is placed with an emphasis no audience can miss. Konstantin has shot one and lays it at Nina's feet, identifying himself with the killed bird. Trigorin, a little later, looks at the bird and sees a story. A girl lives by a lake, a man comes along with nothing better to do, and for want of anything else he destroys her, as this gull was destroyed. Nina is enchanted by the idea of being in a story of Trigorin's. Two years later she returns, ruined in the worldly sense, her child dead, her acting career a grind, Trigorin having tired of her, and she both claims the seagull as her name and pushes past it towards a harsher endurance. The symbol pays off, which is the gun principle kept. It pays off by being fought over. Konstantin wanted it to mean his own wounded exception. Trigorin wanted it to mean a plot he could use. Nina has to live in the plot and then refuse to be only its emblem. A detail can fulfil its promise by becoming the subject of a dispute about what promises are worth, which is a more Chekhovian payoff than a single detonation.

Art as a system of extractions

Trigorin is the play's clearest account of what a writer takes from a life. He describes a compulsion that is not glamorous. He cannot walk, fish, or talk without storing a phrase, and the storing empties the experience it stores. Success has not cured this. It has given him a public that expects the phrases. Nina hears the confession as a romance of vocation. The play lets the audience hear it as a warning she will not take. When he later uses her, and uses the bird, he is doing exactly what he said he does. The honesty of the self-portrait does not save the people he portraits. Chekhov's argument here is dry. A precise description of one's own harmfulness is not the same thing as stopping, and an audience that swoons at the precision has joined the harm. Arkadina, meanwhile, extracts in a different currency. She needs to be loved as an actress more than she can afford to be a mother, and her reconciliation with Trigorin, when he is about to follow Nina, is a performance of flattery that works. She knows her audience of one. The play does not ask us to prefer the young experimenters to these professionals. It asks us to see that both the new forms and the old technique are being used, by someone, as a way of not looking at the person in front of them.

The minor loves are not padding around this theme. They are the same theme in a lower key, and they are a demonstration of economy at the level of a whole company. Masha's black dress is emphasised, and it pays off as a life. She will not get Konstantin. She marries Medvedenko and continues to suffer in a way the household treats as a familiar nuisance. Medvedenko's talk of money is emphasised, and it pays off as the reason his love is never heard as love. Dorn, who sees more than he intervenes, is the one who both covers the final shot and refuses to pretend he did not hear it. Each of these people is a promise kept in the currency they were introduced with. Nobody becomes a different kind of character in order to supply a climax. The climax is what these kinds of character do when the promises come due on the same evening.

The shot in the next room

The last act is the play's verdict on attention. Konstantin has had some literary success and feels none of it as life. Nina arrives, speaks in fragments of the old symbolic play and in fragments of what she has survived, and leaves. He destroys his manuscripts. The shot follows. In the drawing room the lotto game goes on, which is Chekhov's cruellest piece of timing, and Dorn's cover story gives the company a few more minutes of not knowing. The audience knows. The gap between the room and the audience is the point of the ending. A household that has spent four acts failing to hear Konstantin cannot be allowed, by a neat confession scene, to hear him at last. The gun fulfils its promise offstage, and the onstage action fulfils a different promise, the promise that these people will go on being themselves. Economy here includes the refusal of a final speech that would explain the death in the vocabulary of the people who caused it.

This is why The Seagull is a bad example if the example is only "the rifle goes off", and a very good example if the example is the full principle. Emphasised things return. The experimental play returns as quotation in a ruined life. The seagull returns as a story, a pet name, and a meaning Nina tries to outgrow. The bandage returns as a suicide the bandage had announced. None of these returns is a surprise of the puzzle kind. They are the surprise of seeing that the emphasis was a debt, and that Chekhov pays his debts in character rather than in machinery. He called his plays comedies often enough that the word has to be taken seriously. The comedy is the vanity, the boredom, the lotto, the way a tragic fact is muffled by manners. The gun principle does not require the muffling to be stripped away. It requires that the muffling, too, was set up and is doing its work.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading says that an emphasised detail is a promise, and that Chekhov's own plays keep such promises, sometimes literally. This is the play. A gun appears in a failed suicide and is fired, for good, at the end, while a doctor pretends the sound was something else. A seagull is thrust at a girl, stolen as a plot by a novelist, and lived through by the girl until the symbol no longer covers her. A bad experimental play is laughed off the stage and then recited, years later, as the only language a broken actress still trusts. The letters and conversations state the standard of economy in shifting words. The Seagull shows the standard at work in a drama that is not a thriller and not a sermon. It also shows the limit of the rifle anecdote. The richest payoffs are arguments about what the emphasised thing means, conducted by people who need it to mean something that will excuse them. That is still economy. Nothing the play taught you to look at is left unused, and nothing unused was taught to you as if it mattered.
