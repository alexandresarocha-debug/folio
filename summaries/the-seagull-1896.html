In The Seagull, Konstantin Treplev shoots a gull and lays it at Nina's feet. She is horrified and does not know what the gift is supposed to mean. He goes off when he sees the successful writer Boris Trigorin coming. Trigorin looks at the dead bird and says he could use it. A girl lives all her life by a lake, happy and free, like a gull. A man happens by, sees her, and destroys her out of boredom, the way this gull was destroyed. Nina wants that man's life more than she wants Konstantin's. She runs away to act, and to be with Trigorin. By the time she comes back, two years later, the affair is over, the child she had with Trigorin has died, and she is touring the provinces in bad plays, third class, with the educated merchants of the next town waiting to bother her.

Konstantin, who has had some stories published and still cannot get free of her, begs her to stay. She looks at him and says, in Constance Garnett's English, "I am a seagull. No, that's not it. I am an actress." She says the seagull line, takes it back, and says the plain thing. Then she leaves. He tears up his manuscripts. Offstage, a shot. The others are playing lotto and at first have a domestic explanation ready. The doctor, Dorn, takes Trigorin aside and tells him to get Konstantin's mother out of the room. Konstantin has shot himself. A shot killed the bird. Another, offstage, kills the son. The metaphor is the part that fails. Nina will not stay inside Trigorin's story about a girl destroyed for no reason. She has a job in the morning, in Yelets.

The play's own first night looked, for a while, like the destroyed bird. On 17 October 1896, at the Alexandrinsky Theatre in St. Petersburg, the audience jeered. The actress playing Nina, Vera Komissarzhevskaya, was rattled badly enough to lose her voice. Chekhov, who had spent the last two acts backstage, wrote the next day to his friend Suvorin that he was finished with writing plays. He was wrong about the play, if not about that room. The Moscow Art Theatre opened its production on 17 December 1898, with Stanislavski as Trigorin, and took the seagull as the theatre's emblem. What the text already knew, and the first audience missed, is that the famous symbol is something the characters misuse. Trigorin turns a dead bird into a plot. Nina tries the plot on, and then refuses it. The shot at the end is not a symbol. It is a shot.
