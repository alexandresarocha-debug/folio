Background

By 2017 the power-posing claim had already been through a large replication. Ranehill and colleagues had found no effect of the poses on hormones or on risk-taking, and a slight effect on feeling powerful. Dana Carney, the original lead author, had said she no longer believed the effects were real. Joseph Simmons and Uri Simonsohn took a different route into the same controversy. Their 2017 paper, Power Posing: P-Curving the Evidence, does not run a new posing study. It asks what the published statistical results, taken as a collection, look like. Folio's card pairs this paper with the 2018 reply by Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse, as the two sides of an argument about whether the literature contains evidence or only the appearance of it.

A p-curve is a tool for that argument. It is not a vote among experts, and it is not a comment on anyone's character. It is a picture of the p-values a literature chose to call significant, and a theory about what that picture should look like if the effects are real.

The question

Does the set of significant results in the power-posing literature have the shape of a set of results built on a true effect, or the shape of a set selected because they crossed a threshold? Simmons and Simonsohn apply the question to the behavioural and hormonal claims that had made the original paper famous, rather than to every study in which someone stood differently and filled in a questionnaire. The distinction will matter when the reply arrives. A curve that speaks about one body of results cannot be refuted by changing the body. It can be refuted by showing that the curve was drawn on the wrong results, or drawn wrongly. The question of this paper is narrower than the question of whether any postural intervention ever changes a feeling. It is whether the published evidence for the strong power-posing effects carries evidential value.

How a p-curve works

A result that is merely significant has passed a line. The line is silent about whether the result sits just over it or far beyond it. If a literature is estimating a real effect, with reasonable samples and honest analysis, its significant p-values should crowd towards the small end. Most of the significant findings should be findings the data supported with room to spare. A trickle should sit just under the line, because even a true effect sometimes produces a weak sample. If, instead, a literature has been selecting from noise, keeping the analyses that happened to cross the line and discarding or reshaping the ones that did not, the significant p-values should pile up against the line. Many results just significant. Few results strongly significant. The p-curve is that pile, drawn as a distribution.

The method then compares the observed pile with the pile a true effect would be expected to produce. Evidential value, in this jargon, is a property of the distribution. A right shape supports the claim that the literature as a whole is not a selection of flukes. A flat or wrongly skewed shape supports the claim that selective reporting could have produced the papers one is looking at, even if nothing in the poses had any effect on the outcomes. Simmons and Simonsohn's contribution is to make that comparison for power posing, and to say which studies and which outcomes went into the curve. A p-curve is only as meaningful as those choices. Including every distant cousin of a hypothesis, or excluding the awkward papers, will draw a different picture. The discipline of the method is to declare the set.

What they concluded

Their conclusion is the one the card summarises. For the behavioural and hormonal claims associated with power posing, the published record did not show evidential value. The distribution of significant results did not have the shape expected of a literature that is repeatedly measuring a real effect. It had the shape expected when results just strong enough to publish are the results that get published. In combination with Ranehill's direct replication, the curve says something a single failed study cannot say alone. It is not merely that one second laboratory failed to find the hormones and the gamble. It is that the family of published successes, inspected as a family, does not look like a family of true detections.

The paper is correspondingly unimpressed by the original bundle. A small study that reports four coordinated effects is, on this view, exactly the kind of report a p-curve is built to put in context. The context is a literature whose significant p-values do not concentrate where a robust effect would push them. Simmons and Simonsohn do not need to reanalyse every author's spreadsheet to make that point. The p-values as published are the material. If those p-values were the visible part of a strong effect, they would look different. They do not look different. That is the finding.

How to read the method's restraint

A p-curve does not identify which papers are wrong, and it does not measure the true effect size. A literature can lack evidential value in this technical sense and still contain, somewhere inside it, a real but small phenomenon that the published record has mishandled. Simmons and Simonsohn are careful enough to aim their conclusion at the claims whose p-values they curved, the claims about behaviour and hormones that constituted the power-posing effect as advertised. They are not, in this paper, issuing a universal verdict on every self-report of feeling powerful after a change of posture. The card keeps that distinction, and the 2018 reply depends on it. One can lose the argument about the strong effect and continue an argument about a weaker one. Treating the p-curve as if it had personally settled the weaker argument is a misreading. Treating the weaker argument as if it had answered the p-curve is the mirror misreading.

The method is also a product of the same crisis that produced the multi-lab replications. It exists because journals selected significant results, because analysts had choices, and because a list of successful papers is therefore a biased sample of the analyses that were run. Power posing is a case. The tool is general. A reader who understands the curve on this case understands why a literature review that counts positive findings is not yet a review of the evidence. The count is what selection produces. The curve is an attempt to see the selection.

Limits

The limit the reply would press is the limit of the set. Decide that the relevant literature is a particular group of power-posing experiments aimed at hormones, risk and related behaviour, and the curve looks as Simmons and Simonsohn say. Decide that the relevant literature is a wider tradition of postural feedback, including many studies of subjective feeling, and a different curve can be drawn, which is what Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse would do the following year. Both decisions need a justification that is not the curve one prefers. This paper's justification is that the claim under challenge was the claim that made power posing a phenomenon, not the claim that sitting up straight can change a mood item. A reader can reject the justification. The rejection should be about the set, stated openly, rather than about the motives of the people who drew the curve.

There is a further limit inside the tool. P-curve reads the published significant results. It cannot see the studies that were never written up, and its power to diagnose selection depends on how p-values were computed and reported. Misreported statistics, of which the crisis found many in many fields, can distort the picture. Simmons and Simonsohn's conclusion is only as good as the published numbers they inherited. Given those numbers, the shape is unkind to the strong claim. A different archive might be kinder. The archive they had is the archive the field had offered.

What the curve does not decide

It does not decide how critics should speak to the scientists whose work they curve. The card notes that the power-posing episode became a story about the culture of criticism, about blogs, social media, and the difference between attacking a result and attacking a person. That story is real and it is not a p-value. A method can be fair and a surrounding conversation can still be cruel, or a conversation can be polite and a method can still be a muddle. The paper itself is a method. Its tone is the tone of a statistical critique. Readers who want the human episode will not find it in the shape of a distribution, and should not pretend the distribution is a substitute for decency. They should also not pretend that decency requires the distribution to be ignored.

Nor does the curve decide the residual subjective effect. Feeling slightly more powerful, the piece Ranehill's sample still showed, can be true and small while the hormonal literature lacks evidential value. Those are compatible. The mistake is to let the possible small feeling count as rehabilitation of the original bundle. Simmons and Simonsohn's paper is the argument that the bundle's published support does not have the internal structure of real evidence. The rehabilitation, if it comes, has to be for a claim they were not primarily curving.

Why it matters here

The power-posing card is a pair of episodes, a failed replication and a fight about the literature, and this paper is the fight's statistical half. Ranehill and colleagues showed that one large sample did not reproduce the hormones or the risk effect. Simmons and Simonsohn ask whether the published successes, lined up as p-values, look like successes that a real effect would generate. Their answer is that the behavioural and hormonal literature lacks evidential value. Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse answer by drawing the curve around a wider set of postural studies and defending an effect on feelings of power. Read the two papers together, because each is the other's scope condition. Read this one for the tool, and for the conclusion that the strong claim's published record does not survive a look at its own statistics.
