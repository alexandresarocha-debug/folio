Joseph Simmons and Uri Simonsohn asked whether 33 published studies were evidence that power posing works, or a stack of results that existed in print because they had come out significant. Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy, and Andy Yap had answered a failed replication by pointing to those 33 studies of expansive versus contractive posture. A p-curve is the histogram of the significant p-values in a literature. When studies are chasing a real effect, most of those p-values should be very small. When the papers exist because only the analyses that crossed .05 were kept, the histogram is flat, with as many results between .04 and .05 as between .00 and .01.

Two of the 33 studies measured only feelings of power, which Simmons and Simonsohn set aside as a check that people had noticed the pose. Two more did not report the critical statistic. Seven results were not significant and so could not enter the curve. That left 24 significant p-values, 10 of them below .025. A quarter of those significant values sat between .04 and .05. The curve was not right-skewed: the test gave p = .63 on the full set and p = .11 on the half below .025. It was flatter than the curve expected if the studies had even a 33 percent chance of detecting a real effect, p = .0016. The estimated average power of the set was 5 percent, and a check that swapped in 12 alternative statistics still came out at 5 percent, with a confidence interval that excluded anything above 17 percent.

Eva Ranehill and colleagues had already found, in 200 people, no hormone effect and no risk effect, only a change in feeling powerful. The p-curve says the older significant papers do not rescue the claims about behavior and physiology. A significant result is cheap if the literature keeps the significant results and files the rest. Before changing a habit because studies show that a posture improves performance, look at where the p-values sit. In this set they were not piled on the small ones. Simmons and Simonsohn's conclusion was that the existing evidence was too weak to justify telling people to power-pose their way to a better life, and too weak to justify a hunt for moderators of an effect the curve could not find.
