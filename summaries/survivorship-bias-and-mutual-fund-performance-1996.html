Investment funds that do badly tend to disappear. Usually they are merged into another fund in the same family, and the poor record vanishes from the usual databases. Edwin Elton and Martin Gruber of New York University and Christopher Blake of Fordham University set out to measure how much that flatters the funds that remain.

They took every mutual fund that existed at the end of 1976 and followed each one forward, including those that later merged or closed. When a fund merged, they tracked what happened to an investor's money in the fund it merged into, an approach they called follow the money. That gave a sample free of survivorship bias, which they could compare with the survivors alone.

Studying only survivors overstated performance, and the overstatement grew with the length of the study. In one version of their analysis, the bias was about 0.4% a year in a 10-year study and nearly 1% a year in a 20-year study. Over many years, a difference of that size adds up.

The bias also distorted comparisons between kinds of funds. Using survivors only, the smallest tenth of funds by size looked about as good as the largest. With the dead funds included, small funds did much worse, consistent with small funds being more likely to fail and vanish from the record.

What this means in real life: a list of funds, start-ups or strategies with great long-term records is a list of survivors. The ones that failed are usually not on it.

Caution: the size of the bias depends on the time period, the database and the model used to measure performance, and the figures here come from one table of a 1996 study. Later databases include dead funds, which reduces the problem.

Source. Elton, E. J., Gruber, M. J., and Blake, C. R. (1996). Survivorship bias and mutual fund performance. Review of Financial Studies, 9(4), 1097-1120. doi:10.1093/rfs/9.4.1097. https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/9.4.1097
