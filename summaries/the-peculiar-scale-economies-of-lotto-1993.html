Lotto, a game of long odds and large jackpots, was the best-selling lottery game in the United States. Philip Cook and Charles Clotfelter noticed something odd about it: per-person sales tended to rise with the size of the state's population. As a result, smaller states found it worthwhile to join together in multi-state games.

The background figures come from 1989. Lotto was offered by 29 of the 33 state lotteries and made up 40% of lottery sales, an average of $47 per person out of total lottery sales of $115 per person in those states. In Maryland's game, players chose six numbers from 1 to 49, giving 13,983,816 possible combinations. The chance of a jackpot on a single dollar bet ranged from about 1 in 574,000 in Delaware to 1 in 23 million in California. Half of all money bet went into the prize pool.

Bigger states could offer bigger jackpots, but that alone does not explain the pattern, because the chance of winning tends to fall in proportion to the state's population. A rational player who cared only about expected value would not prefer the big-state game.

Their explanation is psychological. The jackpot is widely advertised, as is the outcome of each drawing. If players judge the likelihood of winning by how often someone wins, a larger state can offer a game with longer odds but the same perceived probability as a smaller state. The larger population base hides the smaller chance of winning, while the larger jackpot is highly visible. They note that this fits prospect theory.

What this means in real life: the size of the headline prize, and stories of winners, tell you almost nothing about your own chances.

Caution: the evidence is aggregate sales across states and over time, not interviews with players, so the psychological explanation is inferred. The figures here come from the 1991 working paper, read as a scanned copy.

Source. Cook, P. J., and Clotfelter, C. T. (1993). The peculiar scale economies of lotto. American Economic Review, 83(3), 634-643. Working paper version: NBER Working Paper 3766 (July 1991), https://www.nber.org/papers/w3766
