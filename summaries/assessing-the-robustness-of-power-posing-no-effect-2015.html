Background

Five years after Carney, Cuddy and Yap reported that a brief expansive pose could change hormones, risk-taking and feelings of power, Eva Ranehill and colleagues asked whether the result would appear again in a larger sample. Their 2015 paper in Psychological Science states the answer in the title. Assessing the Robustness of Power Posing: No Effect on Hormones and Risk Tolerance in a Large Sample of Men and Women. The sample was around 200 people, several times the 42 of the original study. Feelings of power moved a little. Testosterone, cortisol and the risky choice did not. Folio's card treats this paper as the direct empirical reply, the one a reader should put beside the original before moving on to arguments about p-curves and tone.

Robustness is a precise word. The question is not whether some posture, held for an hour, in a person who already has status, correlates with something. The question is whether the published procedure, the short pose and the bundle of outcomes, produces the published effects when someone else runs it with more participants and less freedom to adjust.

The question

Do high-power and low-power poses, held for the brief period the original study used, change testosterone, cortisol, risk-taking and felt power when the comparison is run at a scale that could confirm a real effect or expose a fragile one? Ranehill and her co-authors were testing a bundle, and the bundle can fail in parts. A replication that found every piece would have made the 2010 claim much harder to set aside. A replication that found none of the pieces would have been a clean null. What they actually faced, and what the card records, is a split. The physiological and behavioural claims, which were the reason the finding was more than a suggestion to stand up straight, were the claims that failed. The self-report, which is the easiest piece to move and the hardest piece to build a mechanism on, was the piece that showed a small shift.

How the replication was set up

The study followed the logic of the original contrast. Participants held expansive, high-power postures or contracted, low-power postures for a short time. Men and women both took part, which the title flags because a hormonal claim that had been discussed as if it were general needed a sample that was not silent about sex. Saliva was collected so that testosterone and cortisol could be measured, as in the original. A behavioural measure of risk tolerance asked participants to make a choice in which a safer and a riskier option were both available. Feelings of power were reported directly. The design is a replication in the useful sense. It tries to give the reported effect a fair chance to recur, at a size of sample where recurrence would be meaningful and absence would be informative.

Around 200 participants does not make a study infallible. It changes the arithmetic of luck. An effect large enough to matter in a two-minute intervention, and large enough to have been detected in a sample of 42, ought to be more visible, not less, when the sample grows. A result that shrinks to nothing as the sample improves is the pattern of a finding that was carried by noise, by flexibility, or by both. Ranehill and colleagues arranged the study so that this pattern, if it was the truth, would have room to show itself separately for hormones, for risk and for feeling.

What they found

They found no effect of pose on testosterone or on cortisol. The hormonal half of the original bundle, the half that had made the result look like biology rather than like a mood, did not appear. They found no effect on risk tolerance. Participants who had held expansive poses were not meaningfully more willing to take the gamble than participants who had held contracted ones. The behavioural claim, which was the bridge from a feeling to a decision, failed with the hormones. What remained was subjective. People reported feeling slightly more powerful after the expansive poses. The slightness is the card's word, and it is the right word. A small shift in a self-report is not nothing. It is also not the phenomenon the TED talk had been describing.

The split is the paper's real product. A reader who wanted power posing to be either wholly true or wholly imaginary is not given that comfort. The strong theory, posture to hormone to bold choice, is what the large sample refused to repeat. A weak theory, posture to a modest change in how powerful one feels, is what the large sample left standing, without explaining it. Feeling powerful because one has just been arranged into a powerful shape may be a genuine flicker of embodiment. It may be the participant answering a question whose answer the pose has advertised. The 2015 data do not settle which. They settle that the flicker, if it is real, does not come with the chemistry or the gamble.

How the authors read the result

Ranehill and colleagues read the study as a failure of robustness for the claims that had made power posing important. A finding that depends on hormones and on risk is a finding about the body and about choice. A finding that shrinks to a small change in agreement with the statement that one feels powerful is a finding about a questionnaire after a suggestive exercise. They do not deny the small subjective difference. They refuse to let it inherit the prestige of the effects that did not replicate. The title is organised accordingly. No effect on hormones and risk tolerance is the news. The sample size and the inclusion of men and women are the reasons the news is credible. The feeling of power is acknowledged in the paper and, rightly, does not make it into the title's claim.

The reading has a methodological moral that the card's replication path keeps drawing. Direct replication is how a vivid result earns, or loses, the right to travel. Commentary, however sharp, is not a substitute for running the procedure again with more people. This paper is that running. Later arguments about p-curves and about the tone of the debate are arguments about a literature. They sit on top of a fact this study established in its own sample: the hormonal and risk effects were not there.

Limits

A single replication, even a large one, is one protocol. A pose held a little differently, a risk measure with a different stake, a sample under a different kind of stress, could in principle diverge. The limit is real and it is easy to abuse. If every failed replication can be set aside by inventing a variant that was not tested, no result is ever robust and no failure ever counts. Ranehill and colleagues tested the claim as it had been sold, a short pose and a bundle of consequences, in a sample large enough to see those consequences. Variants that abandon the hormones and the gamble are not replications of the 2010 paper. They are different studies, aimed at the residual subjective effect, and they have to be reported as different.

The subjective residue has its own limit. Feeling slightly more powerful is a self-report taken immediately, in the laboratory that asked for the pose. It does not show that the feeling survives a walk down the corridor, or that it changes behaviour the moment the behaviour is not a tick-box. The original theory needed the feeling to be the conscious tip of a hormonal change that would alter a decision. With the tip present and the change absent, the tip is a small thing, and this paper is disciplined about keeping it small.

What the result did to the debate

After 2015 it became difficult to cite the original bundle as a finding and stop. The next year Carney, the original lead author, said publicly that she did not believe the power-pose effects were real and described analytic choices in the first study that had made the results look stronger. That statement is not a result of Ranehill's experiment, but it is part of the same turn. A co-author stepping away, and a large replication finding no hormonal or risk effect, left the defence of power posing with a narrower brief. Cuddy and colleagues would take that brief: not the chemistry, not necessarily the gamble, but a real effect on subjective feelings of power, visible if one looks across a wider set of postural studies. Simmons and Simonsohn would argue that even the published literature, examined as a distribution of p-values, does not support the strong effects.

Ranehill's paper does not need those later moves in order to be clear. It is the empirical centre of the card's caution. Around 200 participants, no effect on hormones, no effect on risk tolerance, a slight increase in feeling powerful. Anyone who wants to recommend the pose as a biological intervention is arguing with this sample. Anyone who wants to recommend it as a way of feeling a bit more powerful for a moment is making a different, smaller recommendation, and should say so.

Why it matters here

The power-posing card is a lesson in what a replication is for. Carney, Cuddy and Yap reported a bundle in a sample of 42. Ranehill and colleagues ran the comparison at several times that size and watched the bundle come apart. The hormones and the risky choice, which justified the excitement, were not robust. The feeling of power, which could have been predicted from the mere look of the poses, was faintly present. Read this paper as the factual pivot of the card. The p-curve debate and the 2018 defence are arguments about how to weigh a literature. This study is a literature's most important single second look, and the second look did not find the effect that had travelled.
