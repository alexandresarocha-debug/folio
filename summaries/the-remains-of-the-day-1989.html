A journey told in the language of a profession

Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, published in 1989, is a novel about a butler who takes a short motoring holiday and, in the course of it, fails and then almost succeeds in telling the truth about his life. Stevens has spent his career at Darlington Hall, in the service of Lord Darlington, and is now kept on by an American owner, Mr Farraday, who has bought the house after the war. The holiday, in the summer of 1956, is officially a trip to the West Country, with the practical idea that Miss Kenton, the former housekeeper, now Mrs Benn, might be persuaded to return and help with a staff problem. Stevens narrates the trip himself, in a prose of extreme formality, and the narration constantly leaves the road in order to justify the past. The justification is the story. By the time he meets Mrs Benn, the reader has assembled a life that Stevens's vocabulary is designed not to name: a father unattended in his death, a love never admitted, and a loyalty given to a man whose political projects included the appeasement of Nazis and the dismissal of Jewish maids.

The novel is the modern demonstration of an unreliable narrator who is not a liar in the ordinary sense. Stevens does not invent a false chronology for the pleasure of deceiving us. He omits, he renames, and he judges. Wayne Booth's distinction fits him with uncomfortable precision. He often reports what happened, and he speaks against the norms the book as a whole makes visible. Dignity, in his definition, is the capacity never to abandon one's professional role, never to be seen off duty, never to let a private feeling disturb the running of a house. The book lets that definition stand for many pages, long enough for its prestige to be felt, and then surrounds it with occasions on which the definition costs a human being. The unreliability is the gap between the occasions and the moral he draws from them. A reader who stayed inside the moral would think they were reading the memoirs of a great professional. A reader who watches the occasions sees a man practising a code that has emptied him.

What the code does to a death and to a love

The death of Stevens's father is the code's exhibition piece. During a conference of Lord Darlington's political guests, the father, himself a butler and now old and failing, collapses. Stevens continues to serve. He climbs the stairs, he comes down again, he attends to silver and to guests, and he presents the episode afterwards as the moment at which he proved himself equal to the dignity of his calling. The facts are in the narration. The father dies in the house while the son is busy with the conference. What is unreliable is the evaluation. The book has given us the means to hear the proof of dignity as a description of absence, and it has done so without a second narrator to correct him. The correction is the reader's, made from Stevens's own details once the glow of his conclusion has been set aside. Later theory would call this a misregarding, a moral misreading of facts that are not exactly falsified, together with an underreporting of feeling. The terms fit. He does not need to say something factually false in order to be, in Booth's sense, out of accord with the norms of the work.

Miss Kenton is the same structure drawn out over years. She and Stevens conduct what is, in her words and in her anger, a relationship. In his words it is a question of staffing, of errors in the running of the house, of a housekeeper who is admirably professional and occasionally oversteps. She teases him, challenges him, and at moments offers a life in which he would have to step out of the role. He answers in the role. When, on the holiday, he finds her and learns that she will not come back, that she has a marriage and a future elsewhere, the narration comes as close as it ever comes to a direct statement of loss. Even then the statement is brief, and it is followed by a recovery into banter and into the evening light, as if a feeling, once admitted, had to be returned to the custody of tone. The love story is told entirely as a professional relationship that happens to contain every sign of love except the name. That is what free indirect style and first-person euphemism can share. The narrator's chosen language is the censorship, and the reader is asked to read the censored feeling in the places where the language works too hard.

Loyalty, and the politics of not noticing

Lord Darlington's story is the public version of the same censorship. Stevens insists that his lordship was a gentleman of sincere intentions, that a butler's honour consists in serving a distinguished man, and that the politics of the house were not the butler's to judge. The narration then supplies, in the order of Stevens's defensive memories, the meetings, the anti-Jewish staffing decision, the use of the house as a place where appeasement could wear the face of hospitality. Miss Kenton objects to the sacking of the maids. Stevens treats the objection as a lapse of professional discipline. The reader, again, has the facts from him and the judgement against him. His unreliability on this point is not ignorance of what occurred. It is a theory of his own life in which obedience was a virtue because the alternative would have been to possess a moral view. The novel's historical argument is carried by that theory. Catastrophes can be staffed by people whose self-respect depends on not being the sort of person who looks up from the silver.

Ishiguro's formal decision is to give this theory a beautiful voice. Stevens's English is measured, Latinate, addicted to understatement and to the pronoun one, and it is capable of real observation about the craft of service. If the voice were merely pompous, the reader would feel superior from the first page, and the book would be a satire of a servant. The voice is seductive. It offers a standard of care, of attention, of not imposing oneself, that a reader may half share. The seduction is the rhetoric Booth described, a design on our agreement, followed by a design on our withdrawal of agreement. We are brought close enough to feel the dignity as dignity, and then moved just far enough back to see what it excluded. The motoring trip is the device that makes the movement possible. A man who will not reflect has been given a landscape and a destination that require him to narrate, and narration, even his kind, cannot help accumulating the evidence his conclusions refuse.

The evening, and the remains

The close of the novel does not grant Stevens a conversion of the kind a less exact book would have wanted. He sits by the sea, he speaks to a stranger, and he considers the remains of his day, the idea that there might still be something to look forward to in the evenings of a diminished life. He also decides, with a return of his old method, that he ought to learn to banter, because Mr Farraday seems to expect banter from an English butler and Stevens has never been good at it. The decision is heartbreaking because it is so characteristic. Even the lesson of the journey is translated, within a page, back into a professional exercise. The book does not mock the translation. It lets it stand as the form in which a man of his training can approach a change, which is to say, as a further refinement of the role. Unreliability, at the end, is not cleared up by a confession that rewrites the previous pages. It is softened by a moment of clarity and then resumed, because a self that has been built out of a vocabulary cannot step out of the vocabulary just by suffering.

This is the novel's instruction in how to read a whole class of narrators. Look for the facts that the voice reports on the way to a conclusion, and test the conclusion against the facts rather than against your affection for the voice. Look for omissions that have a shape, the subjects that are always approached and never named. Look for the moments when the prose becomes more formal as the feeling becomes larger, which is often the sign that formality is doing the work of concealment. Stevens is reliable about the silver, about the routes of the journey, about the sequence of employers. He is unreliable about the meaning of service, the nature of his feeling for Miss Kenton, and the moral character of the man he served. The split is the book. An unreliable narrator, here, is not a puzzle with a solution in the last chapter. It is a life conducted in a language that cannot afford the words the life requires.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading is the unreliable narrator, a voice the reader comes to distrust, defined by Booth as a narrator out of accord with the norms of the work. Stevens is the case of unreliability as self-deception rather than as a lie. He tells a motoring holiday and, inside it, a career, in a language of dignity and professional restraint. The language reports a father's death during a conference, a house used for appeasement, the dismissal of Jewish servants, and a lifelong nearness to Miss Kenton, and it draws from these the moral that a great butler does not desert his post. The novel's norms, visible in the arrangement and in what the prose cannot quite suppress, do not endorse that moral. The voice can be sincere, careful, even admirable in its own terms, and still be the wrong guide to the story it tells.
