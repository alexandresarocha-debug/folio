In the summer of 1956, Stevens, the butler of Darlington Hall, takes his new American employer's Ford and drives west to see the former housekeeper, Miss Kenton, now Mrs. Benn. The trip is the frame. The story he tells himself along the road is about dignity. "The great butlers are great," he says, "by virtue of their ability to inhabit their professional role and inhabit it to the utmost." Lesser butlers drop the role when something private jostles them. The test he is proudest of came on a night in 1923, during an unofficial conference at the house, when his father died upstairs. Stevens stayed at his post, serving the guests. He treats the night as proof that he was not shaken out of the work. Read against the evening he is describing, it is also a night when a son will not go up to his father.

The same standard gets him through a worse order. Lord Darlington tells him to dismiss two Jewish housemaids. He dismisses them. Miss Kenton is furious, and Stevens spends the later years of the story insisting that the episode was unfortunate, that his own feelings were beside the point, and that Darlington was a gentleman whose name has been unfairly dirtied. The facts he himself includes keep pulling the other way. Darlington's political friendships, the dismissed maids, the conference, the woman who left the house: Stevens arranges all of it as the price of professional calm. When he finally sits with Miss Kenton, the feeling the book has been withholding is in the scene, and his reply to his own life is to plan a smaller competence. He will practice banter, so that he can offer his American employer the light talk the employer seems to want.

The novel is a picture of a narrator who is not lying in the way a culprit lies. He is applying a rule that makes his own losses look like victories, and the rule almost holds. You believe in the dignity for as long as you look where he points, at the unbroken service, the unspilled tray, the evening that went off well. The book puts the other evidence in his mouth anyway. That is why the drive matters more than any single confession. He has six days on the road and a full memory, and he still arrives at a pier talking about learning to joke. Anyone who has explained a bad choice as professionalism, in a hospital, an office, or a family, knows the voice. The correction is not a narrator from outside. It is the detail he cannot quite keep out of the explanation.
