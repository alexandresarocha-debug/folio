Twin and adoption studies say about half of the differences in alcoholism are linked to genes. They do not say which genes. To find them, researchers scan the DNA of huge numbers of people and look for spots where people with the disorder differ, even slightly, from people without it.

Hang Zhou, Joel Gelernter and a large team combined four data sets: two from the US Million Veteran Program, one from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium and one from the UK Biobank. In total there were 435,563 people, all of European ancestry. The trait was problematic alcohol use, which joins a diagnosis of alcohol use disorder with a questionnaire about drinking problems.

They found 29 independent genetic variants linked to problematic drinking, 19 of them new. Some sit in genes that break down alcohol, such as ADH1B. Others sit in genes active in the brain, such as DRD2, which makes a receptor for the chemical messenger dopamine. Each variant shifts risk only a little.

The key number is the share of differences that all the common variants together can capture, called SNP heritability. For problematic alcohol use it was 0.068. For a diagnosis of alcohol use disorder in the veteran data it was 0.095. That is far below the 0.49 from twin and adoption studies. The gap is a clear case of what geneticists call missing heritability.

The genetic signal overlapped with many other traits. Problematic drinking was genetically correlated with 138 other traits, including drinks per week, smoking, cannabis use and depression, which had the strongest link among psychiatric disorders. In a separate hospital sample of 67,589 people, a genetic score built from the results was linked to those same conditions.

What this means in real life: there is no single alcoholism gene to test for. A DNA score today tells a person very little about their own risk.

Caution: the study used only people of European ancestry, so the results may not carry over to other groups. ALDH2, a strong protective gene in East Asia, had shown up in earlier studies only in some Asian samples, so a European-only study could not capture it. Why DNA studies capture so much less than twin studies is still debated: rare variants, inflated twin estimates, or both.
