B. F. Skinner, then at Indiana University, argued that a reward does not have to be caused by a behaviour to strengthen it. It only has to follow it. This short paper describes a simple demonstration.

A pigeon was brought to a steady state of hunger by reducing it to 75% of its well-fed weight and put in an experimental cage for a few minutes each day. A clock swung a food hopper into place at regular intervals, holding it there for five seconds, with no reference whatsoever to what the bird was doing.

In six out of eight cases, the birds developed responses so clearly defined that two observers could agree perfectly in counting them. One bird turned counter-clockwise about the cage, making two or three turns between feedings. Another repeatedly thrust its head into one of the upper corners. A third developed a tossing movement, as if lifting an invisible bar with its head. Two developed a pendulum motion of the head and body. The responses were repeated rapidly, typically five or six times in 15 seconds.

Skinner found that 15 seconds between feedings worked well and one minute much less so, although an established habit kept going when the interval was stretched to two minutes. He also showed that a hopping habit, once extinguished by stopping the food, did not come back when free food resumed. The bird picked up a different habit instead.

He concluded that the bird behaves as if there were a causal relation between its behaviour and the food, although no such relation exists. He saw human parallels: rituals for changing one's luck at cards, and the bowler who keeps twisting his arm and shoulder after releasing the ball, as if he could still steer it.

What this means in real life: lucky socks and pre-game rituals can arise from a few chance successes and then be kept alive by the occasional win.

Caution: Skinner reports his observations briefly, with few numbers and no systematic recording of every behaviour. Later, more careful replications questioned how much of what he saw was learned by accident.
