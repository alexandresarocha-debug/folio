If the shared family home mattered a lot, it should show up across thousands of twin studies. Tinca Polderman, Danielle Posthuma and colleagues at VU University Amsterdam, working with Peter Visscher in Brisbane, set out to pool virtually every twin study ever published.

They found 2,748 studies published between 1958 and 2012, covering 17,804 traits, from height and eyesight to depression and personality. Together they reported on 14,558,903 partly overlapping twin pairs, or about 2.2 million after correcting for studies that reported on several traits. Twins came from 39 countries. 34% of studies used US twins, while Asia, Africa and South America were heavily underrepresented.

Across all traits, identical twins correlated 0.636 on average and non-identical twins 0.339. The studies' own estimates put heritability at 0.488 and the shared environment at 0.174. But worked out directly from the twin correlations, the shared environment came to only 0.042. For 69% of traits the pattern was exactly what genes plus unique experience would produce: identical twins twice as alike as non-identical twins, with no room left for a shared family effect. The authors concluded that the data are inconsistent with substantial influences from shared environment.

Personality fit this pattern well. For temperament and personality, identical twins correlated about 0.47 and non-identical twins about 0.23, almost exactly half, and 84% of studies were consistent with no shared family effect.

There were clear exceptions. The twin pattern pointed to a substantial shared environment for conduct disorders, religion and spirituality, and education. Among broad groups of traits, social values and measures of the environment itself had shared-environment estimates of about 0.27.

What this means in real life: for most traits, what makes siblings alike is mostly shared genes; what makes them different is mostly their own experience.

Caution: twin studies assume identical and non-identical twins share their environments equally. The authors treated overlapping samples as independent, which makes estimates look more precise than they are. Few studies report test-retest data, so measurement error, which counts toward the unique share, is hard to separate out. And Asia, Africa and South America were barely represented.

Source. Polderman, T. J. C., Benyamin, B., de Leeuw, C. A., Sullivan, P. F., van Bochoven, A., Visscher, P. M., and Posthuma, D. (2015). Meta-analysis of the heritability of human traits based on fifty years of twin studies. Nature Genetics, 47(7), 702-709. https://doi.org/10.1038/ng.3285
