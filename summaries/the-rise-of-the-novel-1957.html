A question about form and about readers

Ian Watt's The Rise of the Novel, published in 1957, asks why a particular kind of long prose fiction became dominant in eighteenth-century England. The subtitle names the evidence: studies in Defoe, Richardson, and Fielding. Watt is a literary historian with a sociological appetite. He wants the formal features of the new books and the social conditions that made those features intelligible to their first public. The result is a book in ten movements: a definition of formal realism, a chapter on the reading public, two chapters on Defoe, three on Richardson, two on Fielding, and a short note on what happened to realism afterward.

The opening chapter is the one later arguments have had to accept or revise. Watt thinks the novel's realism is a realism of presentation. Earlier prose fiction had used traditional plots, type-named characters, and a looser sense of when and where an action occurred. The new writing presents particular people, in particular places, moving through time by cause and effect, in a prose that aims to be an authentic report of individual experience. He calls that set of procedures formal realism. It is formal because it is a method, visible in names, clocks, rooms, and motives, whatever moral the story happens to carry. Robinson Crusoe, Pamela, Clarissa, and Tom Jones are his main exhibits. They do not resemble one another in temperament. They resemble one another, he argues, in the kind of world they ask a reader to believe is being reported.

Names, clocks, and the refusal of the old plot

Watt draws a line from this method to philosophical habits associated with Descartes and Locke: the individual mind as the place where knowledge is built from experience, the suspicion of innate ideas and of received stories. He does not claim that Defoe sat down to illustrate Locke. He claims that a culture in which particular experience had become the test of knowledge was a culture ready to enjoy a narrative that lingered on particular experience. Ordinary proper names matter in this account. Moll, Pamela, Tom, and Crusoe are not allegorical labels. A name that could appear in a parish register is already a theoretical decision. So is the calendar. Defoe and Richardson are interested in how long things take, in days on an island, in hours in a house where a letter is being written while the event is still warm.

Place receives the same treatment. The novel, in Watt's description, fills in rooms, streets, clothes, and sums of money because the reality it wants is circumstantial. The circumstantial detail is a claim: this happened to someone, somewhere, and the someone was not a standing type from a romance. Watt is careful enough to admit that the claim can be a brilliant lie. Formal realism is a rhetoric of authenticity. Defoe's greatest effects come from using that rhetoric while inventing freely. The lie works because the culture has a new standard for what a true story sounds like.

Defoe, Richardson, and the private page

The Defoe chapters put Robinson Crusoe first as a fable of economic individualism. Crusoe's labour, his inventory of goods, his habit of weighing time and advantage: Watt reads these as the narrative shape of a society that had begun to think of the person as an economic agent. The chapter on Moll Flanders then asks what Defoe's method looks like when the individual is a woman moving through crime, marriage, and money. Watt's admiration for the illusion of reported life sits beside a doubt about moral coherence. Defoe's narrators are so convincing in the moment that the moments do not always add up to a judged life. For Watt, that unevenness is part of what early formal realism looked like before later novelists learned to organise it.

Richardson receives the warmest and longest attention. Pamela turns courtship and resistance into a sequence of letters written close to the event, so that private feeling and social danger share a page. Clarissa, in Watt's reading, is the great extension of that method: a tragedy of private experience, in which the letter lets a reader inhabit durations of pressure, persuasion, and isolation that epic and drama had handled by other means. He connects the letter and the diary to Protestant self-examination and to a middle-class culture of introspection. He also connects them to an audience. The chapter on the reading public sets out the conditions he thinks made such books saleable: growing literacy, more leisure among women of the middle class, circulating libraries, and a print trade able to supply long narratives to people who had time to live with them. The novel, in this sociology, is a form matched to silent, solitary, repeated reading.

Fielding, and the argument's boundary

Fielding is the counter-example inside the book, and Watt uses him with respect. Tom Jones offers formal realism of another kind: a plotted world of inns, roads, and coincidences, surveyed by a narrator who comments, classifies, and laughs. Fielding understood his work as a comic epic in prose, and Watt takes that classical ambition seriously. The narrator's distance, the willingness to generalise, the architectural plot: these are real achievements, and they are different from Richardson's intimacy. Watt's comparison is one of the book's permanent gifts. Eighteenth-century fiction did not march in a single file towards inwardness. It developed at least two strong answers to the problem of how a long prose story could be serious. One answer was the private document. The other was the sociable narrator who keeps judgement in his own voice.

The closing note looks beyond the three writers towards later realism and records limits Watt already sees. A definition built on Defoe, Richardson, and Fielding leaves a great deal of prose fiction to one side. Later critics have said so at length. Women novelists before and beside Richardson, among them Aphra Behn and Eliza Haywood, were writing the kind of book Watt's public was reading. Prose fiction in other languages and centuries, from ancient Greek narratives to the Japanese court tale, makes any claim about the novel's single rise in England look parochial. Michael McKeon and others have retold the English story with a different sense of conflict inside ideology. Watt's book remains the clearest statement of the question those revisions answer. What changed in the telling, and what changed in the life around the telling, so that these long reports of individual experience found a public?

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the rise of the novel is a reading of this book, and the card is frank about the revisions. Formal realism, the particular person in a particular time and place, the link to individualism and to a growing body of readers: those are Watt's claims, stated so that a reader of Crusoe or Pamela can test them on the page. The card sends you to Robinson Crusoe because Watt's third chapter is the place where the castaway becomes an exhibit of economic individualism, and the exhibit is still worth arguing with while you read Defoe's lists and labours. The Tale of Genji sits on the same card as the standing reminder that long prose about inner life was possible in another court, many centuries earlier. Watt asked a local question with unusual clarity. The clarity is what makes the limits visible.
