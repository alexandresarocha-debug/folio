Ian Watt's exhibit for what he calls formal realism is a name. Earlier fiction, he argues, preferred type-names and one-word literary names: Mr. Badman, Euphues. Daniel Defoe broke that habit with Robinson Crusoe and Moll Flanders, full ordinary names for particular people. Samuel Richardson went further and gave his major characters both a given name and a surname. Pamela is a romance name, and Andrews is a commonplace family name that pins her to an actual social world. The novel, on Watt's account, proceeds as if it owed the reader the individuality of the actors and the particulars of time and place.

He shows the same habit in the clock. Richardson dates the letters, often to the hour, and Watt cites the report that Clarissa died at 6:40 p.m. on Thursday, 7 September. Henry Fielding, who still liked names such as Allworthy and Square, had largely given the habit up by the time of Amelia. The main surnames there, Booth, Matthews, Harrison, James, Atkinson, Trent, and Bennet, all appear on the subscriber list of the 1724 folio of Gilbert Burnet's History of His Own Time, a book Fielding owned. Watt's point is that the names were taken from the world of living people, even when a novelist grabbed them off a printed list.

A case history, a news story, or a novel persuades in the same way, by a person who could exist, on a dated afternoon. "A hypocrite" is a type. Pamela Andrews writing a letter at a stated hour is a report. Watt's book, published in 1957, ties that report to eighteenth-century England: Defoe, Richardson, Fielding, a reading public with time and money, and a philosophy that located a person in memory and in one spot on the map. Particular lives had been written before, in other languages and courts. His evidence is how English prose after 1700 started naming them the way a neighbor is named.
