Veronika Job, Carol Dweck, and Gregory Walton asked whether a willpower slump depends on believing that willpower runs out. In the first study, 60 students, 42 women and 18 men, rated statements such as "After a strenuous mental activity your energy is depleted and you must rest to get it refueled again," tucked among other questions so the point would not be obvious. Average agreement sat at 4.13 on a six-point scale, toward the limited-resource view. Everyone then crossed out letters for two five-minute pages. Some kept crossing every e. Others, on the second page, had to follow a stricter rule and sometimes leave an e alone. Then came a Stroop task, 48 trials, half of them incongruent: name the ink color, not the color word. Only students who held the limited view made more mistakes after the stricter letter task. For them the odds of an error rose, with an odds ratio of 1.88. For students one standard deviation toward the opposite view, the two letter tasks left accuracy essentially unchanged.

A second study pushed the belief instead of measuring it. A slanted questionnaire led people to agree either that strenuous work drains you or that it can leave you ready for more, and the same split appeared on the Stroop. Out of the lab, 101 undergraduates started a survey in April. Forty-one of them, 30 women and 11 men, were still answering during finals in the first week of June. Endorsing a limited theory in May predicted more unhealthy food, more time on television and similar distractions instead of studying, and less progress on a personal goal once exams arrived. The same belief did not predict those habits across the quieter stretch from April to May. The authors' conclusion was that the slump can be a theory people apply when they feel exhausted.

The idea is easy to use on a hard week: the sentence you repeat about a tired afternoon can change what you eat and whether you open the work. A preregistered repeat did not find the laboratory effect. Carruth, Ramos, and Miyake, in 2023, ran 187 people through the same letter task and the same Stroop, with the willpower questions measured rather than nudged. They found neither a clear depletion effect nor any moderation by belief. A story about stamina may still matter over a month of exams. It did not decide this task once the study was larger and the analysis was registered first.
