A theory returns to its authors, and reverses

Steven Maier and Martin Seligman published Learned helplessness at fifty: Insights from neuroscience in 2016, in Psychological Review. It is an unusual paper, because the people who introduced a famous theory use it to say that the theory had the mechanism backwards. In 1967 they had reported that dogs given inescapable shocks later failed to escape shocks they could easily have avoided by jumping a low barrier, while dogs given escapable shocks learned the jump quickly. The original explanation was that the animals had learned that their actions did not matter. Passivity was a lesson. Helplessness was learned. The idea travelled into accounts of depression, into Seligman's work on explanatory style, and into the popular book Learned Optimism. Fifty years later, the same authors argue that the passivity is not the lesson.

Folio's card, Learned helplessness, turned upside down, is a reading of this reversal. The card's summary is the one the 2016 paper asks the field to adopt. Prolonged aversive events recruit a default passivity, mediated by serotonin neurons in a brainstem region, the dorsal raphe nucleus. Detecting control, through a circuit that involves the medial prefrontal cortex, suppresses that default. Animals, and by extension the people the theory was always partly about, do not learn helplessness so much as they learn that they have control, which is a kind of hope, or at least a kind of controllability. Experiences of mastery may therefore protect against later stress, because they build the circuit that keeps the default from running the behaviour.

The paper is also a document in scientific manners. Maier and Seligman are unusually candid that a well-known theory, one they had defended and extended for decades, needed to be rewritten in the light of neuroscience they had helped to produce. A walkthrough should give the new mechanism clearly, and should say what the rewrite does to the 1967 experiments and to the later applied work, without pretending that a review article is a new dog study.

The default, and the brake

The revised story begins with the passivity itself. In the original experiments, the striking animals were the ones who sat through shocks they could now escape. The 1967 paper read their sitting as knowledge: they had learned, from the earlier inescapable shocks, that nothing they did would help, and they applied that knowledge to a new box where it was no longer true. The 2016 paper reads the sitting as a biological default that the earlier shocks had switched on and that nothing had switched off. Prolonged aversive stimulation activates serotonin neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus, and that activity produces the inhibition of escape. The animal fails to jump because a brainstem response has, for a time, taken the behaviour over. Learning is not required for the failure. The failure is what the system does unless a higher circuit says otherwise.

The higher circuit is where learning re-enters, with the opposite content. When an animal has experience of control, of turning the shock off by its own action, circuits involving the medial prefrontal cortex detect that control and inhibit the dorsal raphe response. The brake is what has to be acquired. Escapable shock, in the original design, is the training of the brake. Inescapable shock is the absence of that training, so the default runs. Later, in a new situation where escape is easy, an animal with a trained brake inhibits the passivity and learns the jump. An animal without it does not, or does so poorly. The behaviour looks like learned helplessness. The learning, on this account, was the learning of control in the animals who did escape, and the absence of that learning in the animals who did not.

The reversal is easy to miss because the observable pattern stays the same. Inescapable shock, then poor escape. Escapable shock, then competent escape. Everything a reader remembers from the textbook picture can remain in place. What changes is the arrow. The textbook arrow runs from uncontrollable bad events to a learned expectation of helplessness, and from that expectation to giving up. The 2016 arrow runs from prolonged bad events to a default of giving up, and from experiences of control to a learned suppression of the default. Hope, or controllability, is the acquired element. Helplessness is what you get when the acquisition has not happened. The card's phrasing is the cleanest short version. We do not learn helplessness. We learn hope, or at least controllability.

What the reversal does to therapy and to the original ethics

If control is the thing that is learned, then interventions that give people experiences of mastery are aimed at the circuit the theory now says matters. The card draws that implication carefully. Experiences of mastery may protect against later stress, which fits research on how early control buffers later reactions. The practical shift is one of emphasis. A therapy built on the old theory tries to talk people out of a learned belief that action is futile, and to replace pessimistic explanations with more accurate or more useful ones. A therapy built on the new theory still cares about belief, and it cares especially about arranging actual experiences in which action changes an outcome, so that the prefrontal brake has something to learn. Seligman's work on explanatory style, and the programmes that grew from it, is not thrown away by the paper.

The original experiments remain ethically troubling, and the card says so directly. Shocks delivered to dogs, some of them inescapable by design, would be difficult to justify to a modern committee, and the 2016 paper does not relitigate them as if the welfare questions had aged into triviality. The candour of the theoretical reversal sits beside an unavoidable harshness in the evidence base. Much of what is known about the dorsal raphe and the prefrontal brake comes from animal neuroscience that is itself invasive. A reader can accept the direction of the revision, passivity as default and control as the learned brake, and still want the human implications to be carried by human evidence: by studies of mastery, stress, and recovery, rather than by a casual transfer of a brainstem nucleus into a slogan about optimism. The paper is an argument for the transfer.

There is a further limit the authors are well placed to recognise. A review that reinterprets fifty years of work is a powerful narrative, and narratives of reversal are as tempting as the narratives they replace. The dorsal raphe account is a specification of a neural pathway, tied to particular experimental preparations. Depression, grief, poverty, and chronic fear in human lives are larger than a shuttle box. The card's claim is correspondingly modest. The original explanation had the learning backwards. The better description, given the neuroscience Maier and Seligman review, is that controllability is learned and that it suppresses a default passivity. That is a revision of a mechanism. It leaves most of human surrender still to be explained.

The older writings, after the rewrite

Failure to escape traumatic shock, the 1967 paper, is now a report of the phenomenon with an interpretation its own authors have withdrawn. The phenomenon, the difference between animals with and without prior control, stands. The claim that the passive animals had learned their actions did not matter does not, on the 2016 view, describe those animals. It describes the absence of the other lesson. Anyone teaching the 1967 study from Folio's card has to teach it in two layers: what the animals did, and what Maier and Seligman no longer think the doing meant. The paper is unusual precisely because the second layer comes from the same people as the first. There is no hostile critic to discount. The revision is internal.

Learned Optimism, Seligman's 1990 book, applies the older family of ideas to ordinary life, and it sits upstream of the Penn Resiliency Program and of the controversial resilience training Seligman later helped to develop for the US Army. The 2016 paper does not erase that applied tradition. It changes the story one tells about why any of it would work. If the useful ingredient is experience of control, then programmes that give people controllable challenges and a way of noticing their own effect are closer to the mechanism than programmes that only coach a vocabulary of optimism. If a programme ignores the situations people actually face, no vocabulary will install a prefrontal brake. The Army work remains controversial for reasons that go beyond the neural revision, reasons about institutions and about what resilience training is being asked to sustain. The card registers the controversy.

The phrase turned upside down, which the card uses in its title, is worth keeping as a description of the logic and a description of the evidence. The evidence for the neural account is a set of findings about circuits, not a single dramatic demonstration that replaces the shuttle box in the public imagination. Readers who want the shuttle box still have it, in the 1967 paper. Readers who want the current meaning of the shuttle box have this review. The honesty of the review is that the famous name, learned helplessness, names the phenomenon from the side that is not, in the authors' present view, the side where the learning is.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Learned helplessness, turned upside down, is a summary of this paper's reversal. Passivity after prolonged bad events is treated as a default, running through serotonin neurons of the dorsal raphe nucleus, and control is treated as the thing that is learned, by a medial prefrontal circuit that suppresses the default. The 1967 experiments remain the behavioural pattern: inescapable shock, then a failure to jump a barrier that could be jumped; escapable shock, then quick learning. Learned Optimism and the programmes that followed are the applications, written under the old arrow and still in need of the new one. Read the 2016 review for the authors' own change of mind, which is the card's reason for existing. The phenomenon stayed. The lesson, they now say, was hope, or controllability, all along.
