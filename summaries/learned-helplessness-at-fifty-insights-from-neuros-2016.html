Steven F. Maier and Martin E. P. Seligman spent fifty years on a finding from 1967. Dogs given shocks they could not stop, and then placed in a box where a jump would end a new shock, often sat through it. Six of eight dogs in the inescapable group failed to escape on nine or more of ten trials. Dogs who had been able to turn the first shocks off jumped normally. The authors’ original theory said the passive dogs had learned that nothing they did mattered. In a 2016 review in Psychological Review they say that theory had it backwards. Passivity is the default response to a long stretch of pain. It is produced by serotonin neurons in the dorsal raphe nucleus of the brainstem, which inhibit escape and amplify fear. What is learned is control. Detecting that an action turned the shock off uses the prelimbic region of the ventromedial prefrontal cortex. That region then inhibits the dorsal raphe, and the default passivity does not take hold.

The clearest demonstration is a shutdown, not a speech. Amat and colleagues, in 2005, inactivated the path from that prefrontal region down to the dorsal raphe while rats received shocks they could escape by turning a wheel. The rats learned the wheel perfectly. The protection failed anyway. The dorsal raphe fired as if the shocks had been inescapable, and the rats later showed the passivity and the extra anxiety of animals who had never had control. Doing the escape response was not what protected them. The signal that inhibits the raphe was. A second result shows the signal can become an expectation. Baratta and colleagues, in 2009, found that if a rat had had control a week earlier, even uncontrollable shock now turned on the same prefrontal cells that controllable shock normally turns on. The animal responded as if it could stop the shock, and it did not go passive.

For depression, illness, and work, the revised picture changes the question. The useful target is a history of actions that actually end the bad event, so the prefrontal brake gets built, rather than a pep talk about trying harder aimed at someone whose passivity is the unlearned reaction to stress that has gone on too long. Maier and Seligman note that they stopped the dog experiments as soon as they could, and that the same pattern appeared in rats and in people given loud noise they could or could not shut off. The 1967 behavior stands. The story that the animals learned to be helpless does not.
