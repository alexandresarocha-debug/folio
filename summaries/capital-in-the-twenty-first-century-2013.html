In France in 1910, Thomas Piketty's figures put national capital at almost seven years of national income, about one of those years invested abroad. The top tenth of wealth holders owned about 87 percent of all wealth, and the top hundredth owned about 53 percent. The middle 40 percent owned about 12 percent. The bottom half owned about 1 percent. By 2010 the top tenth's share was about 62 percent and the top hundredth's about 24 percent, while the middle 40 percent had risen to about 34 percent. Across Europe, private wealth was worth about six to seven years of national income in 1910, between two and three years in 1950, and between four and six years in 2010. The fortunes were cut by the world wars, the depression, and the inflation and taxes that came with them. They have been climbing back.

The mechanism he puts at the center is a simple comparison. When the return on capital, which he writes as r, runs ahead of the growth rate of the economy, g, inherited wealth compounds faster than output and faster than wages. A society of slow growth and a high return to property drifts toward a rentier pattern, in which the people who already own the capital pull away from the people who work. The nineteenth century, on his numbers, looked like that. The mid-twentieth century did not, because war and policy knocked the capital stock down and taxed what remained. Since about 1980, slower growth and higher returns at the top have pointed the other way. He does not claim that r greater than g is the only force. He claims it is the force that, left alone, rebuilds concentration.

The practical meaning is that a wage increase and a fortune are not on the same clock. In a slow-growing economy, saving and inheritance do more of the work of deciding who is rich than a good year at the office does. A tax on very high incomes and on estates is, in his argument, not a punishment laid on top of an otherwise natural distribution. It is one of the few tools that has actually kept r from outrunning g for decades at a time. Without some such tool, the France of 1910 is the picture a long peace tends to redraw. The middle-class share of wealth, the 12 percent that became 34 percent, is historically recent.

In May 2014 the Financial Times journalist Chris Giles reported problems in the spreadsheets. Some adjustments to the source data were unexplained. For French wealth in 1910, the correction used to turn the distribution among the dead into a distribution among the living was larger than the correction Piketty used for other years, and no reason was given in the file. Several early American top-tenth shares were constructed rather than copied from a source. Piketty answered that the French series, once the newspaper redrew it, looked virtually identical to his, and that the data are good enough to support the broad movement: a top tenth holding around 90 percent of European wealth in 1910, a fall to roughly 50 to 60 percent by the 1960s and 1970s, and a moderate rise since. They are not, he wrote, good enough to support much finer claims than that.
