What the book is measuring

Thomas Piketty’s Capital in the Twenty-First Century, published in 2013, is a history of wealth and income inequality built to answer a forward-looking question. Does a market society, left to its own devices, tend to concentrate capital, or did the more equal mid-twentieth century become the normal state of modern economies? Piketty’s answer is that the equal decades were the exception. They were produced by the shocks of war, depression, inflation, and the taxes and regulations that followed, not by a mature capitalism that had learned to share. The book is both a compilation of data and an argument about the forces that move the capital-income ratio and the distribution of ownership over the long run.

The data are the part of the project that has worn best, and they are not his alone. With Anthony Atkinson, Emmanuel Saez, and a wider group, he assembled long series from tax records, estate records, and national accounts, especially for France, Britain, and the United States, with comparisons that reach Germany, Sweden, Japan, and others. The point of using tax data is that the top of the distribution, which household surveys miss, is where capital income concentrates. The point of going back before 1914 is that a history starting in 1945 mistakes a trough for a baseline. Piketty wants the reader to see the Belle Époque and the present on one scale.

Two relations that organise the argument

The first analytical device is an accounting relation for the long-run size of the capital stock. In a simple steady state, the ratio of national wealth to national income equals the savings rate divided by the growth rate of the economy. When growth is slow and societies keep saving, wealth becomes large relative to the flow of annual income. Piketty uses this relation to organise a striking historical pattern. In Britain and France, the capital-income ratio was very high in the nineteenth century, collapsed with the world wars and the interwar shocks, and has climbed again since the middle of the twentieth century. Low growth, in this framework, is not a gentle background condition. It is one reason inherited wealth can bulk so large.

The second device is the inequality of the rate of return on capital and the growth rate of the economy, which he writes as r greater than g. When the return that owners can earn on accumulated wealth exceeds the rate at which output and wages grow, fortunes that have already been gathered tend to pull away from incomes that have to be earned year by year. He presents this as a force, not as a law that no institution can touch. Taxes, war, inflation, and a high growth rate can all interrupt it. A society that has stopped interrupting it should expect wealth inequality to rise, especially inequality of inheritance. The slogan became more famous than the caveats. The book itself spends many pages on the caveats, because the interesting history is the history of the interruptions.

What happened to inequality

The narrative that these relations are meant to explain has three acts. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, European societies in particular were highly unequal in wealth. A large share of national income went to the owners of land, housing, and financial claims, and the top of the wealth distribution lived on returns that labour income could not match. The novels of Austen and Balzac appear in the book as evidence of a world in which the choice between a profession and a marriage was a choice about capital. Piketty is using fiction as a social document of yields and dowries, not as a source of statistics.

The second act runs from 1914 through the post-war decades. Two world wars, the depression, and inflation destroyed or expropriated a great deal of capital, especially the capital of rentiers who held government debt and colonial or foreign assets. Governments then kept the distribution compressed with progressive income taxes, inheritance taxes, capital controls, and the bargaining power of labour. Piketty’s central historical claim is that this great compression was a political and military event. It was not the downward slope of a Kuznets curve, the mid-century idea that inequality rises in the early stages of industrialisation and then falls of its own accord as economies mature. Kuznets, on this reading, observed one abnormal fall and mistook it for development.

The third act is the rise in inequality since roughly the 1980s, strongest in the United States and Britain, milder in parts of continental Europe. Piketty separates two mechanisms that public debate often glues together. One is the return of capital: wealth-income ratios have risen, and with them the weight of income from property. The other, especially American, is the rise of very high labour incomes at the top of large firms, the “supersalaries” of senior managers, which he does not think are well explained as a simple price of rare talent. A reader who talks only about billionaires’ portfolios, or only about chief executives, is telling half of his story. Europe’s twentieth century is more a story about wealth. The recent United States is a story about wealth and about pay.

What he thinks should be done

The last movement of the book is prescriptive, and it is separable from the measurements. Piketty argues for progressive income taxes, for substantial taxation of inheritance, and for a global tax on capital. He is frank that a global wealth tax is politically remote. He offers it as a useful idea, a way of saying that capital will have to be measured and taxed if democracies want to know what is happening to it and to stop the r-greater-than-g dynamic from running away. Transparency is part of the proposal. A tax that forced large fortunes into the open would be informative even at a low rate. He also argues that the social state, education, and the historical tools of progressive taxation are accomplishments worth defending, not distortions that mature economies outgrow.

The prescription reveals the book’s politics, which are social-democratic and pessimistic about automatic correctives. Growth, in his account, will not by itself repeat the mid-century compression, because the growth rates of the catching-up decades are not the growth rates of economies at the frontier, and because population growth, one component of g, has slowed. If g is low and r is not, the arithmetic leans towards inheritance. Policy is the remaining counterweight. Readers can accept the arithmetic and reject the particular tax. Piketty’s claim is that they cannot accept the arithmetic and then expect the market to repeal it.

Limits

The limits are of three kinds, and they have been argued since the book appeared. The first is empirical. Critics, including a widely discussed challenge to some of the British wealth series, have questioned particular constructions, the treatment of housing, and the way capital gains enter the aggregates. The broad pattern, high inequality before 1914, a sharp compression, and a partial rebound, has held up better than any single cell in the spreadsheets. The second limit is theoretical. Economists have pointed out that r greater than g does not, by itself, determine the distribution of wealth. Who saves, where the return comes from, and whether high returns are competed away all matter. A force is not a forecast. Piketty’s own historical chapters are more convincing than the most abstract statement of the formula, because the chapters are full of the institutions the formula leaves out.

The third limit is one of scope. The book is mainly about inequality inside rich countries, not about the global distribution of income among all the world’s people, which has a different history over the same decades because poor countries have been growing. It is also more confident about capital than about the politics of the reforms it wants. A global tax on capital presupposes a cooperation that the book’s own story, of national tax systems racing and rich households fleeing, gives reason to doubt. None of these limits erases the achievement of the series. They mark the difference between a measured history of wealth and a complete theory of modern inequality.

Why it matters here

The idea alongside this book is Walter Scheidel’s claim that large levellings have come from violent shocks. Piketty supplies the modern evidence that makes that claim concrete for capital. The compression from 1914 into the post-war decades, and the subsequent rise as the shocks receded, is Scheidel’s mass-mobilisation horseman described from the side of tax records and national accounts. The two books do not say the same thing. Scheidel ranges across plagues and collapsed empires, and he is bleaker about peaceful reform. Piketty thinks progressive taxation and a social state can push against concentration, and he spends his last chapters saying how. What they share is the refusal to treat the mid-twentieth century as the destination towards which capitalism naturally moves. Here, Capital is the account of how wealth actually behaved in the rich countries, and of why a politics that wants the compression without the wars has to build institutions the wars once made possible.
