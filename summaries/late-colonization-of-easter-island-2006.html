When did people first reach Rapa Nui? The answer matters, because the classic collapse story needs a long, slow build-up before a crash. An early date of around AD 400 came from a burn layer at the Poike Ditch, dated by Thor Heyerdahl's Norwegian expedition in the 1950s, and lake cores seemed to show major vegetation change by about AD 750.

Terry Hunt and Carl Lipo excavated at Anakena beach, a likely landing place, in 2004 and 2005. The lowest cultural layer sat on an ancient soil containing artefacts, charcoal, bones of the Polynesian rat and the root moulds of the extinct giant palm. Eight radiocarbon dates on short-lived wood charcoal from these layers pointed to first settlement around AD 1200.

They then re-examined older dates. The lake-core dates came from bulk sediment that mixes old and young organic material, making them hundreds of years too old. And 41 radiocarbon dates linked to deforestation from sites around the island all fell after AD 1200, with most after AD 1300.

So the timeline shrank. People arrived later, and forest clearance and the building of monuments and statues began soon after settlement, rather than centuries later. Hunt and Lipo suggested that the island's population grew rapidly, as Polynesian colonists elsewhere did, and that the demographic and cultural collapse came after European contact in 1722, with new diseases striking a population with no immunity.

What this means in real life: a single shift in dates can overturn a famous story about the past, which is why careful radiocarbon dating sits at the heart of debates about environmental collapse.

Caution: the new excavation dates came from a single site, and radiocarbon dates carry wide uncertainty; the authors' own analysis allows a small probability of settlement somewhat before 1200. The late chronology alone does not settle whether deforestation caused social decline; later work on monuments, farming and genomes took up that question.

Source. Hunt, T. L., and Lipo, C. P. (2006). Late colonization of Easter Island. Science, 311(5767), 1603-1606. doi:10.1126/science.1121879. Link: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1121879
