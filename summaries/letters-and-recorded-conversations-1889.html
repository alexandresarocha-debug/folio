Advice that survived in other people's words

The principle now called Chekhov's gun is not a manifesto Chekhov published under that name. It is a cluster of remarks, made in letters and remembered from conversations in the years around 1889, in which he told other writers to be ruthless about what a story contains. The wording differs from source to source, which is what happens when a standard is reconstructed from correspondence and from what acquaintances later said they heard. One familiar image is a rifle hanging on the wall in the first act. If the audience has been made to notice it, the rifle ought to matter before the play is over, and if it is not going to matter it ought not to be hanging there. Other versions of the advice omit the rifle and speak more generally. Anything that has no bearing on the action should be cut. A detail that does no work is not a neutral ornament. It is a distraction, and, once it has been emphasised, it is a promise.

The argument inside the various wordings is about the attention of an audience. Readers and spectators are not cameras. They assume that a story is a designed object, and they treat salience as significance. If a writer spends a sentence, a gesture, or a visible prop on something, the audience files it as a thing that will pay. The filing is not a pedantry that sophisticated people rise above. It is the basic cooperation by which stories are followed. Chekhov's advice is a writer's acknowledgement of that cooperation. You may not introduce a salient particular and then forget it, because the audience will not forget it. And you should not introduce particulars you do not need, because you will have spent attention you required for the particulars you do need. Economy, in this sense, is not minimalism as a style. A rich scene can be economical if every richness is doing something. A bare scene can be wasteful if its one object is a false lead that was never intended as a lead.

Promise, payoff, and the difference between emphasis and world

It helps to separate two kinds of detail, because the gun principle is often quoted as if it condemned every object that does not fire. A story needs a world. Rooms contain things, people have habits, weather happens, and a great deal of this texture is there to locate the action in a life rather than to set up a plot point. The principle bites when a detail is emphasised, placed where attention cannot miss it, described with a care that exceeds its surroundings, or repeated. Emphasis is the promise. An unemphasised chair is furniture. A chair that the opening lingers on, that a character is told not to touch, that the dialogue circles back to, has been recruited. Once recruited, it has a job, and the job has to be done or the recruitment was a lie. Chekhov's various remarks are ways of telling writers to notice when they have recruited something. The rifle is simply the clearest case, because a weapon on a wall is already, in the culture of the stage, an emphasised object. It arrives pre-loaded with expectation.

The principle is therefore a rule about the writer's control of expectation, not a rule that every cause must produce a violent effect. The payoff may be a revelation, a reversal, a joke, a quiet confirmation, or the transformation of the object into a meaning the audience did not expect. What the payoff may not be is absence. Leaving the recruited object unused does not make a story subtle. It makes the audience's cooperation look foolish, and audiences who feel foolish stop cooperating. Mystery writers, who seem to break the rule, are usually obeying a more complicated version of it. A red herring is an emphasised detail whose payoff is the discovery that it was a false trail. The falsehood is the function. The detail was not idle. It was spent on a misdirection the story intends to correct. The gun principle, properly understood, allows that expenditure. It does not allow a herring that even the author has forgotten.

What Chekhov was trying to protect

Read against Chekhov's own plays and stories, the advice is less a demand for tight plotting in the sensational sense than a demand that the stage not be cluttered with effects that the action will not take up. He was severe, in his letters, about writing that explains too much, feels too publicly, and solves its people in speeches. The same severity covers props and incidents. A pistol, a bandage, a seagull shot for no reason and then turned into a speech, a play performed in the garden: these can sit in a work only if the work is prepared to mean them. His famous reluctance to preach is of a piece with the gun. A moral stated and then abandoned is the same kind of broken promise as a weapon shown and then ignored. Both of them tell the audience that the writer's emphasis cannot be trusted. Economy is the condition of trust.

The advice also protects the ending from having to do too much explanatory work. If every salient detail along the way has been a live promise, the ending can fulfil rather than introduce. Writers who litter the early scenes with vivid unused objects often find themselves, at the close, either contriving hasty uses or hoping the reader has forgotten. Chekhov's standard removes that hope. Assume the reader remembers. Write only what you are willing to have remembered. This is why the principle has been adopted by screenwriters under the name of setup and payoff, and why it survives in classrooms that have never read the letters. The classroom version is sometimes cruder than the scattered remarks, as if any object in a filmed room were a moral debt. The remarks themselves, as far as the varying records let us state them, are about necessity and about emphasis. Cut what the story does not use. If you are going to make the audience look at a thing, let the looking have been worth it.

The counter-principle, and the limit of the rule

A serious limit sits next to the rule, and Chekhov's own practice is part of the reason to state it. Realist fiction often includes details that seem to promise nothing and that work precisely by promising nothing. Roland Barthes would later call this the reality effect: a particular that resists being recruited as a function, and that thereby signifies "this is the real, which is fuller than any plot". A barometer on the wall, in his example from Flaubert, need not go off like a gun. Its idleness is the point. If Chekhov's advice were applied as a universal purge of non-functional detail, realism of that kind would be impossible, and so would a great deal of comedy, atmosphere, and the depiction of boredom. The reconciliation is in the notion of emphasis. The reality effect depends on details that are present without being spotlighted. The gun principle governs the spotlight. A writer needs both the room and the promise, and needs the judgement to tell them apart.

There is a further limit inside Chekhov's art itself. His plays are full of objects and lines that several characters interpret differently, so that the payoff is not a single click of plot but a deepening argument about what the object means. The seagull in the play of that name is emphasised and then reused, which satisfies the principle, and it is reused as a literary cliché the characters cannot leave alone, which is a subtler satisfaction than a weapon being discharged. Anyone who reduces the letters and conversations to a thriller manual has lost the writer who made the remarks. He wanted necessity. He understood necessity as including the emotional and thematic debts a story incurs, not only the mechanical ones. The rifle has to matter. Mattering can be a death, or a change in what a symbol is worth, or the quiet revelation that a speech was doing the work of a prop all along.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading is that every emphasised detail is a promise, and that a writer who introduces unused elements breaks an implicit contract with the audience. These letters and remembered conversations are the source of that idea, and they are a scattered source. There is no single canonical sentence. There is a repeated standard of economy, remembered most clearly in the image of the rifle that must become relevant because it was shown, and that must not be shown if it will not become relevant. The Seagull is the play in which Chekhov both honours the standard and complicates it, with a gun that does fire and a bird that becomes a symbol the characters overuse. Barthes's reality effect is the thoughtful objection, the case for details whose work is to be useless to the plot. Hold the objection, and the principle becomes sharper rather than weaker. It was never a ban on the ordinary fullness of a world. It was a ban on emphasis without consequence, stated by a writer whose own consequences are often quieter than the rifle metaphor leads people to expect.
