On 1 November 1889, in Moscow, Anton Chekhov wrote to Aleksandr Lazarev, who published as Gruzinsky, about a vaudeville Lazarev had sent him. The play was called An Old Friend. Chekhov had read it at once. The writing, he said, was fine, and the construction was impossible. Dasha's opening monologue had to go. It stuck out. It would have earned its place only if Dasha were more than a walk-on, and only if a speech that promised the audience so much actually had something to do with the rest of the play. Then the sentence that got loose from the letter: one must not put a loaded rifle on the stage if no one means to fire it. One must not make promises. Better, he said, that Dasha say nothing at all.

He had already put the same rule more sharply, in conversation, the summer before. Ilya Gurlyand later reported what Chekhov told him at Yalta in 1889, and published the recollection in July 1904: if in the first act you hang a pistol on the wall, then in the last act it must be fired. Otherwise do not hang it there. A third wording, from S. Shchukin's memoirs in 1911, moves the rifle from the stage to the page. If you say in the first chapter that a rifle hangs on the wall, it has to go off in the second or third chapter. If it is not going to be fired, it should not be hanging there. The advice is one advice. The prop, the speech, and the chapter are all promises. Dasha's monologue is the case in front of him, a speech that excites the spectator and then never pays.

In practice this is a test you can run late, when a draft feels cluttered. Every conspicuous object, name, or anecdote has opened an account. A gun on the mantel, a scar mentioned twice, a locked drawer, a sibling who is described and then vanishes: the reader is waiting. You can close the account by using the thing, or you can close it by cutting the mention. What you cannot do is leave the promise standing and hope nobody noticed. The rule is not a ban on atmosphere. A room can be full of objects that are only there to be a room. Chekhov's target is the thing you have pointed at, the loaded one, the speech that "promises a great deal." Point less, or make good on the point. Later readers turned the line into a plot trick, as if every story owed them a bang. The letter is sterner and duller than that. It is about not lying to the audience with your own scenery.
