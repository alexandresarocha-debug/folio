Nogales is one town cut by a fence. Daron Acemoglu and James A. Robinson open Why Nations Fail with the two halves. In Nogales, Arizona, they report, the average household income is about $30,000 a year. Most teenagers are in school, and the people north of the fence can choose a job, vote out a bad official, and expect a court to notice if the state takes their property. In Nogales, Sonora, the average household income is about a third of that. Mothers worry about higher infant mortality, many teenagers are not in school, and corruption is an ordinary fact of dealing with officials. The desert, the climate, the food, and, for many families, the cousins are the same. The rules are not.

Korea is the sharper case, because the split is recent. One peninsula, one language, and a shared history were divided in 1945 and then by the war of 1950–53. The north became a hereditary command state. The south became a market economy and, later, a democracy. Any story that starts with "Korean culture" or with the peninsula's geography has to explain why the same culture and the same hills produced opposite results inside a lifetime. The institutional story has an easier time. Inclusive economic rules, in their terms, let new people enter a market and let inventors keep enough of the return to make the work worth doing. Extractive rules move wealth to a narrow group, by forced labor, monopoly, or the plain power to seize. Political power comes first. People who cannot be removed write economic rules that pay themselves.

The everyday version is familiar. A shopkeeper who thinks the license, the tax, or the cousin of the mayor will take the gains does not expand the shop. An elite that can block the new machine, the new port, or the new competitor often will, even when the blocked idea would have made the country richer. Acemoglu and Robinson call that fear of creative destruction. Growth can still happen under extractive rules, by moving people into work the elite approves. They argue that it runs out when the next step would hand power to people the elite cannot control. China, growing fast under a system they class as extractive, is the case that can prove them wrong if the growth does not run out.

The colonial evidence they lean on has been challenged in detail. David Albouy, in 2012, reexamined the settler-death rates in their 2001 paper and found that 36 of the 64 countries had been given a death rate borrowed from somewhere else. After he dropped those countries and separated campaign deaths from peacetime deaths, the statistical link from mortality to institutions was no longer robust. The Nogales fence and the Korean border do not depend on that dataset. The claim that colonial death rates have already measured the size of the institutional effect does.
