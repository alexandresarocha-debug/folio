Rules, not maps or cultures

Why Nations Fail, published in 2012 by Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, is the book-length statement of a theory they had been building for more than a decade. The opening claim is that the prosperity of nations depends above all on institutions, the rules that structure economic and political life. Geography, culture, and ignorance of the right policy are the rival explanations they want to demote. A hot climate did not make Nogales, Sonora poor and Nogales, Arizona rich. A national character did not split Korea. What differs on either side of those borders is the set of rules. Inclusive economic institutions protect property, enforce contracts, let new people enter a market, and let inventors keep enough of the return to make invention worthwhile. Extractive institutions are built to move wealth to a narrow elite, by forced labour, monopolies, or the simple power to seize. The book argues that economic rules rest on political ones. Where power is concentrated, the people who hold it create extractive economics, because those economics pay them. Where power is spread and constrained, inclusive economics can survive the temptation of the next ruler to grab.

Nogales, Korea, and the colonial fork

The first cases are chosen because they hold the usual causes still. The two halves of Nogales share a desert, a history of Spanish and then Mexican rule on both sides until a border was drawn, and families that cross for the afternoon. They do not share a legal system, a police force, or a set of expectations about whether effort will be confiscated. Acemoglu and Robinson treat the income and security gap across that fence as a photograph of institutions. The two Koreas are the sharper photograph. A single nation, divided by the accidents of 1945 and then by war, produced a dynastic command state in the north and a market society, eventually a democratic one, in the south. Any explanation that begins with deep culture or with the peninsula's geography has to say why the same culture and the same geography gave opposite results within a lifetime. The institutional explanation has an easier time, which is why the authors start there. A theory that cannot handle the easy cases will not be trusted with the hard ones.

The colonial fork is their historical engine. European expansion, they argue, created different kinds of societies depending on whether Europeans could settle and on what they found when they arrived. Where dense populations and extractable wealth were already present, as in much of Latin America and in the plantation colonies, the newcomers built institutions to coerce labour and ship surplus to a small ruling group. Where they settled in large numbers in places without a dense population to coerce, they built institutions closer to the ones they demanded for themselves, with broader property rights and, eventually, broader political rights. The 2001 paper is the statistical version of this fork, using settler mortality as the sorter. The book is the narrative version. Spanish colonial institutions, the encomienda and its successors, the mining of silver for an imperial elite, stand on one side. The settler colonies of North America, with their assemblies and their different, not innocent, history of land and labour, stand on the other. The authors are not claiming that settlers were virtuous. They are claiming that a wider group of people with a stake in the rules produced rules that favoured growth.

Critical junctures and creative destruction

Institutions, once set, do not drift gently. The book uses the idea of a critical juncture, a moment when a shock loosens the old arrangement and small differences decide which way a society goes. The Black Death is one of their junctures. A demographic catastrophe hit both western and eastern Europe. In the west, labour scarcity and the relative weakness of landlords undermined serfdom. In the east, landlords tightened it. The same disease, on this account, pushed institutions in opposite directions because the prior balance of power was not the same. The Glorious Revolution of 1688 in England is another. A change in who could constrain the king made property and the public finances more credible, and the authors treat that political change as a foundation of the later industrial economy. A reader does not have to accept every link in that chain to see the form of the argument. History has hinges. The hinge amplifies a small institutional difference into a long divergence.

Creative destruction is the mechanism they think elites fear. New technologies and new groups do not merely add wealth. They displace the old owners of workshops, licences, and political favours. An elite that is politically secure enough to block the new machine, the new port, or the new entrepreneur will often do so, even if the blocked innovation would have enriched the country. Extractive institutions are therefore not only unfair. They are hostile to the very process by which sustained growth happens. Inclusive institutions tolerate the displacement because no single group can stop it, and because the political system gives the displaced a reason to stay inside the rules rather than smash them. The authors describe virtuous circles, in which inclusive politics and inclusive economics reinforce each other, and vicious circles, in which extractive politics and extractive economics do the same. The circles are why they expect persistence. A bad equilibrium is stable. That is the gloomy half of a theory whose hopeful half is that junctures can, sometimes, break it.

What the cases are supposed to prove

The conclusion they want is comparative and practical. Nations fail when their political institutions empower a narrow group that then organises the economy for its own take, and they succeed when political power is constrained enough that the economy can be organised for entry, investment, and innovation. Policy advice that ignores this, a recommendation to liberalise prices, or to build a factory, offered to a government whose survival depends on rents, will be ignored or captured. Foreign aid that strengthens an extractive state can entrench the failure it was meant to ease. The book is willing to be blunt about contemporary cases, and the bluntness is part of its appeal and part of its exposure. A theory that names living governments has to be right about them, not only about the encomienda.

China is the case they know they must face. Decades of very rapid growth under a political system they classify as extractive looks, at first glance, like a refutation. Their answer is that extractive institutions can produce growth by moving labour and capital into activities the elite approves, and that this growth runs out when further advance requires creative destruction that would empower people the elite cannot control. They predict a limit. Critics reply that a limit which keeps receding is not yet an explanation, and that the Chinese combination of a strong state, markets in goods, and tight political control may be a kind of institution their binary does not describe well. The argument is unresolved because it is about the future as well as the past. It shows where the theory can be falsified, which is a virtue, and it shows that the most important contemporary growth story sits awkwardly in the framework, which is a warning.

Limits the theory has been made to admit

The definitions are the first vulnerability. Inclusive and extractive are clear at the extremes, Korea against Korea, and stretchable in the middle. A society can protect property for the majority of its own citizens and deny it to enslaved people, to women, or to colonial subjects. The United States in the nineteenth century is, on the authors' own account, a mixture, inclusive relative to many contemporaries and extractive in the slave South. Once mixtures are allowed, a critic can reclassify awkward cases after the fact. The theory is most convincing where the contrast is stark and the border is recent. It is less convincing where every long-lived society is a blend, which is most of history.

The second vulnerability is the empirical foundation, and they do not hide it. The colonial-origins paper has been challenged on the quality of the settler-mortality data, most prominently by David Albouy, and the authors have replied. A popular book that treats the institutional effect as established is leaning on a design whose historical instrument is disputed. The third vulnerability is what the theory sets aside. Geography is not only a sorter of colonial strategies. Soil, location, and disease still affect what an inclusive institution can get out of a given territory. Culture and ideas, including the belief that the rules ought to be fair, are treated largely as products of institutions, which can be circular if the institutions were themselves the product of an idea. Acemoglu and Robinson would say that ideas matter when they are embodied in rules that someone can enforce. Their critics, including historians of science and of political thought, think that leaves the origin of the rules under-explained. The book is a powerful account of incentives and of persistence. It is not a complete account of how people come to want different incentives.

Why it matters here

The card diamond-geography cites Why Nations Fail as the institutional answer to a geographic theory of who got rich. The contrast between a Nogales explanation and a domesticable-plants explanation is exactly the contrast the card is teaching. The card acemoglu-institutions is the book's home. Inclusive against extractive, the dependence of economic rules on political ones, the colonial fork, the critical junctures, and the problem of creative destruction are the card's summary of this text. The card also records the prize awarded to Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson in 2024 for the research programme, of which the book is the public statement, and it records the two standing objections: that the definitions can be stretched to fit, and that Chinese growth complicates a simple extractive-institutions story. Folio uses the book as the narrative a reader needs before the 2001 paper, the Albouy comment, and The Narrow Corridor will make sense. It is the place where the institutional theory is stated in cases rather than in estimators.
