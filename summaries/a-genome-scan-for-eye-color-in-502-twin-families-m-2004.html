How much of eye colour is in the genes? Gu Zhu, Nicholas Martin and colleagues in Australia answered this with twins and a scan of their DNA.

They rated the eye colour of people in 502 twin families on a simple three-step scale: 1 for blue or grey, 2 for hazel or green, and 3 for brown. Then they read 400 to 757 DNA markers spread across every chromosome, looking for places where brothers and sisters who shared more DNA also had more similar eyes.

One place stood out by far. Near a marker called D15S1002 on chromosome 15, the evidence score (a LOD score) was 19.2. A score of 3 is the usual bar for a real finding. The marker sat less than 1 centimorgan, a very short genetic distance, from a gene called OCA2, which earlier work had already tied to eye colour.

The team then split the differences in eye colour into parts. About 74% were explained by this one region. A further 18% came from many other genes with small effects. Together that is about 92% genetic, which puts eye colour near the top of the 0-to-1 heritability scale.

There were faint hints elsewhere. Scores above 2 appeared on chromosomes 5 and 14, but none reached the bar for a firm finding. The authors concluded that most eye colour variation in Europeans is due to OCA2, with possible smaller modifiers at other places in the genome.

What this means in real life: for most traits, like height or personality, genes are spread thin across thousands of places. Eye colour is the rare case where one small part of the DNA does most of the work. That makes it the clearest example of a trait with heritability close to 1.

Caution: the authors themselves warn that a broad shoulder on the chromosome 15 peak suggests other genes nearby, which may have inflated the 74% figure. The three-step scale is coarse. It lumps very different shades together. The families were of European descent, where eye colour varies most, so the numbers may not hold in other groups. The 92% is measured on a statistical scale of underlying risk, not on the colours directly.
