The Many Smiles Collaboration was a global adversarial collaboration: researchers who believed in facial feedback and researchers who doubted it designed a single preregistered study together, aimed at the conditions most likely to produce an effect. Fritz Strack, author of the original pen study, was among them.

The study ran in many labs across 19 countries, with data from 3,878 participants. Each person did one of three facial tasks: copying a facial expression (facial mimicry), moving particular facial muscles on instruction (voluntary facial action), or holding a pen in the mouth. Some viewed positive images while posing; others did not. Participants then rated how happy they felt.

Two methods worked. Smiling by mimicry raised happiness ratings by 0.49 points compared with a neutral expression, and smiling by voluntary facial action by 0.40, both with strong statistical support. These effects appeared whether or not people were also looking at positive images, so facial feedback could both strengthen and start feelings of happiness.

The pen did not. In the pen-in-mouth task, the difference was just 0.04 points, and Bayesian analyses gave moderate support for no effect. But there was a twist: when the team relaxed its strict exclusion rules, including those for participants judged to be aware of the hypothesis, a small pen effect of 0.14 points appeared. The authors had registered in advance that this would count as a weaker test.

What this means in real life: deliberately putting on a smile can lift your mood slightly, but the hidden, unobtrusive version of the effect is fragile.

Caution: almost half of participants were excluded for showing awareness of the hypothesis under a very strict rule, and the effects are small on the rating scale. The authors note that it is still debated whether being recorded on video interferes with the effect.
