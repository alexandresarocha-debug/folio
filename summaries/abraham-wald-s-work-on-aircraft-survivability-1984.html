By the early 1980s Wald's wartime survivability memos had been declassified and reprinted, but they had not appeared in the open statistical literature. Marc Mangel, a mathematician, and Francisco Samaniego, a statistician, both at the University of California, Davis, wrote this condensed account for the Journal of the American Statistical Association.

They set the scene with dates. December 7, 1981, was the 40th anniversary of Pearl Harbor and of the birth of the Statistical Research Group, where Wald developed techniques for estimating how well aircraft survived enemy fire. His methods, they write, were used in the Second World War and later by the Navy and Air Force in the wars in Korea and Vietnam. There were eight memoranda, totalling over 100 pages.

They describe the operational problem plainly. Planes returning from missions have hits spread over the wings, tail, fuselage and other parts. A commander must decide which tactics would improve survival and how to reinforce the plane, knowing that reinforcement adds weight and hurts performance. The commander does not know the distribution of hits on planes that did not return. That is the basic difficulty: the data on downed aircraft are unobservable.

The authors follow the logic of Wald's derivations, simplify the notation, give sample data, and then re-examine his results using later statistical theory, including maximum likelihood and methods for missing data. Their conclusion is that Wald's treatment of these problems was definitive. They also stress that his work is interesting as an early solution to what statisticians now call a missing-data problem.

What this means in real life: when part of your sample is missing for a reason connected to the outcome, the missing part has to be modelled, not ignored.

Caution: this is an exposition of technical memos, not a history of how the military used them. The popular story of the dotted bomber is not taken from this paper.
