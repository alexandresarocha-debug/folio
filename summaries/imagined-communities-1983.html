Benedict Anderson's definition, in Imagined Communities (1983), describes a limit. A nation, he writes, "is an imagined political community — and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign." It is imagined because "the members of even the smallest nation will never know most of their fellow-members, meet them, or even hear of them, yet in the minds of each lives the image of their communion." The image is strong enough that people die for it. His question is how strangers came to carry it, and why then.

His sharpest case is the creole officials of Spanish America, people of European descent born in the colonies. On the eve of the revolution in Mexico, Anderson reports, citing the historian John Lynch, the viceroyalty had one creole bishop, although creoles outnumbered Spaniards born in Spain by 70 to 1. A creole could rise inside Mexico or Chile. A career in Spain was almost closed. José de San Martín had been taken to Spain as a boy and spent the next 27 years there before he sailed home to a rebellion. The top of the creole circuit was the colonial capital itself. Newspapers in Mexico City, Buenos Aires, and Bogotá already spoke to that public. The United States, Brazil, and the Spanish American republics of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries were led by people who shared a language with the empires they left. The boundary that mattered was the boundary of the career.

Print is the other machine. Printers looking for a larger market than Latin could supply published in the spoken languages. Once a language was in type, people who could not follow one another's dialect could share a page. Anderson calls those fellow readers the embryo of the nation. A newspaper carries a date. The reader knows that thousands of others are holding the same object on the same morning. That daily ceremony is how a society of strangers comes to feel that it occupies one time.

A nation can feel old and still run on recent machinery. A border or a war asks people to treat strangers as kin. On Anderson's account the feeling was trained by presses and by the maps of colonial administration. He puts the first successful wave in the Americas, ahead of most nineteenth-century European nationalisms. Anthony D. Smith, in The Ethnic Origins of Nations (1986), answered that this underplays the older names, memories, and symbols that national movements usually take up. Print can standardize a public. On Smith's argument it does not create the people from a blank page. Anderson's "imagined" means the members will never meet, and have been given a way to picture one another.
