Britain buried an unidentified soldier in Westminster Abbey on 11 November 1920. France buried one under the Arc de Triomphe on 28 January 1921. The United States dedicated the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Arlington on 11 November 1921. Benedict Anderson, in Imagined Communities (1983), starts from rites like these. "No more arresting emblems of the modern culture of nationalism exist than cenotaphs and tombs of Unknown Soldiers," he writes. The reverence depends on the blank. If someone discovered the name and filled it in, the ceremony would be wrecked. The dead man is not a neighbor the crowd can point to. He stands for a people most of whom will never meet.

Anderson's definition is short. A nation is an imagined political community, imagined as both limited and sovereign. Imagined, because even in a small country most members never meet. Limited, because every nation has edges. Sovereign, because this kind of community took hold as kings who claimed a divine right to rule stopped looking obvious. He is not calling the nation a fraud. People die for it. His question is how strangers came to picture themselves as one public.

His sharpest numbers come from the Spanish empire in the Americas. Of the 170 viceroys who served there before 1813, four were creoles, American-born men of Spanish descent. In 1800, of about 3,200,000 whites in that empire, set over about 13,700,000 indigenous people, less than 5 percent had been born in Spain. On the eve of the revolution in Mexico there was one creole bishop, though creoles outnumbered the Spain-born by 70 to 1. A creole official could rise inside Mexico or Chile. Madrid was not, in the ordinary way, the top of his ladder. The colony's own capital was. Anderson's claim is that the later republics grew inside those imperial boxes. Newspapers taught the people in a box to picture one another. In North America between 1691 and 1820, at least 2,120 newspapers were published, and 461 lasted more than ten years. A dated sheet, read on the same morning by people who would never meet, was a daily rehearsal of a public.

That is a test you can take to a national story. When a nation is described as a people who woke after sleeping for centuries, ask what first made a public of strangers picturable. Anderson's answer is print as a business. Citing Lucien Febvre and Henri-Jean Martin, he notes that more than 20 million volumes had been printed in Europe by 1500, and that printers then chased readers in the languages people spoke. A shared page is not a shared ancestry. The creole careers show that the frame was often an empire's map of jobs, drawn before anyone called it a fatherland.

Anthony D. Smith answered in The Ethnic Origins of Nations (1986) that this underplays the older names, myths, and memories national movements usually fasten onto. Print can spread a story. It does not, by itself, explain why that story was the one at hand. Anderson's revised edition of 1991 added the census, the map, and the museum, because in many colonies the state drew the community before most people could read.
