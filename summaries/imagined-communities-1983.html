A definition, and what it does not say

Benedict Anderson’s Imagined Communities, first published in 1983, offers a definition of the nation that has outlived the debates around it. A nation, he proposes, is an imagined political community, and it is imagined as both limited and sovereign. It is imagined because the members of even the smallest nation will never know most of their fellow members, yet each carries an image of their communion. It is a community because the nation is conceived as a deep, horizontal comradeship, whatever inequality actually exists inside it. It is limited because every nation, however large, has finite boundaries beyond which other nations lie. It is sovereign because the idea matured in an age when divinely ordained, hierarchical dynasties were losing their self-evidence, and the legitimate state was coming to be understood as self-governing.

The definition is easy to misread as an accusation of falsehood. Anderson’s “imagined” is not a synonym for “imaginary” in the sense of fraudulent. People really do share the image, and they really do die for it. His question is how such a form of community became possible, and why it became possible when it did. He is writing against two habits: the nationalist habit of treating the nation as an awakening of something ancient, and the habit of explaining nationalism as a European idea that was later exported, fully formed, to the rest of the world. The book is a cultural history of a political form.

What had to weaken first

Anderson begins with the cultural conditions that made the nation thinkable. For a long time, he argues, three older ideas held the frame of the world together. A sacred script-language, Latin, Arabic, or classical Chinese, was believed to offer privileged access to truth. Society was organised around high centres, monarchs who ruled by a cosmic or divine dispensation rather than as representatives of a people. And time itself was understood in a way that mixed cosmology with history, so that the present was not a slice of homogeneous clock-time shared by strangers. As these conceptions lost their grip, a different kind of community could be imagined: a body of people moving together through calendrical time, bounded, and the source of its own political authority.

He takes the phrase “homogeneous, empty time” from Walter Benjamin and uses it for a specific purpose. The nation is a community of people who may never meet and who nevertheless understand themselves to be doing something simultaneously. That understanding is not a philosophical deduction. It is a habit trained by particular cultural forms. Anderson’s candidates are the novel and the newspaper, both of which present a world of parallel lives. A novel moves between characters unknown to one another with a “meanwhile”. A newspaper is dated, and its reader knows that thousands of others are reading the same dated object on the same morning. The ceremony is quiet and daily. Out of it comes the confidence that a society of strangers exists in the same time.

Print-capitalism

The mechanism that produced those forms, and that standardised the languages they were written in, is what Anderson calls print-capitalism. Printing was a business. The market for Latin books was limited, and printers who wanted to grow looked to the vernaculars, where the readers were. Once a vernacular was printed, its grammar and vocabulary began to stabilise. Dialects that could not command a press were pushed towards a standardised language of reading. Speakers who could not understand one another’s speech could share a page. Anderson’s claim is that these print-languages created unified fields of exchange below Latin and above the spoken vernaculars, and that they did so for commercial reasons that nobody designed as a theory of nationality.

This is the junction with the history of the press. Anderson is not repeating a claim that Gutenberg caused the nation. He is saying that once printing became a capitalist trade in vernaculars, it assembled the audiences that nationalists would later name. The Reformation and the novel matter in his account because they were print events that taught people to imagine a wide community of readers. The nation is what that community looks like once it is also imagined as limited and sovereign. Print supplies the fellow readers. Politics supplies the boundary and the claim to rule.

Creole pioneers and official nationalism

The most distinctive historical claim in the book is about sequence. Anderson argues that the first wave of nationalism was not in Europe but in the Americas, among creole elites, people of European descent born in the colonies. Their careers, what he calls their pilgrimages, were bounded by the administrative units of empire. A creole official could rise inside a viceroyalty and could not, in the same way, arrive at the metropolitan centre. Newspapers in Boston, Mexico City, or Caracas addressed a public that was already the public of a future state. The nation grew up inside a colonial jurisdiction. This is his answer to accounts that treat Latin American and North American independence as a late application of a European idea. On his telling, the creoles were pioneers, and European nationalisms of the nineteenth century had American examples in front of them.

He then describes a second pattern, which he calls official nationalism. Once popular national movements threatened dynasties, the dynasties nationalised themselves. Russification in the Romanov empire, and comparable policies elsewhere, were attempts by old empires to look like the nations that were challenging them. The nation, having been imagined from below by reading publics, was then imposed from above by states that had every reason to fear it. A later edition of the book added a further instrument of this official imagination, especially in colonial settings: the census, the map, and the museum. Counting groups, drawing borders, and displaying a past taught colonial subjects, and their rulers, to see the territory as a bounded, classifiable community. That chapter is a development of the 1983 argument rather than its replacement. The core remains print, pilgrimage, and the creole sequence.

Why people die for it

Anderson ends on a question that theories of interest have trouble with. Nations inspire sacrifice. People die for a community of strangers, and they often do so with a language of love, kinship, and destiny rather than with a language of profit. He thinks this is a clue to what kind of thing the nation is. It is closer, in the feelings it can command, to religion and family than to a liberal contract. That does not make it ancient. It means a modern form has captured an old capacity for attachment. The book’s title is an answer to the puzzle. What people die for is an imagined communion, limited and sovereign, made plausible by the daily evidence of print and by the administrative frames in which lives are actually led.

Limits

The criticisms are now part of how the book should be read. Scholars of ethnicity, Anthony Smith chief among them, argue that Anderson underplays the ethnic ties, memories, and symbols that long predate print, and that national movements usually attach themselves to a people who already had a name. Ernest Gellner, publishing his own theory in the same year, rooted nationalism in industrial society’s need for a standardised high culture and a national education system, and was less persuaded that the Americas were the pioneer case. Historians of particular countries have found nations, or something very like them, in places Anderson’s timetable treats as too early, and have found states, churches, and armies doing work he assigns to newspapers. The theory is a theory of cultural form. It is thinner on war, on institutions, and on the coercion that turns a reading public into a citizenry.

There is also a limit inside the print argument itself. Literacy was restricted for centuries after Gutenberg, and the audiences Anderson needs were often small elites. A nation imagined by creole officials and vernacular publicists still had to be taught to peasants, sometimes much later, by schools and conscription. Anderson gives the later, state-led phase a name, official nationalism, without really explaining popular belief in places where the press was thin. The book remains the most influential account of how strangers come to feel like a people. It is not a complete account of how that feeling is enforced.

Why it matters here

The idea in front of the reader is that nations are made, and that print is one of the machines that made them. Anderson is the source of the phrase and of the mechanism most often paired with it: print-capitalism, the novel and the newspaper, and the creole path that complicates a Europe-first story. Gellner’s Nations and Nationalism, from the same year, is the structural companion, tying the nation to industry and schooling rather than to the forms of reading. Hobsbawm and Ranger’s The Invention of Tradition is the companion on ritual, the kilts and jubilees and colonial durbars that make a new community feel old. Elizabeth Eisenstein’s history of the press supplies the earlier technological condition, the standardisation and dissemination without which Anderson’s printers would have had nothing to sell. Here, Imagined Communities is the book that turns those neighbouring arguments into a single definition: a nation is a community of people who will never meet, taught by print to imagine that they share a time, a language, and a state.
