Two characters, and the warning that they are characters

Daniel Kahneman’s Thinking, Fast and Slow, published in 2011, is a long book that puts a lifetime of work, much of it done with Amos Tversky, into a single frame simple enough to remember and, as Kahneman himself insists, too simple to be literally true. The frame is two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic, associative, and always on. It recognises a face, completes a familiar phrase, reads anger in a voice, and produces an impression of a problem before the problem has been stated in numbers. System 2 is slow, effortful, and associated with the feeling of choosing and of paying attention. It is what you engage to multiply awkward quantities, to compare a policy with a principle, or to check a figure on a form. The book’s central claim is not that people are divided into the impulsive and the thoughtful. It is that System 1 proposes and System 2 usually disposes in System 1’s favour. The slow system is lazy. It endorses the quick impression unless something, difficulty, an obvious contradiction, a trained habit of checking, wakes it up.

Kahneman is unusually direct, for a book this popular, about the status of the two names. They are useful fictions, characters in a story, not little people in the head and not anatomical regions one could cut out. The justification he offers is practical. A great many findings about judgement become easier to state, and easier to watch for in oneself, if one is allowed to say that a fast process produced an answer and a slow process failed to inspect it. Researchers who want a more careful psychology of dual processes, Jonathan Evans and Keith Stanovich among them, have spent years saying what this shorthand flattens. The shorthand was a choice Kahneman made so that readers who will never open a journal could carry the findings. The book stands or falls on whether the findings, rather than the characters, survive.

What System 1 is doing

The early chapters catalogue the fast system’s ordinary work, because Kahneman wants the later catalogue of errors to sit on top of a system that is, most of the time, a triumph. Jumping to conclusions is not a defect added to an otherwise careful mind. It is how perception works. You do not deduce the mood of a face. You see it. The same machinery, pointed at a question of probability or value, sees an answer there too. He describes a principle he calls what you see is all there is. The mind builds the best story it can from the information present, and it does not pause to ask what information is absent. Coherence, not completeness, is what produces the feeling of confidence. A good story with thin evidence feels truer than a hedged story with better evidence, because the hedge is a System 2 addition and the coherence arrived on its own.

Substitution is the mechanism he uses to connect this picture to the classic heuristics. When a question is hard, System 1 answers an easier one and offers the answer as if it were a response to the hard one. Asked whether a project will succeed, one answers whether the people in the room looked competent. Asked how frequent a kind of disaster is, one answers how easily an example comes to mind. The heuristics Tversky and Kahneman described in the 1970s, representativeness, availability, and anchoring and adjustment, appear in this book as species of substitution and of the coherence-seeking that follows it. Anchoring gets a full treatment: a number in the environment, even an irrelevant one, becomes the starting point, and adjustment from it is incomplete. The book retells the demonstrations without pretending they are new. Its contribution is the organisation. Errors that had looked like a list of curiosities become the predictable output of a fast system doing what it is for.

Attention, effort, and the famous traps

System 2’s laziness is illustrated with problems that almost everyone can feel failing. The one Folio’s card uses is the bat and ball, from Shane Frederick’s Cognitive Reflection Test. A bat and a ball cost 1.10, and the bat costs 1.00 more than the ball. The price of the ball is five cents, not ten. Ten cents is the answer System 1 offers, fluent and wrong, and it remains the answer of anyone who never spends the extra moment to check. Frederick’s other items work the same way, and Kahneman uses the test as evidence that the disposition to check is unevenly distributed and consequential. People who override the fluent answer behave differently, in his and Frederick’s evidence, on questions of time and risk. The moral is not that the slow are virtuous. It is that the fast answer has a large head start, and that institutions which require the check will outperform institutions which rely on people to feel like checking.

He is equally interested in the conditions that make System 2 even lazier than usual. Effort is a resource. When attention is occupied, depleted, or simply not engaged, the fast answer passes. Some of the particular studies he leans on for the depletion of willpower have not held up well in later replication, and a reader should not treat every experimental vignette in these chapters as settled. The broader observation, that attention is limited and that concurrent demands make careful judgement less likely, is ordinary experience and does not depend on the contested studies. Kahneman’s practical advice follows from the broad observation. Decisions that matter should be made when attention is available, and they should be made with procedures, checklists, outside views, reference classes, that do not require a tired person to be a hero of System 2 at the crucial moment.

Choices, frames, and the two selves

The middle of the book retells prospect theory, the work for which the Nobel Prize was in large part awarded, in the language of the two systems. People evaluate outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point, not as final states of wealth. Losses loom larger than the equivalent gains. People are risk-averse when they think they are choosing between gains, and risk-seeking when they think they are choosing between losses. Probabilities are not used as the textbooks say, especially at the extremes. The Asian disease problem, which Folio’s framing card describes, is the demonstration he gives a general reader: the same numerical outcomes, offered once in the language of lives saved and once in the language of deaths, produce opposite preferences, because one language puts the chooser in the domain of gains and the other in the domain of losses. Framing effects are, in the book’s terms, System 1 responses to wording, endorsed by a System 2 that did not notice the wording was doing the work.

The last part changes the subject from choice to the experience of life over time, and it is the part of the book most purely Kahneman’s. He distinguishes an experiencing self, which lives a moment, from a remembering self, which keeps a story about the moments. The story obeys a peak-end rule. It weights the most intense moment and the ending, and it is largely indifferent to duration. A painful episode that ends slightly less badly can be remembered as better than a shorter episode that ends at its worst, even though the experiencing self suffered more in total. Kahneman treats this as a deep awkwardness for any policy, medical, legal, or personal, that aims at welfare, because the welfare one remembers and the welfare one lived are not the same quantity, and the remembered one is what people use when they decide what to do again. The two systems are still in the background. The story is a System 1 construction. The experiencing self had no vote when the story was edited.

What he later withdrew, and what he did not

The book’s most important weakness is one Kahneman has named himself. In the chapter on priming he put considerable weight on striking studies, including studies in which subtle cues changed behaviour outside the laboratory, that were underpowered and that have largely failed to replicate. In 2017 he said publicly that he had placed too much faith in that evidence. The failure matters because the chapter had made priming seem like a routine demonstration of System 1’s openness to the environment. Some of that openness is real and well established, in perception and in judgement. The theatrical versions, in which a few words on a page change how fast someone walks, should not be treated as part of the book’s solid core. A careful reader can separate the core from the chapter. Anchoring, framing, prospect theory’s main patterns, availability, and the basic laziness of effortful checking are not the priming results, and they have been far more durable.

The other weakness is the characters themselves. Evans and Stanovich’s review of dual-process theory is the right companion precisely because it refuses the casual list of opposites, fast against slow, emotional against rational, ancient against modern, that a popular book almost has to use. Their point, that the defensible distinction is between autonomous processes and processes that require working memory, and that both kinds can be right or wrong, is a correction to the way Thinking, Fast and Slow will be remembered if it is remembered only as a slogan. Kahneman already conceded the slogan’s limits. The correction is still worth reading, because the slogan is what most readers will repeat.

Why it matters here

This book is the source of Folio’s card on two systems, and it is also one of the works behind the card on framing. The two-systems card takes from it the definitions of the fast and slow characters, the laziness of the second, the bat-and-ball problem, and Kahneman’s own warning that the characters are fictions. It points onward to Frederick for the test itself, to Tversky and Kahneman’s 1974 paper for the heuristics the book is popularising, and to Evans and Stanovich for the argument about whether two systems is the right scientific formulation.
