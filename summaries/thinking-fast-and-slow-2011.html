In Thinking, Fast and Slow, Daniel Kahneman opens with a puzzle. A bat and a ball cost $1.10. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? The number that jumps out is 10 cents. That answer is wrong. A 10-cent ball and a bat priced a dollar higher would total $1.20. The ball costs 5 cents and the bat costs $1.05. Kahneman's point is that the 10-cent answer arrives on its own, and that checking it takes a second, slower step that many people never start.

The measurements behind that chapter come from Shane Frederick, who gave three puzzles of this kind to 3,428 people. The average score was 1.24 out of 3. At MIT, 61 students averaged 2.18, and 48 percent got all three right. At the University of Toledo, 138 students averaged 0.57, and 64 percent got none right. The puzzles are easy once someone shows the arithmetic. The miss is the skipped check.

A second case in the book is a disease expected to kill 600 people. One group read that a program would save 200 lives for sure, against a one-third chance of saving all 600. Of 152 people, 72 percent took the sure saving. Another group read the same programs as deaths: 400 would die for sure, or a one-third chance that nobody would die. Of 155 people, 78 percent took the gamble. Nothing in the outcomes had changed. "Saved" made people play it safe. "Die" made them gamble.

That split shows up in ordinary choices. A warranty, a medical consent form, or a sale price can be told as a gain you lock in or as a loss you might avoid, and the telling moves the choice. The bat-and-ball slip is the same habit in smaller clothes. A first answer feels like a fact, so the slow check does not start, whether the question is a price, a hire, or a dose.

Kahneman wrote that more than half the students at Harvard, MIT, and Princeton gave the 10-cent answer. Frederick, whose test that sentence relies on, later said the miss rate on that one item was 41 percent at MIT, 48 percent at Princeton, and 51 percent at Harvard. The rough claim survives. The "more than half at each of those three" line does not. The disease problem has been checked more kindly. In 2014 a 36-sample project led by Richard Klein still found the gain-versus-loss shift, with a weighted effect of d = 0.60 on 6,271 people, smaller than the original classroom result but present in 86 percent of the samples.
