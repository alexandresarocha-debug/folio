The problem Hirsch thinks the humanities have

E. D. Hirsch's Validity in Interpretation, published in 1967, is a defence of a standard. The standard is the author's verbal intention, and the defence is written against a situation in which Hirsch thinks interpretation has lost any way of being wrong. If a text means what a reader feels, or what a present community needs it to mean, or what a method can make it say, then disagreement is only a difference of use, and the idea of a misreading has no object. Hirsch regards that situation as fatal to the humanities as a form of knowledge. A discipline that cannot say, in principle, whether an interpretation is valid can still be lively, inventive and morally serious. It cannot claim to have understood a text better or worse. The book is his attempt to restore the claim. It is unfashionable in the precise sense that the same years produced Barthes's announcement of the author's death, and Hirsch is arguing that the author, as an intending speaker, is the only thing that can stop meaning from sliding into use.

Meaning and significance

The distinction on which the book turns is the distinction between meaning and significance. Meaning is what the author meant, the verbal intention, and it is stable. It is what it was, even if nobody currently cares about it, and even if everybody currently reads it through a preoccupation the author could not have had. Significance is the relation of that meaning to something else, a reader's life, a later historical moment, a political use, a value. Significance changes. It should change. A text that mattered in one way to its first audience can matter in another way now, and Hirsch has no wish to freeze those relations. His wish is to stop critics from calling the change in significance a change in meaning. When someone says that a book means something new in every generation, Hirsch replies that its significance is new and its meaning is not. The reply is the whole method. Collapse the two words, and the text becomes a mirror that cannot be misread. Keep them apart, and a reader may draw a new significance only after, or while, trying to get the meaning right.

The distinction also organises his attitude to history. Historical change is real, and it affects significance constantly. It does not, in his view, reach back and alter what a sentence meant when it was uttered. To think that it does is to confuse the history of reading with the history of the utterance. A later event can make a sentence poignant in a way the author could not have intended. The poignancy is a significance. The sentence's meaning remains the intention that governed it, and a reader who reports the poignancy as if it were the original meaning has made a particular, describable mistake. Hirsch likes mistakes, theoretically. A theory that cannot describe a mistake cannot describe a success either. Meaning versus significance is his way of saying what the mistake of anachronism is, without saying that anachronistic significance is worthless. People may use texts. They should know when they are using them and when they are understanding them.

Intention without mind-reading

The obvious objection is that authors' intentions are hidden, and that a theory which makes them the standard has replaced a public text with a private ghost. Hirsch's reply takes the objection seriously and relocates intention. The intention he means is verbal intention, the intention to mean something by words, not the secret plan to achieve fame or the unpublished motive that a biography might uncover. Verbal intention is the kind of intention we reconstruct whenever we understand anyone, in a conversation or in a letter. We do it by hypothesis. We guess what the person was doing with the words, we test the guess against the rest of the words, against the genre, against the occasion, and we revise. The process is public in the only sense a process of understanding can be public. It uses evidence. It can be argued about. It can fail. Hirsch calls the structure of the process a hermeneutic circle, a movement between a hypothesis about the whole meaning and the parts that confirm or unsettle it. Nothing in the circle requires occult access to a mind. It requires the willingness to treat the author as a person who meant one thing rather than another, and to be wrong about which.

This is his answer to the intentional fallacy as Wimsatt and Beardsley had formulated it in 1946. They held that a poem's meaning should be judged from the poem, that the author's intention is neither available nor a standard, and that the poem belongs to the language. Hirsch agrees that a private, unwitnessed wish is not the standard, and that a poem which fails to carry out a wish has not, by the wish, succeeded. He thinks they were wrong to conclude that intention, in the verbal sense, is dispensable. Language alone, he argues, is too ambiguous to determine meaning. Genre, context, and the author's horizon of beliefs are evidence for a reconstruction that readers are already performing whenever they so much as decide that a sentence is ironic. The New Critical hope of a self-sufficient text understates the inferences that reading requires. Hirsch wants those inferences owned, disciplined, and aimed at the author's meaning, rather than denied in theory and smuggled back in practice.

Validity as a probable conclusion

Because the reconstruction is a hypothesis, validity for Hirsch is not certainty. An interpretation is more or less probable, given the evidence, and the ethic of the interpreter is to submit the hypothesis to the text and to whatever historical knowledge bears on what the author could have meant. Bias is not a scandal in this ethic. Bias is the starting condition of a person, and the discipline is the willingness to let the evidence beat the bias. A theory that begins by declaring the author's meaning unknowable has, in his view, excused itself from the discipline and called the excuse sophistication. He is particularly stern with approaches that treat the text as an occasion for a present project, however admirable the project, and that offer no account of the difference between getting the text wrong and disagreeing with it. Disagreement with an author is a right. Misdescription of what the author said is a different act, and a scholarly community ought to be able to tell them apart.

The practical picture is more modest than the polemical heat suggests. Hirsch does not think every text has a meaning so sharp that two careful readers must converge on a paraphrase. He thinks that the aim of convergence is what makes an argument about a text an argument rather than a swap of impressions, and that many texts are clear enough for the aim to bite. Where the evidence runs out, the honest conclusion is uncertainty about the meaning, not a declaration that meaning is whatever we now say. Uncertainty of that kind is still governed by the standard. It says that we do not know what the author meant, which is a statement about evidence. It does not say that the author did not mean anything, which is a statement about the nature of texts. The difference is the difference between a hard case and a different metaphysics. Hirsch is content to have hard cases. He is not content to let hard cases set the metaphysics.

Where the standard is genuinely difficult

The difficulties are real, and a reading of Hirsch that does not grant them is propaganda. Authors revise, and the intention of a revised text is not a single moment. Some works are collaborative, and the intending subject is a composite that the theory has to reconstruct awkwardly. Some works are anonymous, and the author is a placeholder for a tradition. Some texts, by the design of their authors, court an indeterminacy that makes several readings equally loyal to the intention, which is then an intention to leave a structured openness rather than an intention to convey one sense. Later theory adds a further pressure. If, as Barthes argues, a text is a tissue of quotations whose combinations exceed any person's plan, then intention explains less of what a careful reading finds than Hirsch needs it to explain. If, as Foucault argues, the author is an institutional function, then the intending mind Hirsch treats as the standard is already, in part, a role constructed by editors and by law. These objections do not make the meaning-significance distinction useless. They limit the field in which a single verbal intention is the right object of reconstruction.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the death of the author cites Validity in Interpretation as the defence that has to be heard in the same breath as Barthes. The card's narrative is that Barthes removed the Author as the owner of a single meaning, that Foucault redescribed the author as a function, and that Hirsch defended authorial intention as the standard of a valid interpretation. The card's deep dive adds Wimsatt and Beardsley's intentional fallacy as the earlier American argument, different from Barthes, that a poem should be judged from the text rather than from a plan. Hirsch is answering that argument as well as answering the coming post-structuralist one. His distinction between meaning and significance is the tool the card's middle position quietly uses, even when it does not use his name. Most readers, the card says, now hold a middle position, unwilling to treat biography as the only key and unwilling to treat intention as nothing. Read Hirsch for the strongest version of the second unwillingness. The book says what a valid interpretation would be, and why a culture that can no longer ask the question has confused the freedom to use a text with the discipline of understanding one.
