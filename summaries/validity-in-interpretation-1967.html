E. D. Hirsch Jr., in Validity in Interpretation (1967), draws a line he thinks most arguments about books smudge. "Meaning is that which is represented by a text; it is what the author meant by his use of a particular sign sequence; it is what the signs represent." Significance is something else. It "names a relationship between that meaning and a person, or a conception, or a situation, or indeed anything imaginable." Meaning, on this account, is fixed by what the writer willed when the words were chosen. Significance can widen forever. A poem can come to matter to a later war, a later law, a later grief, without the poem's meaning having changed. What changed is the relation between that stable meaning and a new situation. Hirsch thinks that if you collapse the two, you have no way to say that one reading is right and another is wrong. Any response would count as the meaning. Validity needs a norm, and the only norm he will accept is the author's meaning, shared and repeatable, the sort of thing you can put in other words and still be talking about the same intention.

He is answering a position set out twenty-one years earlier by W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley. In "The Intentional Fallacy" (1946) they wrote that "the design or intention of the author is neither available nor desirable as a standard for judging the success of a work of literary art." The poem, they said, is what you have, not the plan in the poet's head, and not the poet's later account of the plan. Hirsch grants that you cannot open the skull. He denies that this makes intention useless. Understanding, for him, is already an inference to what someone meant, checked against the words and against the kind of utterance it is. A joke, a statute, a lab note, and a lyric ask for different inferences. They do not ask you to ignore the asker. He also separates interpretation from criticism. Interpretation is the attempt to say what the meaning is. Criticism is what you do with it, the judgment, the use, the significance.

The distinction is easy to use and easy to fake. When a friend says "I'm fine," you already treat the words as somebody's meaning, and you already let the significance shift if you learn they have been in a waiting room for six hours. Courts do a formal version of the same split when they argue about what a clause was for, and then argue about what to do with it now. The weak point is the one Wimsatt and Beardsley named. Often the only evidence of the will is the text you are trying to interpret, plus context you are also interpreting. Hirsch gives you a reason not to treat every clever reading as equally good. He does not hand you a method that recovers the will without a fight over the words. Use "meaning" for what you claim the writer did. Use "significance" for what you are doing with it. Say which one you are arguing about before you announce that the book has changed.
