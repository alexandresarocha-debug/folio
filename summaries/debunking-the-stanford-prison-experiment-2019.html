Thibault Le Texier went to the archives. In a 2019 paper in American Psychologist he set Philip Zimbardo’s published accounts of the Stanford Prison Experiment beside the records Zimbardo had given to Stanford in 2011, sixteen boxes, plus a box at the University of Akron: hundreds of pages, six hours of film, and forty-eight hours of audio from before, during, and just after the study. Between May and December 2017 he also interviewed fifteen of the men who had been in the basement in August 1971. The question was simple. Did the archives contradict the story that ordinary students, assigned at random, became cruel guards on their own?

They did. On Saturday, August 14, 1971, orientation day, Zimbardo told the men who would play guards that he had a grant to study mob behavior, violence, loss of identity, and anonymity, and that the point of the mock prison was to recreate that psychological environment. A blackboard listed the states he wanted: boredom, frustration, fear, arbitrariness, loss of privacy, loss of freedom, loss of individuality, powerlessness. On the tape he tells them, “We can create boredom. We can create a sense of frustration. We can create fear in them, to some degree.” He tells them the prisoners will have no privacy, will be called by number, and will be able to do nothing the staff does not permit. David Jaffe, an undergraduate who had already run a prison simulation for a class in Toyon Hall, and who served as “warden,” then coached the counts. He described lining men against a wall, stretching a count out for about forty minutes, and using sarcasm. He said the count was a good time for guards to humiliate.

The rule sheet was not invented on the spot either. Of the seventeen rules used in the study, eleven were copied from Jaffe’s class project. Zimbardo also handed the guards a suggested daily schedule taken from that earlier simulation, including middle-of-the-night counts. Jaffe’s notebook for August 17, 18, and 19 notes that the schedule was followed closely. Guards later said the warden or Zimbardo had directed them to act in specific ways. The cruelty had a script, a rehearsal, and a supervisor.

That matters when someone treats a training exercise, a hazing, or a “see what happens” management game as proof of what people will do if left alone. If the people in charge have already announced the outcome they want, and have shown the staff how to produce it, the harsh behavior is evidence about instruction and permission. It is weak evidence about a role that swallows a person whole. Le Texier’s count of the record is the caution: read the orientation tape before you trust the legend.
