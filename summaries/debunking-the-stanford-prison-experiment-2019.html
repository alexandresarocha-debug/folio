Background

In August 1971 Philip Zimbardo turned a corridor in the basement of the Stanford psychology department into a mock prison. About two dozen male student volunteers were randomly assigned to be guards or prisoners. The plan was to run for two weeks. The study was halted after six days, with guards behaving abusively and several prisoners in acute distress. For decades the episode was taught as proof that ordinary people, given a powerful role, will spontaneously become cruel. The situation, on that telling, explains the behaviour, and personality is beside the point. Zimbardo carried the lesson into his account of the abuses at Abu Ghraib in The Lucifer Effect. Critics had doubted the study for a long time. It had not entered the literature as a fully reported experiment in a leading psychology journal, and the public version, the film and the lectures, did most of the teaching.

In 2018 the French researcher Thibault Le Texier published a book based on the Stanford archives, recordings and documents. The 2019 article in American Psychologist is the English-language reckoning that followed. Folio's card on the Stanford Prison Experiment takes that article as the reason to change the lesson. The walkthrough here is of what Le Texier did with the archive, and of the inference he says no longer stands.

The question

The famous inference depends on a picture of the method. The experimenters are supposed to have assigned roles, set a scene, and then stood back while the roles did the work. If that picture is right, the cruelty is evidence about what situations do to people who were not pushed. Le Texier asks whether the archive matches the picture. What were the guards told before the prisoners arrived? What did the staff do when the guards were mild? Who designed the degradations that look, in the retelling, like spontaneous inventions? And what did participants later say they thought they were doing?

These are historical questions with a psychological point. Demand characteristics are the clues, built into a procedure, that tell a participant what the researcher hopes to see. Leadership is the influence of people who define what a group is for. If the archive is full of both, the study is still a record of harsh behaviour and real distress. It is a poor demonstration that role assignment alone manufactures cruelty. That is the distinction the card asks a reader to keep.

What he examined

Le Texier went to the materials that the textbook summary had smoothed. There are recordings of the orientation given to the guards, notes and memos from the staff, and later testimony from people who had been in the basement. The orientation matters because it is the moment when a role is either left empty or filled in. On the archival account, the guards were not handed a blank uniform and told to do as they liked within a ban on physical violence. They were briefed on the kind of psychological atmosphere the study was interested in producing. Frustration, boredom, arbitrariness and a sense of powerlessness on the prisoners' side were described as aims. When, in the early days, some guards were too soft for those aims, staff pushed. An undergraduate acting as warden pressed guards to be tougher. Zimbardo was not a neutral observer standing outside the simulation. He took the role of prison superintendent, and the superintendent's authority and the principal investigator's authority were the same person.

The elements that later looked like discoveries by the guards were, in large part, designed. Smocks, stocking caps, numbers, ankle chains, senseless rules and the general stripping of individuality were features of the prison as the staff built it, drawing on advice that included a consultant who had been a prisoner in real life. A degradation that is in the protocol is not evidence that ordinary young men invent degradation the moment they are called guards. Le Texier also collects the participants' own later descriptions. Some, including men whose conduct had been among the harshest, said they had been performing. They had understood what the study wanted, and they had supplied it, sometimes with an eye on the camera. One of the most cited emotional collapses was later described by the man who had it as, at least in part, a performance aimed at getting out. Testimony of that kind is not a stopwatch. It is evidence about how the participants understood the situation while they were in it, which is the evidence a demand-characteristics account needs.

What he concludes

Le Texier's conclusion is not that nothing ugly happened. The recordings contain abuse, and prisoners were distressed enough that the study was stopped. His conclusion is about the cause that can be inferred. The guards varied. They were not a uniform mass transformed by a role. The harshest behaviour was encouraged, and it was then selected, in films and lectures, as the meaning of the study. The experimenters were authors of the events they later reported as findings. Once that is granted, the six days are evidence about what people do when an authority they have reason to trust defines toughness as the point of the exercise, watches them, and urges them on if they lag. That is a real and familiar phenomenon. It is leadership and demand. It is a different phenomenon from the one the lectures named, in which the role would have done the work alone.

He also places the study in Zimbardo's existing programme. Zimbardo had already been writing about deindividuation, anonymity and aggression. The prison was a way to stage a view he held, with students, a consultant and a camera, more than it was an open test that could have come out either way. A demonstration run by a believer, edited into a film, and never forced through the ordinary machinery of a full journal report, gained the authority of a classic experiment. Le Texier asks the reader to put it back in the category the documents support. It is a historically important piece of scientific theatre, and a poor piece of evidence for a general law about roles.

Limits

An archival argument is an interpretation of incomplete materials. Tone on a tape can be heard in more than one way. Later interviews are given years after the event, when the men know what the study became and have an interest in how they appear. A participant who says he was acting may be telling the truth, or protecting himself, or both. Le Texier is open to the charge that he emphasises the documents that indict the official story. The right reply is the one the card already models: read him against Zimbardo's defence, which exists, including on Zimbardo's own site, and against the BBC prison study, which asked a related question with a different design. The archive is strong enough to move the lesson. It is not a substitute for a new experiment, and Le Texier did not run one.

There is a limit on what even a successful debunking removes. People in uniforms, with permission and an audience, did treat other people badly. Anyone who wants a situational account of cruelty can still look for it in better evidence. What has to be retired is the specific claim that this basement isolated the effect of a role and found the effect to be spontaneous tyranny. Keeping that claim requires explaining away the briefing, the warden's pressure and the dual role of the superintendent. Those are a lot of things to explain away.

Why it matters here

Folio's card is called The Stanford Prison Experiment, revisited, and the caution under it is blunt. Guards were coached, participants felt a demand to perform, and the classic lesson is unsupported in the form in which it was taught. Le Texier's article is the source the card relies on for that caution. Reading it as a walkthrough, rather than as a slogan, keeps the distress in the story and takes the mechanism out. The situation was powerful partly because it was full of instructions. A bare corridor and a coin flip do not describe what happened.

The card pairs this paper with Haslam and Reicher's BBC prison study, in which guards were not coached into toughness and did not become a tyrannical unit, and with Zimbardo's own book, which states the situational case at full length. Le Texier supplies the reason those two readings cannot be given equal weight as accounts of 1971. The book is the argument the archive complicates. The BBC study is a later simulation built without the coaching, and it does not reproduce the legend. Engaged followership, the idea that people do harm when they identify with a leader and a cause, fits the tapes better than a theory of automatic role possession. That is the revised lesson the card wants available when the famous study comes up.
