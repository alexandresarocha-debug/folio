A common objection to twin studies is that twins are special. Identical twins may be treated more alike, and spouses often pick partners with similar views, which can distort the sums. This 2010 study tried to answer critics by adding the rest of the family.

The data came from the same Virginia 30K survey used in 2005, but this time almost the whole sample: about 30,000 adults aged 18 to 84. There were 14,781 twins, 4,391 spouses, 2,360 parents, 4,800 offspring and 3,184 non-twin brothers and sisters of the twins. They answered the same 28 issue items in 1986, and about two years later a subset of 1,019 men and 2,912 women aged 50 and over answered them again.

Including ordinary siblings matters because they share the same proportion of genes as non-identical twins, but not the twin experience. Comparing the two shows how much of twins' similarity comes from simply being twins. Parents let the model estimate direct cultural transmission, and the repeat survey let the team separate real attitudes from measurement error.

The results did not shrink the genetic part. For most attitudes the additive genetic share fell between 0.3 and 0.7. School prayer and X-rated movies were among the most heritable, at roughly two-thirds of the differences. Attitudes to living together before marriage showed a big sex difference: 0.40 in men and 0.84 in women. Shared environment was usually below 0.10, and direct cultural inheritance from parents was never above 0.11 and usually under 0.05. Party identification was again the exception, with family influences much stronger.

What this means in real life: parents may pass on their party, but this study suggests they pass on views on hot-button issues far more through genes than through teaching.

Caution: the study uses one American sample surveyed in the 1980s, the models make their own assumptions, and the authors themselves found some extra similarity linked to being a twin. Critics who reject the twin method in principle were not persuaded, and the debate continues.
