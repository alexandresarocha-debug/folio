The prediction the trial is built to test

Memories of colonoscopy, published in 2003 by Donald Redelmeier, Joel Katz and Daniel Kahneman, is the intervention that the earlier pain papers make conceivable. If a remembered episode is governed by its worst moment and its ending, and if duration is largely neglected, then a clinician can change the memory by changing the ending. The change need not reduce the total amount of pain. It can even increase the total, provided the added time is milder than what came before and leaves the procedure finishing at a lower intensity. The 1993 cold-water study had produced exactly that pattern under laboratory control. The 1996 study had found that real patients' memories of colonoscopy and lithotripsy already tracked peak and end rather than duration. The remaining question was causal. If you assign the better ending, rather than merely observing it, do the memories and the later intentions move?

Colonoscopy is a serious test of the idea, not a convenient metaphor for it. The examination matters to patients' health, it is widely disliked, and the decision to return for a repeat is a real decision with a clinical stake. A theory of memory that cannot survive this setting is a smaller theory than its authors hoped. A theory that can survive it becomes a claim about how aversive medical experiences should be finished, not only about how they are described in journals of psychology. The trial is randomised so that the ending is not confounded with the sort of patient who happens to have an easy examination, which was the gap the 1996 evidence could not close.

What was added at the end

Patients undergoing colonoscopy were assigned either to the usual examination or to a modified one. In the modified procedure the clinician added a short period at the end during which the instrument was still in place but the examination was less uncomfortable than the examination proper. The addition made the procedure longer. It did not claim to make the intrusive part of the examination gentler, and it did not remove the peak. Its job was to give the episode a milder final interval, so that the last moments stored in memory would be easier than the last moments of a procedure that stopped at the point of maximum clinical business.

The logic is identical to the logic of the extra thirty seconds of slightly warmer water. There, the added interval was still painful and the total pain rose, but the ending improved and the longer trial was preferred. Here, the added interval is a piece of clinical time that would ordinarily not be spent, and the hoped-for gain is not a preference expressed in a laboratory but a memory of a procedure and a willingness to undergo it again. Real-time experience and retrospective memory are both in play. The trial is a success for the peak-end account if the modified group remembers the colonoscopy more kindly, and if that kinder memory shows up in the practical question of return, even though those patients were in the procedure room for longer.

What the randomisation found

The added ending worked in the way the theory said it should. Patients who received the short, less uncomfortable final period remembered the procedure more favourably than patients who received the standard ending. They were more willing to come back. The procedure they had actually undergone was not shorter, and the modification was not a newly invented analgesic for the worst moment. The difference that predicted the difference in memory was the difference at the end. Duration, which from the patient's point of view had moved in the unwelcome direction, did not govern the retrospective judgement. The remembering patient and the experiencing patient were again keeping different books.

This is the step that turns a descriptive rule into an action. The 1996 paper could say that, among the endings that happen to occur, better endings go with better memories. A sceptic could reply that easier endings happen to easier patients. Randomisation answers that reply. The ending was allocated. The memory moved with the allocation. Willingness to return moved with the memory. For a screening test whose benefit depends on people agreeing to have it again when guidelines say they should, willingness to return is not a sentimental extra. It is one of the outcomes that makes the procedure worth offering. A modification that lengthens the examination and improves that willingness is, on the peak-end account, a rational modification rather than a paradoxical one.

How the three papers form one argument

It helps to see the 2003 trial as the third movement of a single claim, not as a free-standing tip about bedside manner. First, in the basin, more pain was preferred to less when the extra pain improved the ending, and the preference was predicted by peak and end rather than by the integral of discomfort. Second, in ordinary clinical records, patients' memories of two real procedures showed the same weighting. Third, when the ending of colonoscopy was improved by design, the memory and the intention to return improved with it. At each step the authors allow the adding-up view to make a prediction, and at each step the prediction fails in the same direction. People do not store the episode as a sum. They store a sketch dominated by the worst moment and the last one, and they act on the sketch.

The clinical moral is easy to coarsen, so it is worth stating at the size the evidence supports. Endings deserve attention because memory gives them weight that the clock does not. A bad final minute can spoil the memory of an examination that was otherwise tolerable. A deliberately gentler final minute can improve the memory of an examination that was long. None of this says that the peak is unimportant. The rule is peak and end, and a trial that left a savage peak untouched would not, on the theory, erase that peak from memory. None of it says that the experiencing minutes do not matter ethically. They matter to the person who is living them, whether or not the later summary honours them. The argument is about the summary that governs the return visit.

What a single trial cannot underwrite

A randomised trial in one clinical context is strong evidence about that context and weaker evidence about every other aversive experience. Colonoscopy has a natural peak, a natural end, and a culturally familiar dread. A modification that helps here may be impractical, or ineffective, for a procedure whose worst moment is the end and cannot be followed by a milder interval, or for a pain that is chronic and therefore has no ending to edit. The peak-end rule has always been a claim about episodes. Where life does not divide into episodes, the rule has nothing to grip.

Later commentary has also kept the rule in proportion. Fredrickson's review of the work argued that peak-end weighting fits some experiences better than others, and that duration is not always ignored. The 2003 trial is a case where the fit was good enough to move a clinically relevant intention. It is not a mandate to lengthen every unpleasant hour on the grounds that memory is foolish. The honest reading is narrower. When an episode will be judged by its peak and its end, spending a little extra time to improve the end can be a kindness to the remembering self, and the remembering self is the one who decides whether to come back. The experiencing self still has to live the extra time. The trial's wager, which the results support, is that the extra time can be mild enough to be a price worth charging for a memory that does not frighten the next decision away.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The peak-end rule: memory edits your experiences, closes its clinical paragraph on this trial. Adding a short, less uncomfortable period at the end of a colonoscopy improved patients' memories and made them more willing to return. That sentence is the practical form of the 1993 paradox. In the laboratory, most people chose to repeat the longer cold-water trial. In the clinic, the longer examination, finished more gently, was the one patients were more willing to face again. The card's warning, taken from later reviews, belongs beside the trial: the rule is not universal, and duration is not always neglected. Held at that size, the three papers are one argument about how memory edits an episode, and about why the edit is large enough to matter in a room where people are in pain.
