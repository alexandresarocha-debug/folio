In 2003 Donald Redelmeier, Joel Katz, and Daniel Kahneman tested whether a gentler ending could change the memory of a colonoscopy. Of 733 consecutive outpatients approached for a colonoscopy at a Toronto hospital, 682 agreed to take part. They were medically stable, mentally competent, and able to speak English. By random assignment, half had the usual procedure. The other half had a short addition at the end: the tip of the colonoscope rested in the rectum for up to three minutes, with no suction, no added air, and no extra anesthetic. The test was longer. The last moments were meant to hurt less.

Randomization left two similar groups. Pain at the end averaged 1.7 on a ten-point scale after the extended procedure and 2.5 after the standard one. Asked about the whole experience, the extended group rated discomfort at 4.4 on a 10-centimeter scale, against 4.9. Asked to rank the colonoscopy among other unpleasant events, with 8 as the worst, they ranked it 4.1 against 4.6. The researchers then used Ontario billing records to see who came back. Over a median follow-up of 5.3 years, 50.4% of patients had another colonoscopy. The odds of returning were higher after the longer procedure, an odds ratio of 1.41, once prior colonoscopy, the reason for the test, and abnormal findings were taken into account.

The added minutes made the procedure longer. They lightened the memory, and the memory is what a person consults when a letter says it is time to repeat the test. The effect on coming back was real and modest: an odds ratio of 1.41, with about half the patients returning either way. A clinic that can make the last minutes less painful, without adding risk, is changing the summary the patient takes home. A milder ending is a poor substitute for controlling the worst pain, and it does not erase the test. It is evidence that those last few minutes carry extra weight in a decision made years later, about a screening test people are already inclined to postpone.

Source. Redelmeier, D. A., Katz, J., and Kahneman, D. (2003). Memories of colonoscopy: A randomized trial. Pain, 104(1–2), 187–194. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0304-3959(03)00003-4
