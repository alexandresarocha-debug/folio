Despite its fame, the pen-in-mouth study had never been repeated directly. A Registered Replication Report, organised by Eric-Jan Wagenmakers and colleagues for the journal Perspectives on Psychological Science, set out to do that with a single protocol agreed in advance and run by many laboratories.

Seventeen independent labs repeated Study 1 of the original, with a combined 1,894 participants included in the analyses. The protocol, analysis plan and computer scripts were fixed before anyone saw the data. Participants again held a pen in their teeth or lips and rated Far Side cartoons. To reduce experimenter influence, instructions came from a video on a computer screen, and a video camera recorded participants so that pen position could be checked.

The original study had found a difference of 0.82 points between the smile and pout conditions on its rating scale. Pooling all 17 labs, the replication found a difference of just 0.03 points, with a 95% confidence interval from -0.11 to 0.16. All 17 lab-level Bayes factors from the default test favoured the hypothesis of no effect, 13 of them clearly.

The authors were careful about what this meant: their findings, they wrote, do not invalidate the more general facial feedback hypothesis. Afterwards it was argued that the replication was not fully direct, because it filmed participants, a change made on the advice of an expert reviewer, and awareness of being recorded might shift attention onto oneself.

What this means in real life: a single famous study, however elegant, can fail when many labs repeat it with a fixed plan, which is why large registered replications have become an important check in psychology.

Caution: a replication tests one procedure in particular conditions. The camera, the specific cartoons and participants' possible familiarity with the famous study may all matter, and whether video recording interferes with the effect is still debated.
