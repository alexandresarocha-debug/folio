Most early work on the genetics of politics came from just a few samples, mainly in Virginia, Minnesota and Australia. Peter Hatemi and a team of political scientists and geneticists set out to test whether the finding holds in other countries, decades and question formats.

They pooled more than 12,000 twin pairs from nine studies in five democracies: Australia, Denmark, Hungary, Sweden and the United States, collected between 1980 and 2011. Together the studies used 19 different measures of ideology, including liberal-conservative attitude scales, right-wing authoritarianism, economic egalitarianism, individualism versus collectivism, and freedom versus equality.

Genes mattered on every measure, in every country and period, with one exception. Combining all samples, the share of differences linked to genes was about 0.40. The shared family environment accounted for about 0.18 and each person's own experiences for about 0.42. The genetic share could be treated as the same across measures and countries without worsening the fit of the model. The environmental shares could not, which suggests the details of what people believe depend on their time and place.

The exception was a question that asked people to place themselves on the left or the right. In Hungary, differences on this question were almost entirely due to the shared environment, and in Sweden the genetic part was minimal. Yet in the same Hungarian survey, a liberal-conservative question gave a genetic share of about 0.39, and in Sweden similar questions gave 0.37 to 0.45. The words left and right seem to work as labels people learn, not as a summary of their values.

The team then searched DNA directly, in three samples with 11,388 people in total: 6,894 Australians from 1990, 1,160 Australians from 2008 and 3,334 Swedes from 2010. No single genetic variant reached genome-wide significance. The authors' conclusion is that ideology, like other complex traits, reflects thousands of variants with very small effects, and finding them will need far larger samples.

What this means in real life: there is no conservative gene to test for. Genes may tilt how people react to issues, but the party labels and slogans they adopt come from their surroundings.

Caution: these are still twin estimates, with the same assumptions as earlier work. The Hungarian sample was tiny, with 29 identical and 14 non-identical pairs, and the DNA scans were far too small to find small effects.
