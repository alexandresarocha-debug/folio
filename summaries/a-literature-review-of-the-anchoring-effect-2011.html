A review of a bias that refused to stay in the laboratory

Adrian Furnham and Hua Chu Boo’s 2011 paper, “A literature review of the anchoring effect”, is a survey of what the thirty-five years after Tversky and Kahneman’s 1974 Science article had done with one of that article’s three heuristics. Anchoring is the tendency of a numerical judgement to move towards a number that has been made salient, the anchor, including when the number is irrelevant to the quantity being judged. The original demonstration, a rigged wheel of fortune and a question about African countries in the United Nations, had been striking because the anchor was so obviously empty of information and the shift in estimates was so large. Furnham and Boo ask what a large subsequent literature had established about the conditions under which this happens, about the mechanisms that might produce it, and about whether expertise is any protection. Their paper is a narrative review. It does not pool every published effect into a single estimate, and it should not be quoted as if it had. Its value is the map: where anchoring has been found, which explanations are still alive, and which limitations a careful user of the finding ought to carry.

The map matters because anchoring had, by 2011, escaped psychology. It was being cited in law, in negotiation, in medicine, in valuation, and in ordinary advice about who should name a number first. A finding that travels that far accumulates loose versions of itself. The review is an attempt to say which versions the experiments actually support. The short form, that any number pulls any judgement, is stronger than the evidence. The longer form, that explicitly considered anchors shift numerical estimates in domain after domain, and that expertise reduces the shift less than one would hope, is the form they think the literature sustains.

Mechanisms that are still in contention

Furnham and Boo organise the theoretical half of the review around the explanations that had accumulated rather than around a single winner. The original explanation was insufficient adjustment. People start from the anchor and move, and they stop too soon, because adjustment is effortful and the starting point has a psychological privilege. This account fits the experience of estimating, and it predicts that anything which makes adjustment harder, time pressure, cognitive load, a low tolerance for effort, should leave people closer to the anchor. A second explanation, developed especially by Mussweiler and Strack, is selective accessibility. The standard experimental question asks whether the true value is higher or lower than the anchor. Answering it requires testing the hypothesis that the anchor is the true value, and testing a hypothesis makes anchor-consistent information easier to retrieve. The subsequent estimate is then built from a biased sample of what comes to mind. On this account the anchor does not work as a starting point one fails to leave. It works as a suggestion that changes the evidence one assembles.

Further accounts, including numerical priming and accounts in terms of attitude change, are given their place. A number can influence a judgement simply by having been recently seen, without a process of deliberate adjustment and without a comparative question. The review’s useful conclusion is not that one mechanism has won. It is that anchoring is probably more than one phenomenon. An incidental number glanced at on a page and a number a person has been forced to compare with a target may not move judgement by the same route, and a literature that uses one word for both will overstate the unity of the effect. Furnham and Boo are particularly attentive to the experimental paradigm. Many of the classic results use the two-step procedure, a comparative question followed by an absolute estimate. That procedure guarantees that the anchor is processed. It is the right procedure for studying considered anchors. It is a poor guide to the effect of a number that nobody has been asked to use, which later work has found to be smaller and less reliable.

Where the effect appears

The empirical half of the review walks through domains, and the walk is the reason a non-psychologist was expected to care. In general-knowledge questions, the setting of the original studies, anchors move estimates of quantities people only roughly know. In legal judgement, the sentences and damages that professionals recommend move with the numbers put in front of them, including numbers from a demand, a cap, or, in the striking studies, a source the professionals themselves know to be arbitrary. In negotiation and in the valuation of goods and houses, a first offer or a listing price shifts the counterparty’s sense of what is reasonable, which is why the practical literature treats the first number as an advantage worth taking. In forecasting and in purchasing, quantities and prices are similarly sticky to whatever benchmark was supplied. The review does not treat every published demonstration as equal. It notes that some paradigms, especially those involving subtle incidental primes, were already less secure than the textbook two-step effect, and subsequent replication trouble in parts of the priming literature has made that caution look wise.

Expertise receives a blunt summary, which is the summary Folio’s card repeats. Experts are generally susceptible, not immune. Real-estate agents, judges, auditors, and other people who make numerical judgements for a living show anchoring effects in the studies the review covers. Expertise helps when the task is one the expert can solve by retrieval or by a calculation that leaves no room for an anchor. It helps much less when the task is an estimate under uncertainty, which is most of the tasks in which anchoring was ever plausible. Experience can even increase confidence without decreasing bias, so that the expert is wrong and sure. Furnham and Boo do not conclude that training is pointless. They conclude that training which merely adds knowledge, without changing the procedure by which a number is produced, should not be expected to abolish the effect.

What moderates it

A review earns its keep in the moderators, and theirs is a practical list. The plausibility of the anchor matters, though not in the comforting way. Wildly implausible anchors can still move judgements, sometimes less than plausible ones, and the fact that they move anything is the fact that needs explaining, because a person who notices the absurdity has all the reason in the world to ignore it. Incentives and high stakes do not reliably make the effect disappear, which is awkward for any story in which anchoring is only laziness. Cognitive load and time pressure tend to increase reliance on the anchor where adjustment is the mechanism, and the review treats the evidence for this as suggestive rather than final. Knowledge of the topic reduces the effect when the knowledge is specific enough to answer the question, and does little when the knowledge is a general sense of being well informed. Mood, personality, and the source of the anchor are all discussed, with the repeated warning that many of these moderators rest on a small number of studies. The robust centre of the literature is the basic shift. The edges, who resists it and under exactly which instruction, were still soft in 2011 and have not all hardened since.

Limitations of the literature they surveyed

Furnham and Boo are frank about the shape of the evidence. A great many studies use students and hypothetical questions. The famous effects in courtrooms and markets are more persuasive precisely because they are less common and because the participants are the people whose resistance would matter. Publication has favoured clear demonstrations over studies of the boundary, so a review of what was published will over-represent success. Mechanism studies and application studies have often proceeded on separate tracks, which leaves open the possibility that the anchoring which moves a legal judgement is not the anchoring which a reaction-time study has explained. They also note that awareness is a weak defence. Telling people about the bias, or telling them that the anchor was random, has not, in the studies they cover, been a reliable way to eliminate it. A procedure is a better defence than a warning: generate an estimate before seeing the other side’s number, seek the outside view, and separate the person who receives a demand from the person who sets the figure, where an institution can arrange it.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on anchoring cites this review as the survey of decades of work across domains, and as the source of the claim that experts are susceptible rather than immune. The card’s other pillars are the 1974 paper that established the effect, the Many Labs replication that showed a standard anchoring result travelling well across laboratories, and the Englich, Mussweiler, and Strack experiments in which legal professionals were moved by anchors they knew were random, including dice they had thrown. Furnham and Boo are the map on which those pillars sit. They explain why the two-step laboratory effect and the courtroom effect can be discussed together, and why a glancing incidental number should not be given the same confidence. Read the review when the card’s practical advice, make the first offer, set your own number before you hear anyone else’s, needs the backing of a literature rather than of a single dramatic study. The literature, as they summarise it, is broad, mechanistically unsettled, and unkind to the hope that expertise alone is the cure.
