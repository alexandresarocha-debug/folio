Adrian Furnham and Hua Chu Boo's 2011 review collects experiments in which the first number people hear pulls the number they later give. One study they cite sent people to a real house in Tucson. Gregory Northcraft and Margaret Neale used a property appraised at $74,900 and changed only the listing price in an otherwise ordinary packet.

Forty-eight business students saw one of four listing prices. When the sheet said $65,900, their average guess of the appraised value was $63,571. When it said $83,900, the average guess was $72,196. Their suggested selling prices moved the same way, from $69,414 to $78,014. Agents were not immune. After two who had already seen the house were set aside, those given a listing of $65,900 put the appraisal at $67,811 on average. Those given $83,900 put it at $75,190. Only about a quarter of the agents named the listing price as a factor they had used.

The review sets that beside the older demonstration in which the first number is openly meaningless. Tversky and Kahneman spun a wheel that could stop only on 10 or 65, then asked what share of United Nations countries were in Africa. People who had seen 10 gave a median of 25 percent. People who had seen 65 gave a median of 45 percent. Furnham and Boo treat these as one family of results, and they argue it is not a single mechanism. Sometimes people start at the number and do not adjust far enough. Sometimes the number makes the facts that fit it easier to call up. Either path leaves the judgment near the number that arrived first.

In a negotiation, a shop, or a salary talk, that first figure does the listing price's job. An asking price, an opening bid, or a "was" price on a tag is in front of you before you have built your own number. Knowing the seller chose it, or even that it looks high, is not by itself protection. The Tucson agents had the house in front of them and years of sales behind them, and the sheet still moved the appraisal by thousands of dollars.

Source. Furnham, A., and Boo, H. C. (2011). A literature review of the anchoring effect. The Journal of Socio-Economics, 40(1), 35–42. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socec.2010.10.008
