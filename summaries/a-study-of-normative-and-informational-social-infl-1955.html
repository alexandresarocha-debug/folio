Background

Morton Deutsch and Harold Gerard published A study of normative and informational social influences upon individual judgement in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology in 1955. It is the paper that gave social psychology the names for two reasons a person might agree with a group. Informational influence is agreement that treats other people's judgements as evidence about the world. Normative influence is agreement that seeks acceptance, or avoids disapproval, regardless of what one believes is true. Asch's interviews had already described both experiences in the line task: people who came to doubt their eyes, and people who saw clearly and went along in order not to stand out. Deutsch and Gerard turned the distinction into a design. Folio's card on Asch points to this paper as the formal statement of that split.

The question

The question is whether the two influences can be pulled apart experimentally, and whether each is large enough to matter on its own. If people only conform because they want to be liked, then removing the possibility of being identified and judged should remove the conformity. If people also conform because they use the group as information, then some conformity should remain even when nobody can call them to account, and it should grow when the stimulus is harder to trust. If both predictions hold, Asch's single error-count really is two processes piled together, and a remedy aimed at only one of them will fail against the other.

They also asked a question about commitment. A person who has already stated a judgement, even privately, may find it harder to move when the group disagrees. A person who has been made to feel answerable to the group, as a member rather than as a visitor, may find it harder to stay put. Those are normative predictions. Testing them is a way of showing that normative influence is not a vague cloud of "pressure" but a response to particular social arrangements that an experimenter can turn up and down.

How the studies were set up

The task was a line judgement in the family Asch had made familiar. People said which of a set of lines matched a standard. Confederates, or a simulated group presenting itself as other participants, gave answers that were sometimes wrong and unanimous. The variations were about what kind of group the person thought he or she was in, and about what kind of access he or she had to the stimulus. In a face-to-face arrangement, the participant could be seen and could see the others. Disagreement was a public act, with all the ordinary consequences of looking obstinate or foolish in front of people. In an anonymous arrangement, responses were given so that the group could not identify who had said what. The informational value of hearing the others' answers could remain, because the answers were still there to be used as clues.

A second contrast varied the person's dependence on those clues. When the lines stayed in view, a participant who trusted his or her eyes had little need of the group as a source of fact. When the judgement had to be made from memory, after the stimulus was gone, the person's own trace was less secure and the group's answers became more useful as information. Deutsch and Gerard expected more agreement in the memory conditions for that reason, even where normative pressure was low. Further conditions varied commitment. In some, people set down their own judgement before hearing the group, so that a later change would be a change away from a position already taken. In others, the instructions bound them into the group more tightly, so that they expected the group to be evaluated, or understood themselves as publicly part of it.

What they found

Both influences appeared, and they added. People conformed more when they faced the group in public than when they answered anonymously, which is the normative difference: the same information, a higher social cost of being the odd one out, and more movement. People also conformed when they were anonymous. The residual agreement is what Deutsch and Gerard take as informational. If the only motive were approval, anonymity should have flattened the effect, and it did not. The memory contrast behaved as an informational account predicts. When the lines were no longer there to be seen, agreement with the group rose. A degraded private stimulus makes other people's reports more valuable, and participants used them.

Commitment moved the normative component in the expected directions. Participants who had already recorded a judgement of their own were less easily pulled off it when the group disagreed. Having taken a stand, even a quiet one, made the group's later answer a pressure to be inconsistent rather than a first draft to be adopted. Participants who had been led to feel more answerable to the group showed more public agreement. The group was no better as a source of optical information in that condition. It was a more important audience. Deutsch and Gerard read that pattern as the signature of normative influence: it tracks the social relationship and the cost of disapproval, not the clarity of the lines. Across the comparisons, the paper's claim is that a single conformity score mixes two contributions, and that the mix shifts when the procedure makes one contribution cheap and the other expensive.

How the authors read the results

The distinction is the contribution, and they state it as a pair of definitions a later experiment can use. Informational social influence is an influence to accept information from another as evidence about reality. Normative social influence is an influence to conform to the positive expectations of another, the expectations that one ought to behave in a certain way if one wants to be liked, accepted, or spared sanction. The definitions are plain on purpose. They let a researcher ask, of any situation, whether people lack a stable view of the facts, in which case they will lean on others as instruments, or whether they have a view and also have an audience, in which case they may announce a different one. Asch's line task, on this reading, contains both. The lines are clear, so the informational need is modest, and yet a unanimous group still supplies a rival report, which is why some participants genuinely waver.

Deutsch and Gerard also draw a practical consequence that the card relies on. The two influences have different cures. More information, a clearer stimulus, a chance to look again, should weaken informational influence and do little about the fear of standing out. Privacy, an ally, a norm that protects dissent, should weaken normative influence and do little about genuine uncertainty. Asch had already shown that a partner and a private ballot change the rate. This paper says why those levers work, and why a lever aimed at the wrong influence will disappoint. Telling a frightened dissenter to look more carefully at a line he already sees is no help. Telling a genuinely unsure person that dissent is welcome is only half a help, if what he needs is a better look at the stimulus.

Limits

The separation is cleaner in the design than in a living person. A participant who is unsure may also be more afraid of looking foolish, so informational and normative motives rise together and the experimenter's contrast only partly untangles them. Anonymity in a laboratory is never the anonymity of a real secret. People may still feel watched by the experimenter, which is a normative pressure the booth does not remove. Memory judgements change more than the availability of information. They change confidence, and confidence is entangled with the wish not to be the one who is wrong out loud. The paper's own results are differences in degree, in a line task, with students, in a short session. They establish that both influences can be detected. They do not supply a formula for how much of a given committee meeting is one or the other.

There is a historical limit too. The study belongs to the same era as Asch, and it uses the same kind of stripped perceptual conflict. Bond and Smith's later meta-analysis shows that the rate of yielding on line tasks has varied with culture and decade. Deutsch and Gerard's mechanisms are candidates for explaining that variation, and the 1955 paper cannot test them across cultures it did not run. A more collectivist setting might raise the normative cost of a public split. It might also raise the informational trust placed in a group. Either reading uses their distinction. Choosing between the readings takes evidence this study does not contain.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on Asch says that he distinguished people who came to doubt their perception from people who knew the majority was wrong and went along to avoid standing out, and that this was an early form of the distinction Deutsch and Gerard formalised. This paper is that formalisation. Reading it beside the monograph keeps the interviews from floating free of a theory. The participant who tells Asch he stopped trusting his eyes is the informational case. The participant who tells Asch he saw the line and could not face being the only one is the normative case. The error mark on the sheet is the same. The psychology is not, and the way one would help the two people is not either.

The card's practical lesson, that one ally makes it far easier to say what you see, can be stated in these terms. An ally reduces normative influence by ending the isolation, and reduces informational influence by providing a second report that matches the participant's eyes. The double action is why the ally is so effective, and why a break in unanimity helps even when the breaker is not accurate: the normative spell of a single public truth is broken, even if the informational picture stays muddy. Deutsch and Gerard are the reason the card can say that sentence as a mechanism and not only as a memory of one clever variation.
