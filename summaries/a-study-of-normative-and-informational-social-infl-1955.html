Morton Deutsch and Harold B. Gerard, at New York University in 1955, took Solomon Asch’s line task apart. One hundred and one students from psychology courses were told they were in a study of perception. On each trial they matched a line to one of three others. The correct match was easy. Three confederates gave wrong answers on cue. The question was which part of the pressure was the wish to fit in, and which part was using other people as evidence about the line.

The largest gap came from calling the session a group. In an anonymous setup, where each person saw the others’ answers as lights and was not face to face with them, people who had been led to act as members of a group made more than twice as many errors in the majority’s direction as people who were simply judging alongside strangers. The averages were 12.47 errors for the group members and 5.92 for the nonmembers, adding the trials on which the lines were still in view and the trials on which the lines had been taken away. Seven of the fifteen people in that group condition later wrote, without being asked, that they had felt an obligation to the others. None of the nonmembers mentioned an obligation. Sitting in the same room, rather than answering through lights, raised the errors by less. With no prior commitment, the face-to-face average was 7.08 errors against 5.92 in the anonymous condition. Taking the lines away before anyone spoke raised errors too: 4.08 against 3.00 in that same face-to-face comparison. Uncertainty made the other answers more informative. Belonging made them more binding.

Asking people to commit to their own judgment first, on paper or out loud, cut those socially pulled errors sharply in both the face-to-face and the anonymous arrangements. The norm “stick to what you said” worked against the norm “go along.” That is a usable fact in a meeting. If everyone writes a number, a diagnosis, or a vote before the discussion starts, the group loses some of its power to rewrite the first impression. If the group has already been told that it is a team and that teams stick together, the same wrong call is harder to resist, even when nobody can see who said what.

Deutsch and Gerard noted that their anonymous condition was only partly anonymous. Some students still felt the pressure of being found out. The study is one campus in 1955, with an easy visual task. What it separates, fitting in versus using others as evidence, still shows up whenever a team has to make a call that has a right answer and a social cost.

Source. Morton Deutsch and Harold B. Gerard, “A Study of Normative and Informational Social Influences upon Individual Judgment,” Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 51, no. 3 (1955): 629–636. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046408
