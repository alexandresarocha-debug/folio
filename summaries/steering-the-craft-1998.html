A workshop that refuses a single rule

Ursula K. Le Guin's Steering the Craft, published in 1998, is a small book of discussions and exercises for people who already write and want to work on the sentence. It is not a theory of the novel in the sense of Lubbock or Booth, and it is not a survey of movements. Its claim is practical and, in its own way, polemical. Craft can be practised in pieces. Rhythm, point of view, and the balance of showing and telling are not mysteries that descend on a talented person fully formed, and they are not laws handed down by a recent fashion in workshops. They are handlings of language, and handlings improve when a writer does them on purpose, under a constraint, and then looks at what the constraint forced. Le Guin writes as a novelist of science fiction and fantasy who has no patience for the idea that those modes are exempt from the sentence, and equally little patience for a literary fashion that would shrink every story to one approved way of telling it.

The title is the method. Steering is continuous small adjustment, not a single decision made at the harbour. A piece of fiction is going somewhere, and the writer's hand on the sentence is what keeps it going there rather than sliding into habit. Le Guin is suspicious of habits that present themselves as morals. The most famous of them, for the audience of this book, is the rule that one must always show and never tell. She treats the rule as a useful provocation that has been mistaken for a constitution. Telling is how fiction crosses time, summarises a situation the scene cannot afford to play, and lets a narrative voice think. Showing is how fiction makes a moment thick enough to be lived. A story that only shows becomes slow and strangely blind, unable to say what its people know. A story that only tells becomes a report. The craft is the steering between them, and the book exists to give a writer occasions to practise the steering instead of reciting the slogan.

Sound, before argument

She begins, characteristically, with the sound of prose. Sentences have pace, weight, and pitch. A reader hears them, even reading silently, and a writer who never reads aloud is missing the instrument's feedback. Le Guin asks for attention to repetition, to the length of a sentence beside the length of its neighbours, and to the difference between a rhythm that is alive and a rhythm that is only correct. This emphasis is her answer to a kind of workshop talk that jumps straight to character arcs and themes. The theme will not survive a paragraph that the ear rejects. Science fiction and fantasy, which must often carry strange names and unfamiliar social facts, are especially exposed. If the sentence cannot hold a made-up word with conviction, the world the word belongs to will not hold either. The exercise of listening is therefore not a preliminary to the real craft. It is the craft, applied to the surface on which every other choice will be felt.

The same ear governs her dislike of prose that has been cleaned of all risk. She will set a writer a constraint, such as writing a passage without adjectives and adverbs, not because modifiers are sinful but because a writer who has never done without them does not know which ones were load-bearing. Constraint reveals habit. After the exercise, the modifiers can come back, chosen. This is the pedagogy of the whole book. A discussion names a piece of the craft, warns against the dogma that has grown up around it, and then proposes a short piece of writing that makes the piece unavoidable. The writer is not asked to agree with Le Guin in the abstract. The writer is asked to produce a page under a pressure that the agreement would have left optional.

Point of view, including the one that went out of fashion

On point of view she is precise and unfashionable. First person, limited third person, and the various degrees of distance inside them are described as tools with known dangers. Head-hopping, the drift from one character's mind to another's inside a scene that has not earned the drift, is a danger because it spends the reader's trust. A deliberate shift, marked by structure and by need, is not the same danger. The distinction is Lubbock's discipline, kept, and then stripped of his ranking. Le Guin does not think the limited viewpoint is the moral summit of the form. She thinks it is one steering choice, superb when a story is the story of what one person can know, and wrong when a story needs a wider air.

The wider air is the omniscient voice, and her defence of it is the most pointed chapter in the book's relation to the show-and-tell tradition. Late-twentieth-century workshops often treated omniscience as a beginner error, a failure to pick a character and stay there. Le Guin treats it as an old and legitimate power. A voice that can move among people, that can generalise, that can look down a street and then into a century, is the voice of a great deal of the fiction she cares about, and it is the natural voice of stories about peoples, places, and long consequences. Used badly it is fog. Used well it is a mind the reader is glad to be in company with. The exercise that accompanies the defence asks the writer to try the voice on purpose, so that omniscience becomes a decision rather than an accident of not having noticed whose head one is in. Booth would recognise the argument. Commentary and a governing intelligence are not intrusions into a pure story. They are ways a story can know itself. Le Guin puts that argument back into the room where writers are actually practising.

What the exercises are for

The exercises share a morality even when their subjects differ. They are short, they change one variable, and they assume that a writer learns by comparing versions. Write the scene from this viewpoint and then from that one. Write it by telling the outcome and then by staying inside the occasion. Listen to the paragraph aloud and mark where the ear stumbles. The comparison is the lesson. No exercise ends by declaring a winner that all future stories must obey. This is the respect in which the book is an answer to Lubbock rather than a repetition of him. Lubbock offered a history in which the novel ascends towards the scene and the limited view. Le Guin offers a bench on which every one of those techniques, including the ones his history called lower, can be picked up and handled. The writer leaves not with a ranking but with a larger set of available motions, and with the experience of having felt, in a particular paragraph, what each motion costs.

There is a limit to what a book of this kind will do, and the limit is part of its honesty. It will not supply a plot theory, a theory of genre, or a philosophy of what fiction is for. A writer whose difficulty is structural, a story that does not yet know its ordeal or its promise, will not solve it by a cleaner sentence, though a cleaner sentence may reveal that the structure was the difficulty. Le Guin does not pretend otherwise. Steering assumes a boat that is already in the water. The book is for the hand on the tiller, not for the chart of the whole sea. Within that charge it is unusually free of fake certainty. The freedom matters because the readers of craft books are vulnerable to certainty. They have come for a rule that will make the next page less frightening. Le Guin's refusal to give the single rule, and her insistence on giving the next exercise instead, is the pedagogical form of a literary judgement. Fiction is a repertoire. Repertoires are learned by playing more than one piece.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading is show, don't tell, and the reasons to ignore it. Lubbock states the scenic ideal with the most analytical force. Booth demolishes the ideal as a law, by showing that telling is rhetoric and that rhetoric is the condition of all fiction, shown or told. Le Guin's book is the third step, the one that returns the argument to a writer sitting at a desk. She keeps showing as a power, the power of the scene and of the limited view. She restores telling as a power, the power of summary, of thought, and of an omniscient voice that workshops had tried to retire. And she refuses the retirement. The exercises, in sound, in viewpoint, and in the deliberate mixing of scene and statement, are how the refusal becomes a practice rather than a counter-slogan. Read her after the two theorists when you want the quarrel to change the next paragraph you write, and when you want a standard of craft that is strict about the sentence and uninterested in promoting one narrative fashion to the rank of a moral law.
