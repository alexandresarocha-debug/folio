Ursula K. Le Guin built Steering the Craft (1998) out of the workshops she ran, and the exercises are the argument. In the chapter she calls "Indirect Narration, or What Tells," she asks for a page or two of pure dialogue. Write it like a play. The speakers are only A and B. No stage directions, no description of either person, nothing but the words they say. The story and the two characters have to arrive through talk alone. A later part of the same exercise turns the screw the other way. In 200 to 600 words, with no viewpoint character in the room, describe a place that someone inhabits, a room, a garden, an office, a bed, and let the absent person show through the place. Then do it for an event that is not happening in the piece: the room, the street, or the field where something severe occurred or is about to occur. She tells the writer to use the pathetic fallacy on purpose, the old device in which weather and furniture carry a human mood.

The next chapter, "Crowding and Leaping," is about how much of that material you keep. Her tactic is to crowd the first draft, to put the talk and the furniture and the history in, and then to cut whatever merely repeats or slows the story until what remains is what counts. Leaping is the cut. What you leap over is larger than what you leave on the page. The dialogue exercise and the room exercise are two ways of refusing the paragraph that explains a person from a height. One way, the person is nothing but speech. The other way, the person is not even present, and the coffee cup and the unmade bed do the telling.

Taken together, the exercises are a better account of "show, don't tell" than the slogan. Showing, here, is not a ban on sentences that inform. It is a demand that information come in carrying a person, a pace, or a pressure. A fantasy writer, she notes in that chapter, has an extra burden, because the reader does not already share the world, and a lump of explanation will sink the scene. You still have to get the rule of the world in. You get it in by letting A and B argue under it, or by letting the room obey it. The first draft is allowed to blab. The revision decides what told. Writers who treat every sentence of context as a moral failure end up with talk that never sits in a world, or with a world that never changes anyone. Le Guin's drills are there to make you practice both halves, the crowded page and the leap, before you pretend you know which one your story needs.
