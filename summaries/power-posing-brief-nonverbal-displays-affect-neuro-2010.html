Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy, and Andy Yap had an experimenter pose 42 people, 26 women and 16 men, for one minute in each of two postures. High-power posers sat with feet up and hands behind the head, then stood leaning on a table. Low-power posers sat drawn in, then stood with arms and legs crossed. The cover story was about sensors above and below the heart. Saliva was taken before the poses and again about 17 minutes after. Relative to their own baselines, testosterone rose for the high-power posers and fell for the low-power posers. Cortisol did the reverse. Given 2 dollars and a coin-flip chance to risk it for 4 dollars, 86.36 percent of the high-power group rolled and 60 percent of the low-power group did. High-power posers also said they felt more "powerful" and "in charge," 2.57 against 1.83 on a scale from 1 to 4. Two minutes of posture, the paper claimed, had shifted hormones, feelings, and a financial risk.

The people arranging the bodies knew which pose was which. In a statement she posted in 2016, Carney wrote, "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real." She noted that five people had been excluded and the published sample was 42, that the team checked the gambling result as the data came in, in chunks of roughly 25, then 10, then 7, then 5, and that they reported a likelihood-ratio p-value of .05 for the gamble rather than the Pearson chi-square of .052. She also noted that people were told immediately whether they had won, and that winning itself can raise testosterone, so the hormone gap could have been a prize effect. Participants could win the extra 2 dollars only if they took the risk, and more of the expansive posers took it.

Eva Ranehill and colleagues, in 2015, ran a blinded version with 200 people. Feelings of power still moved. Testosterone, cortisol, and risk-taking did not. Standing open before a meeting may change a self-report for a minute. The 2010 paper is the study that turned that posture into a claim about hormones and decisions. Its first author later called the data flimsy, and the larger test of the hormones and the gamble did not back them.
