Background

In 2010 Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap published a short paper in Psychological Science with a large claim. Holding an expansive posture for a brief period, a minute or two with the body opened rather than folded, would do more than change a mood. It would raise testosterone, lower the stress hormone cortisol, increase willingness to take a risk, and increase the feeling of being powerful. The study had 42 participants. The postures were the ones that later became famous: hands on hips, or feet up on a desk, set against contracted postures that take up less space. Folio's card on power posing begins here, and it begins with the caution the later papers force. The hormonal and behavioural effects did not replicate. The lead author later said she no longer believed they were real.

The paper is still the right place to start, because the claim has to be stated before it can be checked. A walkthrough that opened with the debunking would be describing a controversy without the object of the controversy. The object is a two-minute pose, a small sample, and four outcomes that were supposed to move together.

The question

Can a nonverbal display that signals power to other people also signal power to the person making it, deeply enough to change hormones and a risky choice? The question joins two traditions. One is the study of nonverbal behaviour, in which expansive postures are associated with status and contracted postures with subordination. The other is the newer interest in embodiment, the idea that adopting the outward form of a state can produce the state, rather than merely expressing it. Carney, Cuddy and Yap asked whether the outward form, held briefly and in private, would be enough. If it was, a person about to enter a stressful evaluation could change her chemistry and her nerve by arranging her body, with no change in skill, status or preparation.

How the study was set up

Participants were assigned to hold either high-power or low-power poses. The high-power poses were expansive. The low-power poses were closed, limbs drawn in, body made small. They held the poses for a short period, the two minutes that the card and the subsequent argument treat as the dose. The setting was a laboratory, not a boardroom. The poses were instructed rather than chosen in the course of a real negotiation, which is what makes the study a test of posture as such, stripped of the power the posture usually accompanies. A person who sprawls because she is in charge is a different object from a person who sprawls because an experimenter asked her to.

The outcomes were chosen to be a bundle. Feelings of power were reported on a scale, the subjective piece. Risk tolerance was measured with a behavioural choice, a gamble the participant could accept or decline. Saliva samples were taken so that testosterone and cortisol could be compared before and after the poses. The prediction was directional on each measure. Expansive posture should raise felt power, raise risk-taking, raise testosterone and lower cortisol. A result in which only the feeling moved would have been a milder paper. The paper that was published claimed the bundle.

What they reported

The reported results matched the bundle. Relative to the contracted poses, the expansive poses were associated with higher feelings of power, a greater tendency to take the risky option, higher testosterone and lower cortisol. With 42 participants, the study was small for a claim about hormones, which are noisy measurements, and small for a claim that would soon be given to the public as a tool. Small studies can be right. They can also be the size at which ordinary analytic flexibility, a choice of covariate, a choice of how to code the gamble, a choice of which comparison to trust, moves a result across the line of significance. The paper presents the four effects as a coherent hormonal and behavioural response to posture. That coherence is what made the finding exciting. It is also what later critics, and the lead author herself, came to doubt.

Nothing in the original report is a long-term follow-up. The measurements are immediate. The paper does not show that a habit of standing differently changes a career, a negotiation or a hormone profile across weeks. It shows, or it reported, an acute difference between two groups who had just held different poses. The distance between that report and the advice that later travelled is the distance the card is about.

How the claim travelled

The public life of the finding is part of its scientific life, because the public life is what made the replication urgent. Cuddy's 2012 TED talk presented the two-minute pose as a practical act before a job interview or a difficult meeting, and the talk became one of the most watched of its kind. The advice was simple, the mechanism sounded biological, and the cost of trying was a minute of privacy. Those are the conditions under which a small laboratory result can become a ritual. The ritual does not have to be cynical to be ahead of the evidence. A speaker who believes a finding, and a finding that is true in one room of 42 people, are not yet a finding a stranger should use to prepare for an interview.

Inside the field, the paper sat at the junction of embodiment and hormones, two areas that were producing striking results at the time and that would shortly become case studies in fragility. The junction made the claim more interesting and more exposed. A failure of the hormonal piece would leave, at best, a change in a self-report. A failure of the risk piece would leave a feeling without the decision the feeling was supposed to explain. The bundle was the theory. Unbundling it, which is what the later papers do, is not a minor qualification. It is a different claim.

Limits

Even read as generously as the original report asks to be read, the study is one experiment. There is no second laboratory inside the paper, no preregistered analysis, and no demonstration that the poses work when the person cares about the outcome in the way a candidate cares about an interview. Demand is a real concern. People know which poses look powerful. Asked to hold one and then asked whether they feel powerful, they may answer the question the posture has already been labelled with. The hormone results were the paper's reply to that concern, because saliva is not a polite self-report. If the hormones do not replicate, the reply fails, and the feeling of power has to be interpreted again, as a possible real change in mood and also as a possible answer to a leading arrangement of the body.

The sample size is the other limit that does not require hindsight. Forty-two people, split between conditions, is a thin basis for four correlated claims about physiology and choice. The card states the number because the number is part of the caution. A large effect in a small sample is sometimes the sign of a large effect. It is also the sign a field had not yet taught itself to distrust on principle. This paper was published into that earlier teaching.

What the later record did to it

The later record is the rest of the card, and it should be previewed here so the original claim is not left hanging as if it were current. In 2015 Eva Ranehill and colleagues, with a sample of around 200, found no effect of the poses on hormones or on risk-taking. Participants did report feeling somewhat more powerful, which is a narrower result than the bundle. In 2016 Carney, the first author, posted a statement that she did not believe the power-pose effects were real, and she described analytic choices in the original study that had inflated the findings. Simmons and Simonsohn's p-curve of the literature concluded that the published behavioural and hormonal evidence did not have the shape of a real effect. Cuddy and colleagues answered, in 2018, that a broader look at postural feedback still supports an effect on feelings of power. The card's summary is the fair one. The strong claim did not survive. A smaller claim about feeling powerful may.

None of that later history changes what the 2010 paper says on its own pages. It changes what a reader is entitled to do with those pages. They are the source of a hypothesis and of a cultural episode. They are not a manual.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Power posing: the TED talk that didn't replicate, is a case study, and this is the study. The two-minute expansive pose, the 42 participants, the predicted rise in testosterone and fall in cortisol, the increase in risk-taking and in felt power, are the claim as published. Ranehill and colleagues are the direct replication. Simmons and Simonsohn are the examination of the wider literature's statistical shape. Cuddy, Schultz and Fosse are the defence of a reduced claim about feelings. Read the 2010 paper in order to see exactly how much was being asserted, because the assertion, not a vague memory of a talk, is what failed to replicate. The talk matters because it shows how quickly a small, vivid result can become advice. The paper matters because advice that precise should have been this vulnerable to a second look.
