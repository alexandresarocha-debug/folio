Tyler W. Watts, Greg J. Duncan, and Haonan Quan went back to the claim that a preschooler’s wait for a second marshmallow forecasts the rest of life. They used the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development study, in which children had been given a delay task at 54 months and then followed to age 15. Of the children with a usable wait time and with achievement and behavior scores at 15, there were 918. The analysis that carries the paper is the 552 whose mothers had not finished college, a group about ten times the size of the famous Stanford follow-up and much less affluent than the Bing Nursery School children in that follow-up.

In that group of 552, children waited 3.99 minutes on average. The task stopped at 7 minutes, and 45 percent waited the whole time. Twenty-three percent waited less than 20 seconds. Each extra minute waited lined up with about a tenth of a standard deviation more achievement at age 15. The bivariate correlation was about half the size of the correlations Yuichi Shoda, Walter Mischel, and Philip Peake had reported in 1990, where, in 35 children, wait time correlated .57 with SAT math and .42 with SAT verbal. Once Watts and colleagues added family background, early cognitive ability, and the home environment, about two-thirds of the association was gone. Most of what remained of the achievement gap was the difference between children who quit in under 20 seconds and children who managed to wait longer than that. Waiting 4 minutes rather than 2 did not mark off a higher achiever in the way the original story suggested. Links to behavior problems at 15 were smaller still, and rarely clear.

Children whose mothers had a college degree waited longer, 5.38 minutes on average, and only 10 percent of them quit in under 20 seconds. The 7-minute ceiling piled that group up at the top, which is one reason the authors treated the two groups separately. A test that many advantaged children max out is a weak way to rank them.

For a parent, a teacher, or a program that wants to “teach grit,” the practical reading is that the minute spent waiting is partly a minute about the child’s circumstances. Coaching a four-year-old to hold out for a promised treat will not, on these numbers, move adolescent achievement by anything like the original correlations. The children who cannot wait even 20 seconds are the ones whose later scores sit apart, and those children are concentrated where money, schooling, and the home already differ.
