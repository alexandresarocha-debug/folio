The parable, and the sample underneath it

Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan published Revisiting the Marshmallow Test in 2018, and the title is a precise description of the job. Walter Mischel and his colleagues had, in the late 1960s and early 1970s, offered young children at Stanford's Bing Nursery School a choice between one treat now and two treats if they could wait while the experimenter left the room, often for up to fifteen or twenty minutes. Later follow-ups, above all Shoda, Mischel and Peake in 1990, reported that the children who waited longer were rated as more competent adolescents and scored higher on the SAT. The waiting became a parable. Self-control in a preschool room, on that telling, was a window onto the kind of person a child would become, and a key to later success.

Watts, Duncan and Quan ask what happens to the parable when the test is repeated as a conceptual replication in a larger and more diverse sample, and when the association between waiting and later achievement is asked to survive the facts of family life. Their sample includes over nine hundred children. The original follow-ups were small, and they were drawn from the children of Stanford staff and students. An association estimated in that narrow, relatively advantaged group can look like a law of child development when it is really a description of variation inside one privileged setting. The 2018 paper is the attempt to separate those two readings.

Folio's card, The marshmallow test: willpower or circumstance?, takes this paper as its source. The card's caution is the paper's conclusion in plain language. Larger and more diverse samples show a much weaker link between waiting and later success once family background is taken into account. A walkthrough has to show how that weakening was measured, and what it does and does not say about the original studies of how children manage to wait at all.

What was repeated, and what was new

A conceptual replication keeps the idea and loosens the exact theatre. Watts and his colleagues were interested in delay of gratification as a predictor of later achievement, the claim that made the test famous, rather than in reproducing every detail of Bing Nursery's trays and bells. The children in the newer sample faced a version of the choice between a smaller reward now and a larger reward later. The outcomes were measures of achievement in later childhood, the sort of measures an educational researcher would recognise. The design then does the thing the original follow-ups, given their size and their homogeneity, could not do well. It asks how much of the association remains when family background, early cognitive ability, and the home environment are held constant.

That set of controls is the scientific point, and it is easy to caricature as an attempt to make a real effect disappear. The caricature gets the logic backwards. If waiting predicts achievement only because the children who wait also come from homes that already supply the ingredients of later achievement, then waiting is a marker of circumstance. An intervention aimed at the marker, a training programme in staring down a marshmallow, will disappoint. If waiting predicts achievement even among children who share a background, a level of early ability, and a similar home, then the delay itself is carrying information about something a school or a parent might want to understand. The paper's controls are a way of asking which of those worlds the data live in.

The original studies of the test, as distinct from the famous follow-ups, were largely about strategy. Children waited longer when they could distract themselves, and when they thought about the treat in an abstract way rather than staring at its tempting features. Mischel's own later writing emphasises that self-control is a set of learnable strategies. Those experiments can be true, and useful, whether or not the number of seconds a child waits at age four is a strong independent predictor of adolescent test scores. Watts, Duncan and Quan are revisiting the predictive parable. They leave the strategy research standing. Keeping the two apart is the difference between a careful update and a bonfire.

How the association shrank

The card states the quantitative result without drama, and the lack of drama is appropriate. The association between delay and later achievement was about half the size of the association in the original follow-up. Once family background, early cognitive ability, and home environment were controlled, the remaining association was reduced by roughly two thirds. Waiting was still related to later achievement in the simple comparison. It was a shadow of the parable, and most of that shadow was shared with the circumstances the child had already arrived with. A reader who wants a single moral has to choose. The honest moral is graded. Delay of gratification is a real behaviour, with a modest raw association with later scores, and with a much smaller association once the child's world is taken seriously.

Half the original size, and then a further large reduction under controls, also says something about why the parable travelled. The original follow-up sample was small and advantaged. Small samples produce unstable estimates. Advantaged samples compress the variation in family background, so a behaviour that is entangled with background can look, inside the sample, like a pure individual difference. Publish that estimate, attach it to a vivid image of a child alone with a sweet, and the culture has a story about character. The 2018 paper replaces the story with a comparison that has room for character and room for circumstance, and that finds circumstance doing most of the predictive work the story had assigned to character.

None of this says that waiting is random, or that children who can distract themselves gain nothing. A modest residual association can still be real. The paper's warning is about the use of the test as a ranking of destinies, and about interventions designed as if the residual were the whole original effect. Folio's caution is written for that use. Teach the test as a demonstration that circumstances and trust and strategy all sit inside an apparent act of will. Teach it as a sorting hat for who deserves success, and the 2018 evidence is against you.

Circumstance includes whether adults can be believed

A related line of work, which the card places beside this paper, changes what waiting means even before the longitudinal statistics are consulted. Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri and Richard Aslin showed in 2013 that children who first met an unreliable experimenter, one who broke promises about art supplies, waited much less than children who met a reliable one. Waiting is, in part, a belief about the environment. A second treat that may never arrive is a bad trade, and a child who has evidence that adults do not follow through is drawing a rational conclusion. The marshmallow is a probe of willpower only in a world where the promised second marshmallow is a sure thing.

That finding helps interpret Watts, Duncan and Quan's controls. Family background and home environment are bundles of many things, and one of the things they bundle is the reliability of the adults a child has known. If reliability varies with background, then a statistical control for background will shrink the apparent willpower effect for a reason Kidd and her colleagues made visible in a single afternoon. The child who eats the first treat is expressing an expectation. The expectation may be about their own capacity to endure a craving, and it may be about the trustworthiness of the person who left the room. A predictive study that ignores the second reading will congratulate the children whose worlds have been trustworthy, and call the congratulation a science of self-control.

The 2018 paper does not, by itself, measure trust. It measures the predictive claim under broader sampling and under controls. Read next to the 2013 experiment, the controls stop looking like a technicality. They are a rough way of holding constant the worlds in which waiting is a sensible gamble. A child from a stable, well-resourced home and a child from a home where promises slip are solving different problems when the experimenter steps out. Averaging them, and calling the average a trait, was the parable's move. Separating them is the revision.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The marshmallow test: willpower or circumstance?, is organised around this revision. The famous follow-up said that preschool waiting predicted adolescent competence and SAT scores. Watts, Duncan and Quan, with over nine hundred children and a more diverse sample, found an association about half as large, and then found that controls for family background, early ability, and home environment cut the remaining link by roughly two thirds. The card's other readings fill in the mechanism the revision implies. Shoda, Mischel and Peake are the follow-up being revisited. Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin are the demonstration that an unreliable adult changes how long children wait. Read the 2018 paper for the predictive claim as it looks beyond Stanford's nursery. The strategies Mischel studied can still be real. The destiny story cannot keep the size it had.
