In 2005 three political scientists, John Alford, Carolyn Funk and John Hibbing, asked a question most of their field ignored: could people be born with a lean to the left or the right? They used a large set of American twins known as the Virginia 30K, gathered by the geneticist Lindon Eaves and colleagues from a Virginia birth registry and from volunteers recruited through the AARP, the US retirees' association.

The twins answered 28 quick items in a format called the Wilson-Patterson inventory. Each item is one or two words, such as school prayer, death penalty, immigration, unions or gay rights, and people answer agree, uncertain or disagree.

The logic is simple. Identical twins share almost all their genes. Non-identical twins share about half of the genes that vary. Both kinds grow up in the same home. So if identical twins agree with each other more than non-identical twins do, genes are probably part of the reason.

They did. On all 28 items identical twins were more alike than non-identical twins, and every difference was statistically significant. Heritability ranged from 0.18 to 0.41, and more than half the items were at 0.3 or above. The average across items was 0.32, against 0.16 for the shared family environment. Shared environment beat genes on only 4 of the 28 items. School prayer and property tax were at the top, at 0.41. The item 'Liberals' was at the bottom, at 0.18.

The authors then added the answers into one liberal-to-conservative score. For this index, about 2,100 identical pairs correlated 0.65 and about 1,400 non-identical pairs 0.43. That gave a heritability of 0.43, a shared environment of 0.22 and an unshared environment of 0.35.

One result went the other way. For party affiliation, heritability was only 0.14 and the shared environment 0.41. Which party you support looked much more like something learned at home than your views on the issues.

What this means in real life: you may pick up your party from your parents, while your gut reactions to issues are partly inborn. That fits the common experience of children who keep the family party label but drift on particular issues.

Caution: this is a twin-only study with simple calculations from correlations. It rests on the assumption that identical twins are not treated more alike than non-identical twins, which critics dispute. Later studies with parents and siblings, more countries and better models found broadly similar figures.

Source. Alford, J. R., Funk, C. L., and Hibbing, J. R. (2005). Are political orientations genetically transmitted? American Political Science Review, 99(2), 153-167. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055405051579
