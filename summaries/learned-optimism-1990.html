In 1986 Martin Seligman and Peter Schulman gave a questionnaire to people whose job is mostly rejection. Life insurance agents at Metropolitan Life filled out the Attributional Style Questionnaire, which asks how you explain good events and bad ones. Among 94 experienced agents, the half with the more optimistic style had sold 37% more insurance in their first two years than the more pessimistic half. A second group of 103 newly hired agents took the same questionnaire before they had sold anything. Over the next year, the optimistic half were still in the job at twice the rate of the pessimistic half, and they sold more. The industry figure Seligman cites puts the stakes in view: about 78% of life insurance agents hired in the United States quit within three years.

The questionnaire sorts an explanation along three lines. A bad month can be read as permanent or temporary, as something about the person or something about the situation, and as a cloud over every part of life or as a problem in one place. Learned Optimism, Seligman's 1990 book, treats that pattern as a skill a reader can practice. The exercises ask you to catch a pessimistic sentence and rewrite it so that it is still true and less global. He calls the target accurate optimism. A story that denies a real constraint is a poor goal. The useful rewrite is the one that does not pile permanence, spread, and self-blame onto a setback that does not require them.

At work, in a clinic, or at home, the style shows up in the minute after a no. The next call, the next application, or the next conversation with a patient depends on whether the last rejection is filed as "I am the kind of person who fails" or as "that person was not a buyer today." The insurance studies are evidence about a job that is built out of failure. They are evidence that the explanation a person reaches for predicts who keeps going. They leave poverty, illness, and a bad boss in place. Those still set the odds.

In 2016 Steven Maier and Seligman went back to the animal experiments that began this line of work and argued that the original story had the learning backwards. Shutting down, they wrote, is the default reaction to a long run of bad events. What is learned, when an animal or a person can learn it, is control.
