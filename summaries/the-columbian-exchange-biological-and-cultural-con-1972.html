In December 1518 and January 1519, a disease identified as smallpox appeared among the people of Santo Domingo, on the island of Hispaniola. Alfred W. Crosby puts that outbreak at the start of a biological invasion. A few Spaniards got sick. Among the Taíno, who had no previous experience of the virus, the dying was on a different scale. The infection spread through the Greater Antilles and on into Mexico. Crosby argues that smallpox, not Spanish tactics alone, decided the fight for Tenochtitlan. An Aztec army that had not just been hollowed out by epidemic, he writes, could have driven Cortés's force out. The Old World had lived with smallpox as a childhood disease. The Americas had not.

The same ships carried animals, and the animals remade daily life almost as fast as the germs remade the population. Horses, cattle, pigs, sheep, and goats had no American counterparts of equal use, apart from the llama and the alpaca in the Andes. Pigs and cattle multiplied in the islands and then on the mainland. Horses changed war, travel, and hunting, first for the invaders and then for Indigenous nations who took the herds up. Crosby treats this as an exchange, not a one-way disaster. Maize, potatoes, and manioc moved the other direction and later fed Europe, Africa, and Asia. Wheat and sugar cane moved west. His name for the whole traffic, the Columbian Exchange, stuck because it described a collision of biotas, not only a conquest.

The practical consequence is that a voyage can matter more as an ecological event than as a political one. A virus does not need a viceroy. A pig does not need a land grant to eat a garden. Once smallpox was in the Caribbean, the empires of Mexico and the Andes were fighting invaders who had already been helped by a death rate the invaders did not suffer. Once maize and potatoes were in the Old World, populations there could grow on crops no European had planted in 1491. Anyone trying to explain the modern world from kings and treaties alone is starting the story after the plants and the germs have already changed the numbers.

How large those numbers were is still disputed, and Crosby was writing near the high end of the argument. Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian, reviewing the demographic literature in 2010, note that estimates of the population of the Americas in 1491 still run from about 8 million, Alfred Kroeber's 1939 figure, to more than 110 million, Henry Dobyns's 1966 figure, and that decades of research have not narrowed the range. They report a separate estimate that central Mexico fell from just under 15 million in 1519 to about 1.5 million a century later. The direction of the catastrophe is not in serious doubt. A single body count is.
