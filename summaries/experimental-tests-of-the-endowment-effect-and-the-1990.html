Background

The Coase theorem is one of the cleanest claims in economics. If people can bargain without friction, and if they know their own values, goods end up with whoever values them most. Who starts out holding the good affects the direction of payment. It does not affect the final allocation. A buyer who wants a mug more than its owner will buy it. An owner who wants it more will keep it. Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler were not, in 1990, attacking that claim as a piece of logic. They asked whether the psychology of ownership would stop the logic from appearing in a market built to give the theorem its best chance.

The paper belongs on Folio's prospect-theory card because the prediction comes from the 1979 value function. Giving up something one already holds is coded as a loss. Acquiring the same thing is coded as a gain. If losses loom larger than gains, the owner demands more to part with the object than a non-owner will pay to get it, and trade dries up. That gap is the endowment effect. This paper is the experimental argument that the gap shows up at once, in ordinary goods, and that it is not an artefact of a badly explained market.

The question

Does the initial allocation of a good change how much of it is traded, once the usual excuses have been stripped away? Transaction costs can be made negligible. The rules can be taught. Payment can reward people for stating a price they mean. If owners still will not sell at prices buyers will pay, the theorem's prediction about the volume of trade has failed, and the failure has the shape prospect theory expects. A second question keeps the first one honest. Does the same reluctance appear when the object is not a thing to be used, but a token whose cash value the experimenter has assigned, so that attachment has nothing to grip and the rational sorting of the tokens can be calculated in advance?

How the studies were set up

The markets were run in classrooms. In the token markets, each person received a personal redemption value, a price at which the experimenter would cash a token. Some people began with a token and could sell. Others began without one and could buy. Everyone stated a price, the market cleared, and tokens were meant to move from lower values to higher values. Because the values were assigned by the experiment, the volume of trade implied by the Coase theorem is a calculation rather than a hunch. These rounds are the control. If the procedure itself frightens people out of trading, the tokens will show the fright.

The goods markets put a real object in place of the token. The object Folio's card has in mind is a coffee mug, and other rounds used a pen. Half the room was handed the good. Half was not. Sellers named the least they would accept. Buyers named the most they would pay. A price was crossed from those statements, and goods changed hands or stayed where they were. Rounds were repeated, so that simple confusion about the rules had time to fade. Nobody was asked to introspect about ownership. The observations are prices and the count of trades.

A further comparison asked whether the gap was a bargaining pose or a change in the reference point. Owners who had just been handed a mug were set beside people asked to choose between receiving a mug and receiving money, a choice that does not require surrendering something already held. If the status of owner is doing the work, the reluctance should appear immediately, and it should exceed the reluctance of people who are only deciding which benefit to take.

What they found

The token markets did what the induced values said they should. Trade occurred in the volume the assigned values implied, and the institution was usable. People were not, as a general habit, refusing to sell, and they were not lost in the instructions. The procedure can carry a Coase prediction when the object is already a stand-in for cash.

The goods markets did not follow that prediction. Owners of mugs asked more, in order to sell, than non-owners offered to pay, and fewer mugs changed hands than would have moved them to the people a frictionless bargain says should have wanted them. Pens produced the same pattern. Running the market again did not teach the gap away. The reluctance was present as soon as the good had been given out. It did not require a long acquaintance with the object, a story about it, or a seller's ritual of opening high and climbing down. People choosing between a mug and money, without already holding the mug, did not show the owners' reluctance. On the authors' reading, what stung was giving up an endowed good, a loss, rather than failing to acquire one, a foregone gain.

How the authors read the results

Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler treat the result as prospect theory applied to a market, not as a curiosity about crockery. The value function evaluates changes from a reference point, and the reference point shifts when the mug is placed in the hand. The owner is no longer contemplating a gain. The owner is contemplating a loss, and losses loom larger than gains. Buyers, and people choosing a benefit they do not yet hold, still evaluate the mug as a gain, and they put a lower number on it. The two prices are evaluations from two origins. They are not two noisy estimates of one true value.

The Coase theorem remains intact as a piece of reasoning about a world without this asymmetry. As a prediction about volume, it becomes unreliable when the good is the sort of thing a person codes as a loss to surrender. Tokens slipped through, because trading a token with a posted cash value is a calculation. Consumption goods did not. For those goods, the initial entitlement changes how much trade a cheap bargaining process will actually produce. A legal rule that hands a right to one side is therefore not guaranteed to be undone by subsequent deals, even when the deals are easy to make. The starting allocation can stick.

Limits

The markets are brief, the goods are small, and the participants are whoever was in the room. A mug is not a house, a job, or a permit. The paper shows that an endowment effect appears under conditions arranged to favour the Coase prediction. It does not measure a single margin by which every real market will miss its predicted volume, and it does not claim that experience, competition and large stakes never narrow the gap. Later critics, Gal and Rucker among them on this same card, have asked whether part of the gap is a taste for leaving things as they are. The chooser condition is this paper's reply to that worry. A reader can still ask whether a classroom chooser is a faithful model of a buyer who has walked into a shop.

A gap between buying prices and selling prices shows that the two roles evaluate the good differently. That pattern fits loss aversion. It does not, on its own, pin down how much steeper the loss side of the value function is, and this walkthrough will not invent the ratio. The claim the design supports is qualitative. Owners resist surrendering the good more than non-owners strive to acquire it.

Replications and critiques

The mug market became a standard demonstration, and versions of it have been repeated often enough that the basic reluctance is not a story about one afternoon. The explanation is what remains in dispute. Loss aversion, as the 1979 theory states it, is the account these authors defend. A strategic habit of asking high, a misunderstanding of the rules, and a general preference for inaction have all been offered as rivals. The token markets and the chooser condition exist to make those rivals earn their place. Gal and Rucker argue that inertia can still carry much of the load, and that loss aversion is the wrong universal law to hang on every gap of this kind. Their argument is intelligible only against the evidence this paper put on the table.

A separate caution concerns the institution. Reservation prices stated once, in a room, are not a price discovered over a season of trade, with reputation, repetition and the chance to regret a refusal. Where those forces are strong, the endowment effect can shrink. Where a person trades once, in a good just handed over, the paper's warning is the relevant one. Initial ownership is already inside the valuation.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on prospect theory names the endowment effect as a puzzle the 1979 theory was built to explain, and it sends the reader here for the experiments. The mugs are the concrete case. The tokens are the control that makes the mugs readable. Together they show why a reference point is not only a device for hypothetical gambles. It changes the volume of trade, which is the quantity the Coase theorem had promised to free from the initial allocation. Read the 1979 paper for the value function, Gal and Rucker for the case that loss aversion has been overworked, and Thaler's Misbehaving for the way this result was carried into the profession. Read this paper for the markets.
