Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch, and Richard Thaler handed coffee mugs to some students and not to others, then opened a market. In one test, 77 students at Simon Fraser University were split into sellers, buyers, and choosers. Sellers were given a mug and stated the lowest price at which they would give it up. Buyers stated the most they would pay. Choosers, who had no mug, picked mug or cash at each price, so they were as rich as the sellers in every way that mattered except that the mug was not yet "theirs."

The medians were $7.12 for sellers, $3.12 for choosers, and $2.87 for buyers. If the mug had been worth the same in every hand, about 12.5 trades should have happened between buyers and sellers. Three did. A repeat at the University of British Columbia, with 117 students and the price tag left on the mug, came out the same way: medians of $7.00, $3.50, and $2.00, and one trade where about 19 were expected. The choosers sat with the buyers, not with the sellers. The gap was a reluctance to give the mug up, not an inability to pay.

A market for tokens, where each person was simply assigned a redemption value by the experimenter, traded at the volume the textbook predicts. In an early Cornell session the induced-value tokens produced 12, 11, and 10 trades against 11 expected. The same people, trading mugs that sold for $6 at the campus store, produced a median selling price of $5.25 against a median buying price around $2.25 to $2.75, and only a handful of trades. Practice rounds did not close the mug gap. Whatever was blocking trade was specific to giving up a thing, not to the rules of the market.

The everyday version is the asking price you put on something you own. A car, a ticket, a piece of furniture, the coat already in the closet: the sum that would make you give it up is larger than the sum you would pay to get the same thing tomorrow. Negotiations stall in that gap. A buyer who offers the price he would pay, and a seller who hears an offer well below what giving it up feels like, can both be reporting an honest number.

Charles Plott and Kathryn Zeiler, in 2005, copied the mug procedure and got the gap again, with medians of $1.50 to pay and $4.50 to accept. When they added anonymity, training, and practice on the selling rule, the gap disappeared. In one of those experiments both medians were $5.00. Part of the famous difference may be confusion about the question, not only a deeper dislike of giving things up.
