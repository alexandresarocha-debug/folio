Background

By 2019 the Justinianic plague had a bacterium and a large historical role. Ancient DNA had identified Yersinia pestis in sixth-century remains. Kyle Harper and others had argued that the pandemic, beginning in 541 and returning in later waves, killed on a scale that reshaped the late Roman economy and weakened the eastern empire before the losses of the seventh century. The literary set pieces, above all Procopius on Constantinople, were read again as demography. Lee Mordechai, Merle Eisenberg, and their colleagues thought the maximal reading had outrun the evidence. Their paper asks a blunt question in the title. Was the Justinianic plague an inconsequential pandemic?

The word inconsequential is a provocation, and it is also a defined claim. They do not mean that nobody died, or that the disease was mild in a city it struck. They mean that the evidence, taken more widely than the famous narratives, does not show a demographic and economic break large enough to explain the empire's later history. A terrible local epidemic and a turning point for a civilisation are different propositions. The paper is an attempt to force them apart.

The question

The question is empirical. If the plague had the mortality and the duration often claimed, then independent records of human activity should move together in the decades after 541. Fewer people should leave fewer dated inscriptions, a thinner trail of documents, a disturbed coinage, a retreat of cereal farming visible in pollen, and a written record in which the disease remains a continuing fact rather than a shocking episode. If those series do not move together, the maximal story is asking a few literary texts to do too much. The authors set out to assemble the series and see.

How the study was built

The design is a comparison of proxies across the eastern Roman world and, where the evidence reaches, beyond it. They gather quantitative and semi-quantitative traces: the chronology of written references to plague, the production of inscriptions, the evidence of the coinage, papyrus documents from Egypt, pollen records that speak to cereal cultivation, and the burial evidence that would mark mass death where it has been looked for. No one of these is a census. The method is the method of convergence. A real, empire-wide collapse of population should be hard to hide in all of them at once. A disaster that is genuine and local should appear in some of them, in some places, and leave others relatively intact.

They also look at time. A pandemic that returns for two centuries should leave a long signature, not a single spike of attention in the 540s. The paper is as interested in the supposed recurrences as in the first outbreak. A disease that contemporaries mention intensely once, and then rarely, is a different historical object from a disease that structures life for generations.

What was found

The authors find that the non-literary series do not line up behind the maximal story. Narrative sources describe a severe plague. Inscriptions, coins, and Egyptian documents, in their reading, show important continuities through the middle of the sixth century. Pollen evidence does not show a uniform abandonment of cereal agriculture timed to the outbreak. Later waves are unevenly attested, and the written habit of talking about plague does not stay at the pitch of the first descriptions. They conclude that the case for a transformative, long-lasting demographic disaster is not supported by this broader evidence. The pandemic can have been locally devastating and still inconsequential for the trajectory of the empire.

The finding is negative, and negative findings need care. They have not produced a new low number for the dead that replaces a high one. They have shown, to their own satisfaction, that several archives a high number ought to disturb are not disturbed in a simple, simultaneous way. The title keeps the question mark. The body of the paper leans towards yes, it was inconsequential at the scale claimed.

What the authors make of it

Their interpretation is about method as much as about 541. Late antique history, they argue, has let a brilliant contemporary account set the magnitude of an event, and has then used that magnitude to explain later political failure. Once the magnitude shrinks to what the other evidence can bear, the explanations have to move. The eastern empire's wars, its fiscal politics, the Persian conflict, and the Arab conquests need causes that are allowed to be political and military. Plague remains part of the sixth century. It stops being the hidden motor of the end of antiquity. They also imply a wider caution about pandemics in history. A disease can fill a chronicle and a cemetery and still not be the reason a state later loses its provinces.

Limits and later criticism

Each proxy has a leak. People set up inscriptions for reasons of fashion and law, not only because they are alive in large numbers. Coinage records the state's output and the survival of coins, not the population. Papyri survive in Egypt because of the desert, and Egypt is one region. Pollen sites are few, and a change in cultivation can have many causes. A patchy plague can kill a great many people in ports and cities and leave a countryside, or a distant province, looking continuous. Absence of a clean break is not a census of survivors. The rhetorical force of the word inconsequential can also hide a more modest result, which is that the evidence is mixed.

Harper and others replied that the paper set an extreme bar, underweighted the biological evidence and the repeated waves, and treated coarse series as if they were sensitive enough to rule out high mortality. The exchange is useful because it cannot be settled by choosing a side in advance. It turns on whether independent archives move together. A reader can think Mordechai and his colleagues were right to demand that test, and also think some of their series are too blunt to register the kind of loss a maximalist describes. Later work has continued on both the genomes and the proxies. The responsible summary is the one the quarrel itself suggests. The pathogen is established. The imperial consequences are not.

Why it matters here

The Folio card harper-climate-plague presents Harper's environmental account and then refuses to let it harden into a settled death toll. It says some historians think the mortality of particular plagues has been overstated, and it names this 2019 paper as the argument that the Justinianic plague had a smaller impact than often claimed. The card's point is that the debate is alive. This paper is why that sentence is more than good manners. It is a concrete alternative reading, built out of inscriptions, coins, papyri, pollen, and the distribution of texts, aimed directly at the maximal use of 541.

The connection to the rest of the card is a division of jobs. The tree-ring study shows a long cold phase. The genomic study shows Yersinia pestis in sixth-century dead. Harper's book puts those facts inside a story of an empire knocked down by nature. Mordechai and his colleagues accept that a plague happened and dispute the historical weight placed on it. A reader who skips this paper will think the card's caution is vague. A reader of the paper can see the actual standard being applied: if the plague remade the empire, the non-literary record should show it, and in their view it does not. That standard can be questioned. It is the right kind of question for the card to leave open.
