Procopius watched the plague in Constantinople in 542, and his pages are the ones later writers quote. Lee Mordechai and colleagues timed him. Plague occupies less than 1 percent of his surviving work. Gregory of Tours, the main witness in Gaul, gives it at most 1.3 percent. Their paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (2019) takes on the claim that this pandemic, from 541 to about 750, killed between a quarter and a half of the people around the Mediterranean and helped end the ancient world. They look for that death toll in records that would have had to move if the population had collapsed.

In Egypt, where the disease entered the Mediterranean at Pelusium, they count 505 papyri dated to a single year between 520 and 570. The yearly total does not fall after 541, and they report no papyrus that clearly mentions the plague. The archive of the Apion family, the best-documented great estate of the age, shows income up by about 30 percent between the 540s and the 580s. In Syria, 288 inscriptions dated to a particular year between 500 and 650 show no dip at the outbreak. They drop in the 610s, when a Persian army conquered the region. Pollen from lakes in Greece, Bulgaria, and Turkey shows no mid-sixth-century crash in cereal farming and no sudden spread of pine across abandoned fields. The sharpest of those records, the layered sediments of Nar lake in Cappadocia, does show cereal and olive pollen collapsing, and the collapse comes at the end of the seventh century, in step with the early Islamic conquests.

A frightening chapter can be true of one city in one season and still be a poor guide to the size of a pandemic. Leases, gravestones, coins, and pollen are duller, and they sit closer to the question of whether fields were left empty and taxes stopped. Peter Sarris, writing in Past & Present in 2021, answered that Egyptian contracts and tax receipts almost never describe disease, so their silence is weak evidence, and that the pool of relevant papyri is far smaller than the "tens of thousands" the paper invokes. He also pointed to plague DNA from a sixth-century cemetery at Edix Hill in Cambridgeshire, which shows the bacterium reached Britain. Presence in a graveyard is not a death rate. The dispute is now about which kind of silence, and which kind of bone, you are willing to treat as a measure of the dead.
