Lee Mordechai, Merle Eisenberg, and their colleagues started from a claim that had become common: the plague that reached Egypt in 541 killed between a quarter and a half of the people of the Mediterranean, tens of millions, and helped end antiquity. Their 2019 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences tests that claim against records that are not narratives. In Egypt, where the outbreak entered the empire, they counted 505 papyri that can be dated to a single year between 520 and 570. The number of documents does not fall after 541, and it does not jump in the way a wave of deaths and new contracts would produce. They found no sign in those papers of abandoned land or of a collapse in tax revenue. On the estates of the Apion family, the best-documented large landlord in the late Roman world, revenue rose by about 30 percent between the 540s and the 580s. They report that they know of no papyrus, among the tens of thousands surviving from the period, that definitely mentions the plague.

The second test is pollen, which records what was growing. In Sweden, cereal pollen drops after the Black Death of the 1340s, as fields went out of cultivation. Across Greece, Bulgaria, and Turkey, the sixth century shows no matching break. Regions that were already losing cereal cultivation keep that trend. Regions that were expanding do not reverse. At Nar lake in Cappadocia, where the mud is layered year by year, cereal and olive pollen crash at the end of the seventh century, in step with the early Islamic conquests, and show no such crash in the decades after 541. The authors also note how little space the famous writers actually gave the disease. Plague takes up less than 1 percent of Procopius and at most 1.3 percent of Gregory of Tours.

The practical point is about what silence and continuity can prove. A contract, a tax receipt, and a lake bed do not record every death. They do record whether a countryside stopped farming and whether a great estate stopped collecting. Mordechai's group reads those series as continuity, and reads the terrifying daily death tolls in Procopius and John of Ephesus as texts too thin, and too rhetorical, to carry a theory of civilizational collapse.

The reply has been equally specific. In 2019 Marcel Keller's team published Yersinia pestis genomes from sixth- and seventh-century burials in Britain, France, Germany, and Spain, including Edix Hill near Cambridge, so the bacterium was present far outside the Egyptian archives. Peter Sarris, in Past & Present in 2022, argued that the minimalist case leaned on the absence of the word "plague" in the papyri and gave too little weight to those genomes and to the narrative descriptions of mass death. The papyri and the pollen are real. So is the DNA. They are evidence about different questions: whether the farming economy broke, and whether the microbe was there.
