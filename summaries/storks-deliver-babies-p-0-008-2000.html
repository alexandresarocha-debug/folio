Statistics textbooks routinely warn that correlation does not imply causation, but Robert Matthews thought their examples were either trivial or lacked real data. So he built a better one from a piece of folklore: that storks deliver babies.

The white stork is common in many parts of Europe, and he found counts of breeding pairs for 17 European countries, covering 1980 to 1990, along with population and birth figures from the Britannica Yearbook for 1990. The table runs from Belgium, with a single pair, to Poland with 30,000 pairs and Turkey with 25,000.

He regressed each country's annual number of births on its number of stork pairs. The correlation coefficient was r = 0.62. With 17 countries, a standard t-test gives t = 3.06 and, with 15 degrees of freedom, a p-value of 0.008. By the usual standard of p below 0.05, the stork theory of birth would be called statistically significant.

The point, of course, is what is missing. Some factor common to both can create a correlation between two things that are not directly linked, just as children's shoe size correlates with reading skill because both grow with age. Matthews suggested land area as one candidate here, since bigger countries have room for more storks and more people, and invited readers to test it with the data in his table.

What this means in real life: a significant p-value tells you a pattern is unlikely to be pure noise, not why it is there. Always ask what else differs between the things being compared.

Caution: this is a short teaching article using country-level figures from different sources and years. Its purpose is to show a confounded correlation, not to study storks or births.
