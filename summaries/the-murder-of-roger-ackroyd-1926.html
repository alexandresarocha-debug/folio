Dr. James Sheppard, the narrator of Agatha Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926), is the village doctor in King's Abbot and the man who helps Hercule Poirot look into the stabbing of Roger Ackroyd. He is also the killer. Mrs. Ferrars has already killed herself, after admitting that she poisoned her husband and that someone was blackmailing her. Ackroyd is about to read her letter naming the blackmailer when Sheppard, who is that man, stabs him. The alibi is a machine. Ackroyd had given Sheppard a dictaphone to repair. Sheppard stabs him, leaves by the front door, puts on Ralph Paton's shoes, and climbs back through the study window, so the footprints point at Ackroyd's stepson. Inside, he sets the dictaphone by the window, timed for half past nine, and pulls a chair out to hide it from the door. The secretary and a house guest later hear Ackroyd's voice and think he is still alive. When the body is found, Sheppard has a few minutes in the study. He writes that he "did what little had to be done." In the last chapter he says what the little was. He shoved the dictaphone into his medical bag and pushed the chair back.

The nasty elegance is that the lying chapter is grammatically true. "The letters were brought in at twenty minutes to nine. It was just on ten minutes to nine when I left him, the letter still unread. I hesitated with my hand on the door handle, looking back and wondering if there was anything I had left undone." He invites the reader to imagine a row of stars between those sentences. The ten minutes are the murder. He never writes a false clock time. He leaves the act out, and he writes the book as if it were going to be the history of one of Poirot's failures. Poirot, retired in the village, works it out and tells him the police will have it in the morning. The closing chapter, "Apologia," is Sheppard finishing the manuscript. Christie had serialized the story the year before, from July to September 1925, in the London Evening News, under the title Who Killed Ackroyd?

The trick changed what a first-person mystery is allowed to hide. A narrator can omit, can choose adjectives, can spend a paragraph on the wrong footprint, and still not type a sentence that is false on its face. Readers who felt cheated were noticing a real shift in the contract. The Scotsman, on 22 July 1926, said the reader would feel he had been "fairly, or unfairly, sold." The New York Times Book Review, on 18 July 1926, called the same device "a perfectly fair procedure," because Christie had left the hints and had not described the doctor as anything but noncommittal. Both responses are about the stars Sheppard did not print. When you trust a voice that says "I" and supplies the times, it is worth asking what the clock is doing in the minutes the voice calls little.
