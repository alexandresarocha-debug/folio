A Watson who keeps the books

Agatha Christie's The Murder of Roger Ackroyd, published in 1926, is a detective novel narrated by Dr James Sheppard, a physician in the village of King's Abbot. Roger Ackroyd, a wealthy man to whom Sheppard has ties of neighbourhood and of medical attendance, is found dead in his study. Hercule Poirot, retired and growing vegetable marrows next door, is drawn into the case. Sheppard becomes his companion and, as it seems, his chronicler, the sensible local man who will write down the investigation in the way Dr Watson wrote down Sherlock Holmes. The village supplies the usual Christie company of suspects: relatives with expectations, a housekeeper, a butler, people with secrets that look motive-shaped. The investigation proceeds by conversation, timetable, and the small physical facts of a study. For most of the book the reader has no reason to think the narrator is anything but the aid and the scribe. The last movement reveals that he is the murderer, and that the book we have been reading is his manuscript, a confession shaped as a case report.

The revelation reorganises every chapter without requiring the chapters to have been full of lies in the ordinary sense. Sheppard's method is omission and implication. He records his movements so that a gap, a period on the night of the murder, does not present itself as a gap. He reports other people's suspicions, including suspicions of himself, with the mildness of a man who has nothing to fear from them. He is present at the discussions in which Poirot thinks aloud, and his presence, which the reader has taken as the narrator's natural privilege, is the murderer's opportunity to know how close the truth has come. The unfairness, if there is unfairness, lies here. The person who decides which sentence follows which sentence is the person with the strongest interest in our misreading. Christie builds the book so that a second reading can see the management. The first reading is designed to feel like help.

A voice that never has to say the false thing

Booth's account of unreliability, written decades later, fits this narrator with a clarity Christie herself did not have the term for. Sheppard does not speak in accordance with the norms of the work, if the norms of a detective novel include the convention that the Watson figure is the reader's friend. He speaks in accordance with the norms of a murderer who would like to be thought the Watson figure. The divergence is the unreliability. It is worth seeing how little of it depends on extravagant misstatement. A narrator who said "I was elsewhere" when he was in the study would be a simple liar, and a simple liar can be caught by contradiction. A narrator who says what he did say, and leaves out the act that would change the meaning of the surrounding sentences, is caught only when the omitted act is supplied from outside, by Poirot. Until that supply, the prose is locally true and globally false. The global falsehood is a structure of gaps. Unreliability here is an editorial act, a way of using the fact that narration is always a selection.

The famous mechanism of the alibi belongs to this editorial art and is part of the plot the book is known for, so it can be described as the design it is. Ackroyd's voice seems to be heard after the time at which he has already been killed, because a recording on a dictaphone has been arranged to speak in a room that no longer contains him. The apparent time of death moves. Sheppard, who has the medical access and the neighbour's access the plot requires, is the person who can make use of that movement, and his narration makes use of it a second time by letting the reader live inside the false timetable. The device is mechanical, as golden-age devices are, and the narration is what makes the mechanism feel like ordinary experience. We do not watch a trick from the stalls. We read a doctor's evening. The trick and the prose style are one design, which is why the book is a landmark in unreliable narration and not only a landmark in puzzle-making.

The quarrel about fair play

The book's notoriety is a quarrel about rules. Detective fiction of the period was beginning to codify fair play, the promise that a reader might in principle solve the crime from what has been shown, without the author depending on a twin, a secret passage never mentioned, or a fact kept wholly off the page. Later decalogues of the rules, associated with writers and critics of the Detection Club's world, are the explicit version of a standard many reviewers were already applying. By that standard, a narrator who conceals his own guilty act can look like a cheat. The reader was not in a fair contest with Poirot, because the reader was being addressed by the criminal in the person of a companion. Defenders of the book answer that Sheppard's omissions are legible, that the clues to his position, his timing, and his interest are present, and that a reader who assumed the narrator must be innocent assumed something the text never promised. The quarrel is not a side issue. It is the critical content of the novel's form. Christie forced a decision about whether the narrator of a detective story is a neutral window or a person, with a person's liberty to withhold.

The decision has consequences beyond the genre. If a narrator can be the culprit, then every first-person narrative carries, as a structural possibility, the possibility that the act of telling is part of the crime or part of the cover. Later literary fiction would use that possibility for moral and psychological ends Christie's puzzle only needs as a spring. The spring is already literary. It depends on our trust in a tone, the tone of a decent provincial doctor, observant, a little dry, properly shocked. Christie understood that the tone was a tool, and that the tool was more powerful than any locked room because readers do not inspect a tone the way they inspect a window latch. The outrage of some contemporary readers is evidence that the tool worked. People who feel cheated have usually been persuaded. The persuasion is Booth's subject, appearing here in a popular form so clean that it can be used as a teaching case. A voice asked for our trust in its capacity as a guide, and the book, in its last pages, shows that the guide was the thing we needed guiding through.

What kind of unreliability this is

Set beside other famous narrators, Sheppard occupies a particular square. He is not naive, reporting horrors he does not recognise as horrors while the book winks over his head. He knows. He is not, primarily, a self-deceiver in the manner of a man who has talked himself into a flattering account of a weak life, though he does like the picture of himself as Poirot's sensible associate. He is a strategist. The manuscript is written to be read, and written to survive a reading by someone who was not in the study. The last chapter, addressed to Poirot after the exposure, changes the audience and therefore changes the voice. Confession becomes possible when concealment has failed. That change is the novel's way of labelling the earlier chapters as rhetoric. They were not the inner truth finally slipping out. They were the public story, composed under threat. Unreliability of this strategic kind asks the reader to be a detective twice, once of the murder and once of the paragraph, and it asks the second job even of readers who do not try to solve the first.

The limit of the case is the limit of a puzzle's psychology. Sheppard's motives are the motives a detective plot needs, money and pressure and the logic of a prior death, and Christie is not engaged, as a later novelist might be, in a long study of how a respectable man lives with a narration that lies. The respectability is a surface the plot requires. Once the surface has done its work, the book ends. Within that limit the formal lesson is complete. A first-person narrative can be fair in its local sentences and unfair in its silences. A companion can be the threat. A reader who trusts the nearest voice is a reader a novelist can use. The convention of trust, the trustworthy doctor with the pen, was the convention Christie spent.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading is the unreliable narrator in several kinds, and this novel is the kind who lies by omission. Booth defined unreliability as a narrator's departure from the norms of the work. Sheppard departs from the deepest norm of the Watson tradition, which is that the friend with the manuscript is on the side of the solution. He writes the investigation of a murder he has committed, uses a false impression of the time of death, and lets his silences do what a false statement would have done more clumsily. The controversy about fair play is the contemporary name for the feeling that this departure breaks a contract. Whether it breaks the contract or merely reveals that the contract was never signed is the argument the book still stages. Set beside Stevens in The Remains of the Day, who misjudges a life he largely tells truly, Sheppard is the other pole: a narrator whose facts are managed because he is guilty, and whose guilt is the key to the plot rather than the tragedy of the character. The idea needs both poles. Distrust of a narrator can mean "this voice cannot see", and it can mean "this voice is hiding the act". Christie is the second lesson, done so cleanly that the lesson and the puzzle are the same machine.
