Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review gathers the ways people seek and read evidence so that it comes out in favor of what they already think. The cleanest demonstration is Peter Wason's rule task from 1960. Twenty-nine undergraduates were told that the triple 2, 4, 6 fit a rule, and were asked to discover the rule by offering further triples. The rule was any three numbers in increasing order. Most people formed a tighter rule, such as "even numbers rising by two," and then tested triples that fit it, such as 8, 10, 12. Those tests can only confirm. They cannot show that the tighter rule is too tight. Six of the 29 announced the correct rule on the first try. Thirteen announced one wrong rule first, nine announced two or more wrong rules, and one announced none.

A second case Nickerson uses is what happens when the evidence is mixed and the belief is already strong. Charles Lord, Lee Ross, and Mark Lepper recruited 24 Stanford students who supported the death penalty and 24 who opposed it, each side already convinced that the research was on their side. Everyone read two studies of whether executions deter murder, one that seemed to find deterrence and one that seemed to find none. Both sides judged the study that agreed with them to be the better conducted. After the methods, the critiques, and the replies, supporters reported that they were more in favor of capital punishment and opponents reported that they were more against it. The same packet widened the gap.

At work this is the project you already want. The slide that helps it looks solid. The slide that hurts it looks flawed, small, or out of date. In a purchase, the review you click is the one that matches the choice you are leaning toward, and a long search can leave you surer than the five minutes before you started. Nickerson's practical point is that the cure is not a general vow to be open. It is a search aimed at the triple that would kill the idea: the case that should be true if you are right and is not, or the study you would have to explain away.

Wason's task has been easy to repeat. The stronger claim, that mixed evidence makes both sides more extreme rather than merely more dismissive of the other study, depends on people reporting how their own view moved, which is the measure Lord, Ross, and Lepper used.
