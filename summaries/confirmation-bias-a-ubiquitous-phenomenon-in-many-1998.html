Background

Confirmation bias is a phrase people use as if it named a single habit, the habit of liking evidence that agrees with them. Raymond Nickerson's 1998 review in the Review of General Psychology is an attempt to slow that usage down. Across philosophy, psychology, medicine, law and ordinary life, researchers had described a family of tendencies: seeking one kind of information rather than another, interpreting a mixed record as support, remembering hits and forgetting misses, and holding a belief more stubbornly than the evidence that remains would justify. Nickerson asks whether these are one phenomenon, how far the evidence really reaches, and where a sensible strategy has been mislabelled as a bias. Folio's card on confirmation bias uses this review as its map.

The question

Is there a pervasive tendency to search, interpret and remember in ways that protect a belief one already holds, and if so, what are its forms? Nickerson is wary of two easy answers. One is that the tendency is so universal that every disagreement is explained. The other is that apparent confirmation-seeking is usually rational, because a positive test of a hypothesis is often the informative test. His question is diagnostic. For each guise of the bias, what is the demonstration, what would the rational strategy have been, and how wide is the gap between them?

How the review is organised

The paper is not an experiment. It is a tour of demonstrations, sorted by what the person does. First comes restricted search. Peter Wason's rule-discovery task, the sequence 2, 4, 6, is the exhibit. People who think the rule is even numbers rising by two test further sequences that fit, and they neglect sequences that would break the hypothesis. Wason's selection task, the four cards, is the companion exhibit. Asked which cards must be turned to test a conditional rule, people turn the card that can confirm it and leave unturned the card that could falsify it. Nickerson treats both as evidence that a search is aimed at what a hypothesis expects, and he records the long argument about whether that aim is foolish.

Next comes interpretation. The same mixed body of evidence persuades people who already disagree in opposite directions, each of them finding the congenial part more telling. Studies of belief persistence, in which a basis for a belief is discredited and the belief partly remains, belong here, as do clinical and legal settings in which a working hypothesis makes later information look like corroboration. Then comes memory. Nickerson asks whether people preferentially remember confirming instances. The record, as he reads it, is real but less tidy than the record on search. People do not simply store one side and delete the other. They weight, rehearse and reconstruct, and the bias in memory is often a bias in what was encoded because it was attended to in the first place.

The last sections leave the laboratory. Science, he argues, has built institutions, peer review, replication, and a culture of challenge, partly because individual scientists are not immune to favouring their own hypothesis. Those institutions work imperfectly. Medicine accumulates cases that fit a diagnosis. Lawyers build the file for their side. Ordinary people give more scrutiny to an argument against a belief than to an argument for it, a pattern later writers call myside bias. Nickerson's method throughout is to set a concrete task beside the loose slogan, and to say which inference the task supports.

What he concludes

He concludes that the tendency is real, widespread, and many-shaped. People seek evidence that their hypothesis would lead them to expect. They interpret ambiguous evidence as fitting. They require more from evidence against a view than from evidence for it. The phenomenon survives changes of domain, which is the sense in which he calls it ubiquitous. He does not conclude that people never look for trouble, or that groups and institutions cannot correct the individual. The review is full of occasions on which a design, a training or a norm of scepticism reduced the bias. Ubiquitous, in his usage, means recurrent across guises, not irresistible in every case.

The most useful distinction he draws, borrowing from earlier critics of the Wason tradition, is between a positive test strategy and a bias properly so called. Testing a case you expect to fit is often the efficient way to learn. If you think a substance is an acid, you try it on a reagent that acids affect. Calling every such test confirmation bias would indict ordinary competence. The bias begins where a test that could challenge the hypothesis is available, inexpensive and ignored, or where contrary evidence is explained away by a special pleading that would not be accepted in the hypothesis's favour. Nickerson's review is, in large part, a sorting of the literature into those two piles, and an admission that some famous demonstrations sit near the border.

Limits

A review reflects the examples it was possible to assemble in 1998, and it reflects Nickerson's judgement about which of them deserve the name. He is an honest judge, and he is still one judge. Studies that failed to find a bias are easier to underweight in a narrative than in a meta-analysis, and this paper is a narrative. It does not produce a pooled estimate of how large the tendency is, because the tasks are too unlike for a single number to mean anything. Anyone who quotes the review as if it had measured a coefficient of confirmation has misread the genre.

There is a conceptual limit he states and cannot escape. To call a search biased one needs a standard of the search that should have been done. That standard is obvious in a logic puzzle with a correct falsifying card. It is not obvious in science, where nobody knows which experiment is the decisive one, or in politics, where the parties disagree about what would count as contrary evidence. The cleaner the laboratory task, the less it resembles the beliefs people will not give up. The more it resembles those beliefs, the harder it is to prove that the person handled the evidence wrongly rather than differently.

Replications and critiques

Wason's tasks have been replicated, varied and partly reinterpreted for decades. The basic observation, that people in the 2-4-6 task test instances that fit a narrow rule, is sturdy. The moral, that this is a general failure to try to falsify, has been qualified. Klayman and Ha argued that a positive test is a reasonable default, and that the 2-4-6 task makes it look like a vice because the experimenter's rule is unusually broad. The selection task, too, becomes much easier when the rule is a social obligation rather than an abstract conditional, which suggests that the original difficulty is partly about the material, not a pure thirst for confirmation. Nickerson registers these qualifications. A reader who wants the bias to be a simple sin will find the review disappointingly careful.

Later work has split the slogan into pieces Nickerson already suspected were distinct. Myside bias, the soft handling of one's own arguments, has a large experimental literature of its own. Motivated reasoning, in which the conclusion one wants guides the scrutiny one applies, overlaps confirmation bias without being the same claim. Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber, in the paper and the book paired with this card, offer an explanation Nickerson does not: that the imbalance is what reasoning is for, if reasoning evolved to argue. Their account needs his catalogue. Without a documented family of imbalances, an evolutionary story about argumentation would have nothing to explain. The criticism they invite, and that Nickerson's caution already suggests, is that a successful institution, a laboratory, a court, a newsroom, exists precisely because the individual tendency is not the last word.

Why it matters here

The card defines confirmation bias as the tendency to seek, interpret and remember in ways that support what we already believe, and it points to Nickerson for the breadth of that claim across science, law, medicine and everyday life. This review is the breadth. It is also the warning, built into the card's own later move, not to treat every confirming test as irrational. Read Wason's 1960 paper for the task that made the idea vivid. Read Nickerson before you export that task to every disagreement you have ever had. Then read Mercier and Sperber, who take the imbalance Nickerson documents and ask why a mind would be built to argue for its own side. The review is the inventory. The argumentative theory is one answer to why the inventory is so long.
