Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky gave students and faculty a choice, with the sums in Israeli pounds. A family's median net monthly income at the time was about 3,000 pounds, so the stakes were a month's pay. Of 95 people offered a sure 3,000 or an 80 percent chance of 4,000, 80 percent took the sure 3,000. The gamble's average payoff is 3,200, so the sure thing was the worse bet on paper. Of 95 people offered a 20 percent chance of 4,000 or a 25 percent chance of 3,000, 65 percent took the thinner chance of the larger prize. Scaling both options down by the same amount should not have reversed the choice. More than half the people who faced the pair made the combination that expected-utility theory forbids, because one of the first options had been certain and people overweighted certainty.

The mirror image, losses, reversed the preference again. Of 95 people, 92 percent preferred an 80 percent chance of losing 4,000 to a sure loss of 3,000, even though the gamble's average loss is 3,200, the worse deal. On the scaled-down pair, 58 percent of 95 preferred a 25 percent chance of losing 3,000 to a 20 percent chance of losing 4,000. Faced with gains, people locked in the sure thing. Faced with losses, they rolled the dice to avoid a certain loss. The same pattern turned up with students and faculty at the University of Stockholm and the University of Michigan.

Kahneman and Tversky read both results as evidence that people judge changes from a reference point, not final wealth, and that losses from that point weigh more than gains of the same size. The value of a gain flattens as the gain gets larger. The pain of a loss does too, but the curve is steeper on the way down. A sure gain is precious. A sure loss is so unwelcome that a worse gamble looks better.

That is why a bonus described as money you will definitely receive beats a larger bonus you might not, and why a repair you will definitely have to pay for is harder to accept than a chance of a bigger bill later. A discount framed as money you fail to lose can move a shopper more than the same discount framed as a gain. The choices in the paper were hypothetical, which the authors flag, but the direction has been the working assumption of a great deal of pricing, medicine, and negotiation since.

A 2018 review by David Gal and Derek Rucker argued that "losses loom larger" is not a general law. When obtaining a good and keeping a good both required an action, people did not pay more to avoid the loss. The certainty result above, the sure 3,000 beating the better gamble, is a separate finding and has been easier to repeat.
