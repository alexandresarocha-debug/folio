Background

Expected-utility theory was, in 1979, the orthodox account of decisions under risk. A rational person, on that account, weighs each outcome by its probability and by the utility of the wealth it produces, and then chooses the option with the higher sum. The theory had been built to satisfy axioms that sounded undeniable, among them that people care about final states and that equivalent descriptions should not flip a preference. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky had spent the decade showing that ordinary choices violate those axioms in regular patterns. Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk, published in Econometrica, is the paper in which they replaced the orthodox formula with one built to fit the patterns.

It is not the Asian-disease paper. That framing demonstration belongs to 1981. The 1979 paper is about risky prospects, mostly hypothetical sums of money, and about the shape of a value function and a probability weight. Folio's card on prospect theory takes its three features from here: reference points, a different attitude to gains and to losses, and a warped sensitivity to probability. Loss aversion is part of the theory, stated here as a shape rather than as a measured multiplier.

The question

Which departures from expected utility are systematic enough to deserve a theory, and what is the smallest change in the formula that will capture them? Kahneman and Tversky were not trying to show that people are careless. Particular violations had been known for years, above all the paradoxes associated with Maurice Allais, in which a sure thing is treated as more than its probability warrants. The open question was whether those violations, the reversal of risk attitude between gains and losses, and the appeal of long shots and of insurance, were one family. If they were, a single value function and a single weighting function might organise them.

How the studies were set up

The evidence is a series of choice problems, given mostly to students and university faculty. Each problem offers two prospects. People choose one. The problems come in pairs, and the pairs are the point. A pair is built so that expected-utility theory requires the same choice in both, because one pair is the other with a common consequence cancelled, or with every outcome sign-reversed from gain to loss. If people switch, the axiom fails, and the direction of the switch is the finding.

In the problems that display the certainty effect, one option is a sure gain and the other is a larger gain with a high probability and a small chance of nothing. A companion problem scales both probabilities down, so that neither option is certain, while preserving the ratio between them. In the problems that display the reflection effect, the same numbers are offered as losses. A sure loss stands against a larger loss with a high probability of occurring and a chance of losing nothing. Further problems put a small probability on a large gain, the shape of a lottery, or on a large loss, the shape of insurance. A separate set uses two-stage games. A first stage may end the game with nothing. If it does not, the person faces a second-stage choice whose probabilities, once multiplied through, match a single-stage problem already asked. The question is whether people notice the multiplication.

What they found

Faced with a sure gain of 3,000 against an 80 per cent chance of 4,000, a majority preferred the sure gain. Faced with a 25 per cent chance of 3,000 against a 20 per cent chance of 4,000, a majority preferred the chance of the larger sum. Expected-utility theory cannot have both majorities. The second problem is a scaled version of the first, and the preference should have travelled with the scaling. It did not. Certainty carried extra weight. The same pair, written as losses, reversed. A majority would now gamble, accepting an 80 per cent chance of losing 4,000 rather than a sure loss of 3,000. People were risk-averse for gains and risk-seeking for losses. Kahneman and Tversky called the reversal the reflection effect.

Small probabilities were overweighted in the other direction. A small chance of a large gain was attractive beyond what its expected value suggested, which is the shape of a lottery ticket. A small chance of a large loss was aversive enough to make a sure payment for removing it attractive, which is the shape of insurance. The same person can buy both, and the theory was built to allow that, because a probability weight that rises too fast near zero inflates both the hope and the fear. In the two-stage games, people chose as if the first stage had not happened. They compared the second-stage prospects and ignored the common chance of being knocked out before the second stage began. The authors called this the isolation effect. Shared structure was discarded. The choice was made on the part that differed.

How the authors read the results

Prospect theory splits the decision into an editing phase and an evaluation phase. In editing, people simplify. They set a reference point, usually the status quo, and recode outcomes as gains and losses around it. They cancel components that the two options share. They round awkward probabilities and drop outcomes that look negligible. Editing is not a small preface. The isolation effect is what editing looks like when a shared first stage is cancelled and a sure thing inside the remainder is then overweighted.

Evaluation uses two functions. The value function is defined on gains and losses, not on final wealth. It is concave for gains, so a sure gain is preferred to a gamble of equal expected value, and convex for losses, so a gamble is preferred to a sure loss. It is steeper for losses than for gains, which is loss aversion stated qualitatively: a loss of a given size moves value more than a gain of that size. The weighting function transforms probabilities. It gives small probabilities more weight than they deserve and moderate and high probabilities less, and it does not behave well at the endpoints, which is the mathematical face of the certainty effect. People do not multiply value by probability. They multiply value by a weight that is only loosely a probability.

The reference point is the part with the longest reach. Because value depends on changes, the same final wealth can be a gain or a loss depending on what was expected, and the choice will switch. That is the seed of framing, though the disease problem that made framing famous was still two years away. It is also the seed of the endowment effect. A mug one owns is a loss if given up, and only a gain if acquired, so the selling price and the buying price need not match. The 1979 paper does not run the mug markets. It supplies the reason later experiments expected those markets to fail.

Limits

Almost all of the choices were hypothetical, and the sums, though carefully paired, were not life-changing. People choosing among imaginary payments can be playing a different game from people choosing among real ones, and later work has had to check which patterns survive real stakes. The respondents were students and academics, which was ordinary for the field and is still a narrow base for a theory that soon migrated into finance, law and medicine. Editing, meanwhile, is flexible enough to be a danger. If the theorist can decide after the fact which components people cancelled and which reference point they used, almost any choice can be made to fit. The discipline comes from predicting the coding in advance, and the paper is at its strongest where the pair of problems forces the coding.

The original formula also had a technical flaw that Kahneman and Tversky later repaired. Cumulative prospect theory, published in 1992, rebuilds the probability weighting so that it applies to cumulative chances, and that version became the one economists usually mean. The 1979 equations are the founding statement, not the last word on the mathematics.

Replications and critiques

The certainty effect and the reflection effect have been observed again and again, in money and in other goods, and they are among the more durable findings in the heuristics tradition. The Asian-disease problem of 1981 is a descendant, not a member of this paper, and its replication record belongs with framing. What has not settled is the generality of loss aversion. Gal and Rucker argued in 2018 that many classic demonstrations can be explained by inertia or by a preference for the status quo, and that a loss does not loom larger than a gain in anything like every setting. Others have answered with large-sample evidence that, in risky choice at least, losses still weigh more. The 1979 paper asserts the asymmetry as a feature of the value function. It does not, by itself, settle how large the asymmetry is, or how often it appears outside carefully built gambles.

A further critique concerns the reference point. The theory says value is relative, then often assumes the reference point is the status quo, though it can be an expectation or a goal. That gap invites later work. The piece hardest to dislodge is simpler. People evaluate changes, they treat gains and losses differently, and they do not take probabilities at face value.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Prospect theory: losses loom larger than gains, is a reading of this paper, with the caution, stated on the card, that the size of loss aversion is contested. The concave and convex value function, the qualitative fact of loss aversion, and the overweighting of small probabilities are here, and so is the reason one person might buy both a ticket and a policy. The endowment effect, the disposition effect, and Kahneman's Nobel are consequences and honours that came after. Gal and Rucker, the mug experiments, and Thaler's memoir are the card's further reading because each pressures a different part of the theory: whether losses really loom, whether ownership changes price in the way the value function predicts, and how the whole argument fought its way into economics. Read the 1979 article for the formula and the choice problems. Keep the disease problem in 1981, where it belongs.
