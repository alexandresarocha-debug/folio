In the summer of 1935 a man who could not read sang an epic poem into a recording machine in Bijelo Polje, in what is now Montenegro. His name was Avdo Međedović, and he is the best witness for what Albert Lord argued in The Singer of Tales.

Lord, a student of the classicist Milman Parry, had gone with Parry to Bosnia and Montenegro to record the guslari, singers who accompany themselves on a one-stringed instrument called the gusle. They collected thousands of discs. The Parry Collection of the 1930s has 3,584 twelve-inch records, and nearly one-seventh of them, 499, are conversations with and songs by Avdo. Avdo had a repertory of 58 epics. Parry recorded nine on discs and had four more taken down in dictation.

Two episodes in the book show what the singers actually do. In one, Lord asked Avdo to sing a song he had heard only once, from another singer, Mumin Vlahovljak. Mumin's song, Bećiragić Meho, ran 2,294 lines. After one hearing Avdo sang it back, but he made it almost three times longer: 6,313 lines. In the other, Avdo had learned The Wedding of Meho, Son of Smail from a friend who read a printed pamphlet to him five or six times. The printed text had about 2,160 lines. Avdo's version was about 12,300 lines in 1935, and when Lord recorded him again in 1950 it was 8,488 lines. The opening scene, an assembly of lords in the city of Kanidža, takes 141 lines in the printed version and 1,053 in Avdo's. Lord wrote that Avdo could sing songs of about the length of Homer's Odyssey.

From this, Lord argued that the singers do not memorise poems word for word. They learn a large stock of formulas, which are phrases that fit the line, and of themes, which are standard scenes such as a council, an arming or a journey. Each performance is a new composition built from those parts. The song is not a fixed text. It is more like a language the singer speaks.

What this means in real life: much of what we call a text may be only one performance of a living tradition, and memory and creativity are not opposites. A good musician improvising in a style, or a speaker giving a talk they have given many times, is doing something similar.

Criticism: the Balkan model is one example, and critics such as Ruth Finnegan have said it should not be applied to all oral poetry. Showing that Homer-like poems can be composed aloud does not show that the Iliad was.

Source. Lord, A. B. (1960). The Singer of Tales. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. Second edition ed. Stephen Mitchell and Gregory Nagy, 2000; third edition ed. David F. Elmer, Center for Hellenic Studies, 2019.
