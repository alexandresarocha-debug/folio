A book revised across a life

Mikhail Bakhtin first published his study of Dostoevsky in 1929, under the title Problems of Dostoevsky's Art. He returned to it decades later. The 1963 book, Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics, is that return: a revised and enlarged argument, written after years in which Bakhtin had worked far from the centre of Soviet literary life. English readers usually meet the 1963 text in Caryl Emerson's translation, which also prints a short preface by Bakhtin, fragments from the 1929 edition, and notes he made in 1961 while planning the reworking. The book is therefore both a reading of Dostoevsky and a record of a critic thinking the same thought again, with more patience and a wider frame.

The claim that organises the whole is announced early. Dostoevsky, Bakhtin argues, invented a kind of novel in which several consciousnesses stand with their own rights. He calls this the polyphonic novel, borrowing the musical image of independent voices sounding together. In the novels he has in mind, Crime and Punishment, The Idiot, Demons, and The Brothers Karamazov, the characters who carry ideas are allowed to speak those ideas to the end. Raskolnikov's theory of extraordinary men, Ivan Karamazov's rebellion, the underground man's spite and confession: these are fully voiced positions. The book asks what that does to plot, to the author's place, and to the sentence itself.

What earlier critics had made of him

The opening chapter is a long argument with the criticism already surrounding Dostoevsky. Bakhtin reads the major Russian accounts and finds a repeated habit. Critics had treated the novels as a vessel for Dostoevsky's own religious idea, or as a psychological clinic, or as a social document of the 1860s and 1870s. Each of those readings, he says, looks for a last word standing above the characters. Bakhtin thinks the novels refuse that last word. The author's design is real, and it is a design of a special kind: the design is to set voices side by side so that none of them is converted into an illustration of the author's private conclusion.

This is the hardest part of the book for a reader who wants a simple moral. Polyphony is a claim about form. It says that the structure of these novels keeps more than one evaluation in play, and that the plot does not step in at the end to award a prize to the author's favourite. A character can be judged by other characters, and can judge himself, and the judgement remains inside the dialogue. When Bakhtin calls the voices unmerged, he means that the novel does not melt them into a single wise summary. The reader leaves The Brothers Karamazov with Ivan's argument still standing, even after the stories, the trial, and the elder Zosima have answered it in their own ways.

The hero who will not become an object

The second chapter turns from critics to the hero. Bakhtin describes Dostoevsky's central figures as people who are present to themselves, endlessly commenting on what they are and what others say they are. They are poor material for a finished character sketch, because a sketch would fix them, and they experience every fixing word as something to answer. The underground man is the clearest case, though the chapter's reach is wider. A hero of this kind is always in the act of having the last word about himself, and always discovering that someone else, or he himself a moment later, has another word waiting.

The third chapter follows the idea as it lives in such a person. In Dostoevsky an idea is rarely a proposition parked in a narrator's essay. It is something a person suffers, tests, and uses on other people. Ivan's article, Raskolnikov's published theory, the underground man's little speeches: the idea enters the room as a voice, and other voices push back. Bakhtin treats this as the novel's real philosophical method. Dostoevsky thinks by arranging collisions. The collision is the thought. A reader who extracts a single doctrine and calls it Dostoevsky's has, on this account, stepped outside the form that made the doctrine worth writing down.

Carnival, and the kinds of speech

The fourth chapter is the most historical. Bakhtin asks where a novel of this shape could have come from, and he answers with a long account of serio-comic traditions: the Socratic dialogue, Menippean satire, and the carnival sense of the world. Carnival, as he describes it, turns ranks upside down for a limited time. The beggar plays the king, the solemn is laughed at, the body is acknowledged, death and birth sit at the same table. He thinks Dostoevsky inherits that freedom of contact. Strangers meet and speak of ultimate things at once. Scandals, threshold scenes, public confessions, and dreams in which a person meets his double all belong to this inheritance. The plot of a Dostoevsky novel, on this reading, is less a slow social chronicle than a series of crises in which voices are forced into the open.

The fifth chapter, the longest, comes down to the sentence. Bakhtin sorts prose discourse into kinds. Some words point straight at their object. Some words treat another person's speech as an object to be described from outside, in a single tone. A third kind is double-voiced: two intentions occupy one stretch of language. Parody does this. So does the hidden argument with an imagined listener. So does the word that leaves itself a loophole, a reservation that will let the speaker deny the confession a moment later. Dostoevsky's narration, Bakhtin argues, is saturated with this third kind. Even when a narrator seems to be telling us facts, the telling is bent towards someone's evaluation, and towards the answer that someone is expected to give.

The underground man as the method in miniature

Notes from Underground is the short work in which a reader can see the method without the bulk of the great novels. The speaker addresses an imagined audience that is already sneering. He boasts, then spoils the boast. He tells the story of the officer on the Nevsky, the disastrous dinner, and the evening with Liza, and every anecdote is told by a man who hears how it will be used against him. Bakhtin gives this habit a name that has become famous in later criticism: the word with a sideways glance. The sentence looks at its object and, at the same time, looks sideways at the person who is about to misunderstand it. That double look is why the novella feels so unstable, and why it is a poor source for quotations meant to represent Dostoevsky's settled views. The underground man is settling nothing. He is holding a position open by attacking it himself.

The same habit scales up. In Crime and Punishment, Porfiry and Raskolnikov conduct a conversation in which each anticipates the other's next move, and the narration lets us hear that anticipation. In The Brothers Karamazov, the chapters of rebellion and of the Grand Inquisitor are immense speeches that still expect an answer, and the novel gives answers that do not cancel the speeches. Bakhtin's conclusion is short relative to the chapters that feed it. He thinks Dostoevsky changed what a novel can be responsible for. A novel can be a field in which persons remain persons, and in which the author's mastery shows itself as the power to keep their words intact.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on polyphony takes this 1963 book as its source, and it sends the reader towards Notes from Underground as the place to hear a sideways glance at full length. The card's claim is that Dostoevsky's people are independent voices, and that dialogism names a wider fact about language: every utterance answers something and expects an answer. Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics is where that claim is worked out in detail, chapter by chapter, from the argument with earlier critics to the classification of double-voiced speech. The Dialogic Imagination, written earlier and published in English later, spreads the same habits of thought across the history of the novel and across language itself. Read this book when you want the claim tested against one writer's pages. The test is concrete. Open a confession, a scandal, or a debate in Dostoevsky, and ask whether the novel has left the speaker a last word of his own.
