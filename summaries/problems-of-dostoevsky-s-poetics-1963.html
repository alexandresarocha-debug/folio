In Dostoevsky's first novel, Poor Folk, the poor clerk Makar Devushkin reads Gogol's "The Overcoat" and takes it personally. Mikhail Bakhtin, in Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics, says Devushkin recognizes himself in Akaky Akakievich and is outraged. His poverty has been spied on, his whole life analyzed, described, and finished. "And here now all one's private and public life is being dragged into literature, it is all printed, read, laughed and gossiped about!" He is especially offended that Akaky dies unchanged, the same man he always was, with nothing left over. Bakhtin calls this a revolt against a literature that turns a person into an object. Gogol can show you the coat, the bald spot, the clerk, and close the case. Dostoevsky, even this early, puts that finished portrait where the hero can see it, hate it, and answer it. "We see not who he is, but how he is conscious of himself."

The letters in Poor Folk already behave this way before anyone mentions Gogol. Devushkin writes to Varenka and, after almost every word, looks sideways at how she will take it. He is afraid she will think he is complaining. He tries in advance to undo the impression that will be made by the news that he lives in the kitchen. The word "kitchen" arrives in his sentence from the imagined listener, not from a narrator labeling a room. Bakhtin's name for a novel built like this is polyphonic. The author does not keep the last word. The characters' voices are not illustrations of a truth the author already holds. They argue, and the argument is not settled by a description from above. He contrasts this with what he calls the monologic novel, in which the hero remains an object of the author's discourse. The 1963 book is a revision of a book he published in 1929, Problems of Dostoevsky's Art. The claim about Devushkin is already the claim about Raskolnikov, Ivan, and the underground man. A person, in this art, cannot be "totally quantified, measured, and defined to the last detail."

The practical consequence is a test of whether your characters can embarrass you. If every voice in a scene could be predicted from the point you set out to make, you have written the monologue Bakhtin heard in the usual novel, however many people are talking. If a character can read your portrait of him and call it slander, and if that protest changes the page, you are closer to the polyphony he found in Dostoevsky. This is not a requirement that novels be arguments, or that authors pretend to have no opinions. Bakhtin's Dostoevsky has a design. The design is to leave the hero's word standing, unfinished, next to other words that do not absorb it. Readers know the difference. Some books use people. Some books let people answer.
