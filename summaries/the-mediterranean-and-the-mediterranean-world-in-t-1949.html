A book written against the event

Fernand Braudel’s The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II is one of the few history books that changed what historians thought a subject could be. The first edition appeared in 1949. A heavily revised second edition appeared in 1966, and the English translation most readers meet follows that later text. Much of it was conceived while Braudel was a prisoner of war in Germany, working, as he later described it, from a prodigious memory and from the notes he had been allowed to keep, in an argument conducted silently with the history of events that the war itself seemed to embody. The book takes Philip II’s Mediterranean, the sea of Lepanto, of the Spanish and Ottoman empires, of corsairs and grain fleets, and refuses to tell it as the story of Philip II. It is organised instead as three histories piled on top of one another, moving at three speeds. The argument is that the speeds are not a literary device. They are a claim about causation. What changes slowly constrains what changes at a medium pace, and both together dwarf the apparent freedom of a battle, a treaty, or a king.

Braudel was writing inside, and then leading, the Annales tradition associated with Lucien Febvre and Marc Bloch, which had already declared war on a history made only of politics and of great men. His Mediterranean is the demonstration of what that war looked like when it was won on a huge scale. Geography, climate, demography, prices, and trade are not the background to the narrative. They are the narrative. Philip appears late, and when he appears he is powerful in the way a swimmer is powerful, able to choose his strokes inside a current he did not make and cannot leave.

Geographical time

The first part is the history of the environment, what Braudel made famous under the name longue durée. Mountains, plateaux, plains, peninsulas, islands, and the sea itself are treated as actors with very long lives. A mountain range is a barrier, a refuge, and a way of life. It produces pastoralism, emigration, and a certain independence from the states of the plain, and it does so in the sixteenth century much as it had done for centuries before and would do for centuries after. The plain produces grain, malaria, great estates, and the taxation on which empires sit. The coast produces ports and the temptation of the sea. Braudel’s Mediterranean is not one climate and one culture. It is a ring of contrasting landscapes held together by water, and the water is itself various: a sequence of seas, of winds, of seasons in which one may sail and seasons in which one may not. Winter is a historical force in this part of the book, because it stops fleets. So is distance. An order from Madrid or from Istanbul travels at the speed of a ship or a horse, and the time it takes is not colour. It is a limit on what an emperor can intend.

The method of this first part is to make human choices look smaller without making them unreal. People choose crops, routes, and settlements, and the menu of choices is written by geology and by the season. Transhumance, the seasonal movement of flocks, is one of his recurring figures for a life adapted to a constraint so old that it feels like nature rather than history. The longue durée is not an excuse for ignoring politics. It is a demand that politics be set on a stage whose dimensions have been measured. A reader who finds this part slow is having the correct experience. The slowness is the point. He is trying to induce the sense of a time in which a human lifetime is an incident.

Social time

The second part accelerates into what he calls social time, or the time of conjunctures: rhythms measured in decades and generations rather than in centuries or in days. Here the actors are economies, states, societies, and civilisations. Empires, the Spanish Habsburg complex and the Ottoman state above all, are treated as structures that rise and strain and spend themselves, not as the biographies of their rulers. Trade routes, the movement of grain, the arrival of American silver and its passage through a Mediterranean economy that silver did not simply enrich, the movement of prices, the size of cities, the patterns of banditry and of war as a social institution: these are the events of this middle speed. Braudel’s famous interest in the quantitative, in serial evidence where he can find it, lives here. A price series is a way of watching a conjuncture that no single merchant intended.

Civilisation, a word he uses without irony, means for him a long-lived package of techniques, beliefs, and habits, Christian and Muslim above all in this sea, which outlast governments and which meet along coasts in relations of exchange, conversion, and hostility that a diplomatic history will misread if it only counts treaties. The second part is where a reader who wants the economic Mediterranean will live. It is also where the book’s ambition is most exposed to later criticism. The serial evidence was uneven. The Ottoman side of the archive was much less open to him than the Spanish, and a Mediterranean seen from Simancas is not quite the same sea as a Mediterranean seen from Istanbul. The conjuncture can also become a metaphor that explains too much. When every political decision is the expression of a price curve, the price curve has become a new kind of king. Braudel at his best does not do this. He sets the curve beside the decision and asks the decision to notice the curve.

The history of events, and Philip

The third part is the history he had dethroned, and he writes it anyway, at length: the politics of the second half of the sixteenth century, the war between Spain and the Ottomans, Lepanto, the diplomacy, the truces, the deaths of princes, Philip II himself. He compares this layer to foam on the waves. The comparison has been resented, because foam is not what the dead at Lepanto were, and because a history that calls politics superficial has to be careful not to become a history that excuses itself from understanding politics. In practice the third part is detailed, concrete, and full of individuals. The theoretical demotion does not stop Braudel from being an excellent narrative historian when he allows himself the mode. What the demotion changes is the moral. Philip is a hard-working, intelligent, abundantly documented monarch whose intentions are continually revised by distance, by empty treasuries, by revolts, and by the season. To understand him is to watch a man at the top of a system discovering the system’s limits. The limits were the subject of the first two parts. The third part is their proof, in the form of a reign.

Lepanto is the test case. As an event it was a spectacular Christian naval victory. As a conjuncture it did not destroy Ottoman sea power, did not unify Christendom, and did not end the structural stalemate between two empires that both had too much sea to hold. Braudel’s reading of such battles is the reading his whole architecture enforces. The event is real, it matters to the people in it, and its consequences are bounded by structures the event cannot rewrite. Later historians who returned to political and military narrative, and who insisted that events can break structures, were reacting against this bound. They were not imagining it. The bound is the book’s thesis.

What to keep, after a lifetime of argument

The Mediterranean has been criticised from every direction a great book attracts. Geographers have found the environmentalism too stable, underplaying climate variation and the ways people engineer their landscapes. Ottomanists have rewritten the eastern sea with sources he did not use. Historians of events have rehabilitated the political decision. Historians of the Mediterranean itself, notably Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell in The Corrupting Sea, have argued that his sea is too unified, too much a single world of Philip II, and not enough a mosaic of tiny regions whose connections flicker. His later work, above all the three volumes of Civilization and Capitalism, took the same layered method to the whole early modern economy, and some readers prefer it as economic history. None of this has retired the tool the 1949 book put into everyone’s hand. It is still a discipline to ask, of any story that is moving very fast, which slower storey it sits on, and whether the apparent cause is a cause or a foam. The discipline can be mechanical. In Braudel’s own hands it was a way of giving the sea, the harvest, and the price of bread their due against the vanity of courts.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on Braudel’s three speeds of time is a summary of this book’s architecture: geographical time, social time, and the time of events, with Philip II arriving late and looking almost powerless against the slower forces. The card’s advice, to ask of a news story which layer it belongs to, is the book reduced to a habit, and the habit is fair to him. The card also points to The Corrupting Sea as the modern successor, which is right if the word successor includes disagreement. Horden and Purcell keep the Mediterranean and the long duration, and they break Braudel’s unified sea into micro-regions and fragile connections. Read Braudel for the original experience of scale, the sense that a political history of the sixteenth century is a thin layer on top of a physical and economic history, and for the set of the three speeds, which is still the cleanest statement of the idea. Read him remembering that the third speed, the foam, is still part of the book. He did not skip Lepanto. He made Lepanto share a binding with the mountains.
