Fernand Braudel was called up in 1939 and captured by the Germans in the spring of 1940. He spent the next five years in prisoner-of-war camps, first at Mainz and then at Lübeck, and there he drafted, largely from memory, The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II. The book was published in Paris in 1949. He said later that the news pouring in from the enemy's radio and newspapers was something he had to outdistance and refuse. "Down with occurrences, especially vexing ones." The war he was living through was all occurrences. The history he wrote was an argument that occurrences are the surface.

The book is in three layers, and they move at different speeds. The first is the sea itself: mountains, plains, winds, routes, the almost unchanging constraints on anyone who lives around the Mediterranean. The second is the slower social time of economies, states, and empires, measured in decades and generations, including the long rise of prices in the sixteenth century. The third is events. Battles, treaties, and the letters of Philip II sit in that third layer, and Braudel treats them as foam on a much heavier sea. Philip, ruling from Spain over an empire whose messages took weeks or months, arrives late in the book and looks nearly powerless against the distances and the structures around him.

Lepanto is the event he uses to show what foam can and cannot do. On 7 October 1571 the fleet of the Holy League defeated the Ottoman fleet in a single day's fighting and destroyed a large part of it. The news was immense. Cyprus, which the Ottomans had taken in 1570 and 1571, stayed Ottoman. A new Ottoman fleet was at sea for the next campaigning season. Spain's attention drifted toward the Atlantic. The battle changed reputations and it did not change the shape of the two empires or the limits the sea set on both of them. A historian who stops at the victory has, on Braudel's terms, described the spray and missed the water.

The habit is useful whenever a famous day is asked to stand for a whole change. Sort the speeds before deciding what mattered. A battle can be real, costly, and celebrated, and still leave the map of trade, distance, and power where it was. A mountain range, a sailing time, or a century of prices can bind kings who believed they were deciding. The mistake is to let the loudest afternoon do the work of the constraint.

Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell, in The Corrupting Sea (2000), kept the long view and rejected the picture of one sea acting on its peoples. Their unit is the small district, linked to the next district by short coastal trips. The Mediterranean, they argue, was made by that traffic, not handed to its inhabitants as a climate and a destiny. Braudel's geography is too much of a cause, and the people on the boats are too little of one.
