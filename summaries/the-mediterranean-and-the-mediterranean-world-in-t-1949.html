Fernand Braudel was captured on 29 June 1940 and held first at Oflag XII-B in Mainz, then at Oflag X-C in Lübeck. He wrote La Méditerranée et le Monde Méditerranéen à l'Époque de Philippe II in school exercise books and sent the notebooks to Lucien Febvre. The book was published in 1949, when he was forty-seven. The conditions of the writing are part of the method. Without the archive in front of him, he organized the sea by the speed of change rather than by the order of the files. He distinguishes three times. The slowest is geographical time, almost immobile: mountains, plains, the length of the coast, the winter closure of shipping. The middle speed is the time of economies, states, societies, and trade routes. The fastest is the time of events, of battles, letters, and kings. Philip II of Spain, who reigned from 1556 to 1598, sits in the third.

Lepanto is his example of how little the third time can do on its own. On 7 October 1571 the fleet of the Holy League, commanded by Don John of Austria, destroyed the Ottoman galleys off the Greek coast. Philip, in Spain, learned of the victory weeks later. A galley war had a season. Winter shut the sea, and a campaign that missed its months was over whether or not the king had decided. An order from the Escorial could arrive when the ships were already laid up. Braudel's image for the battle, and for events of its kind, is foam. Events are surface disturbances, "crests of foam that the tides of history carry on their strong backs." The tides are the slow constraints of distance, climate, and staple crops. Philip matters. He does not steer the water his couriers have to cross.

The use of the three speeds, when someone is deciding what happened in that sea, is to ask what the season and the distance allowed a dated decision to accomplish. A treaty signed in Madrid, a battle fought in an afternoon, a letter that takes a month, are all real. They are the top layer. Reading only that layer makes the king's correspondence look more powerful than the shipping calendar it had to obey. Reading only the depths makes the king look like a passenger. Braudel's own emphasis is the depths. He called himself, by temperament, a structuralist. The revised edition of 1966 grew larger without giving the event the last word.

Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell, in The Corrupting Sea (2000), argued that the one Mediterranean he composed is too whole. Their sea is a crowd of small, unlike places, microecologies, tied together by short hops along the coast rather than by a single climate and a single destiny. The foam image has a cost they are pointing at. It can make the people who did choose, Philip among them, look as if the tide had already voted.
