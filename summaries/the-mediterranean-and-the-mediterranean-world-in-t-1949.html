On 7 October 1571 the fleet of the Holy League, under Don John of Austria, destroyed the Ottoman navy at Lepanto. Fernand Braudel tells the battle, and then he tells the next year. 1572 was, in his chapter title, a dramatic year, and it did not become the year Ottoman sea power ended. Venice made a separate peace and left Cyprus to the Ottomans. Tunis, taken in the aftermath, was lost again in 1574. Philip II, who ruled from Spain and never commanded at Lepanto, remained a hard-working king whose orders traveled at the speed of a horse or a galley. The victory was real for the men who fought it. It did not rewrite the stalemate between two empires that each had too much coast to hold.

The constraint shows up earlier, at Malta. From 18 May to 8 September 1565 an Ottoman fleet besieged the Knights and failed to take the island before the season turned. Braudel's first volume has already measured why a campaign has an end date. Distance is, in his heading, the first enemy. Winter laid the galleys up. An order from Madrid or Istanbul could be weeks old when it arrived, and the sea it referred to had already changed. Mountains, grain, malaria, and the price of bread move on that slower clock. He wrote much of this while a prisoner of war in Germany, from 1940 to 1945, and published it in 1949. Philip appears late on purpose. The reign is the proof of the limits, after the limits have been measured.

When someone is deciding what a sixteenth-century battle did, the date is the start of the question. Lepanto was 7 October 1571, and the strategic result was visible by 1573 and 1574: Venice out of the war, Cyprus Ottoman, a new fleet at sea. A king could choose a stroke inside the season. He could not choose a different Mediterranean. Braudel's three speeds are that distinction, made into the shape of one book. Geography and climate, then economies and empires over decades, then the politics of days.

Peregrine Horden and Nicholas Purcell argued in 2000 that this sea is too much a single stage. Their evidence is local. The Biqa valley, south Etruria, Cyrenaica, and the island of Melos do not share one fate under Philip II, and the links among them turn on and off. The battle and the season remain. The unity of the sea is the part their cases put under pressure.
