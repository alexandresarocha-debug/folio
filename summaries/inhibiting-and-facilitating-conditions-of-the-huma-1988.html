The facial feedback hypothesis says that our facial expressions do not just show our emotions but also feed back into them. Earlier tests often simply asked people to pose a smile or a frown, which risked telling them what the experiment was about. Fritz Strack, Leonard Martin and Sabine Stepper wanted a way to change facial expressions without participants realising it.

Their solution was a pen. Participants were told the study was about how people perform tasks with parts of the body not normally used for them, as injured or disabled people often have to. Holding a pen gently with the front teeth, without letting the lips touch it, pulls the face into something like a smile. Holding it with the lips only, keeping the teeth off it, makes a smile impossible. A third group held the pen in their non-dominant hand.

In Study 1, 92 undergraduates at the University of Illinois rated how funny they found cartoons from Gary Larson's The Far Side, on a scale from 0 to 9. The average ratings were 4.32 in the lips condition, 4.77 with the hand and 5.14 with the teeth. The difference between teeth and lips was statistically reliable, though the authors themselves called the effect small. Study 2, with 83 students at the University of Mannheim in Germany and different cartoons, separated two parts of the humour response: the pen changed how amused people felt, but not their more detached judgment of how funny the cartoons were.

The study became one of the best-known findings in social psychology, cited as evidence that emotions can be shaped by the body even when people are unaware of their own expression.

What this means in real life: the study is the scientific root of advice such as 'smile and you will feel better', though it tested a brief effect on cartoon ratings, not lasting mood.

Caution: the samples were modest and the effect was small. In 2016 a registered replication across 17 laboratories found essentially no difference between the pen conditions, and later work suggests that the pen method is a weak and fragile way to produce facial feedback.
