Research comparing reading on paper and on screens had produced mixed results. Pablo Delgado, Cristina Vargas, Rakefet Ackerman and Ladislao Salmerón pooled the studies published between 2000 and 2017 that compared comparable texts on paper and on digital devices.

Their search turned up 1,840 records, from which 54 studies met all the criteria: 38 with different people reading on each medium and 16 in which the same people read on both. Together they involved 171,055 participants. Both kinds of design gave the same result: an advantage for paper, with an effect size of g = -0.21, small but consistent.

Three moderators made a difference. The paper advantage was larger when reading was time-limited than when people could read at their own pace. It held for informational texts, or a mix of informational and narrative texts, but not for studies using only narrative texts. And the advantage of paper increased over the years, rather than fading as people grew more used to screens.

The authors link these patterns to a tendency to process more shallowly on screens, especially when the situation seems to make that acceptable, as under time pressure. The effect did not differ by educational level.

What this means in real life: for study materials and exams, especially dense informational texts read under time pressure, paper may still offer a small edge. For reading stories for pleasure, the medium seems to matter little.

Caution: a meta-analysis is limited by the studies available, which used many different devices, texts and tests. The effect is small, it concerns comprehension tests rather than long-term learning, and the analysis of scrolling as a possible cause did not reach significance.
