Barbara McNeil, Stephen Pauker, Harold Sox, and Amos Tversky asked people to imagine they had lung cancer and to choose between surgery and radiation. The numbers favored surgery over the long run and radiation in the first weeks. Of 100 people having surgery, 90 live through the treatment, 68 are alive at one year, and 34 are alive at five years. Of 100 having radiation, all live through the treatment, 77 are alive at one year, and 22 are alive at five years. Another group saw the same counts as deaths: 10 die during surgery, 32 have died by one year, 66 by five years; none die during radiation, 23 have died by one year, 78 by five years. Ninety alive is ten dead.

They put the problem to 238 outpatients with chronic illnesses, 491 graduate students, and 424 physicians, 1,153 people in all. Some versions named the treatments and some did not. Some gave the cumulative chances above, and some gave life expectancy. On the cumulative chances, the share who preferred radiation rose from 18 percent when the numbers were lives, out of 247 people, to 44 percent when the numbers were deaths, out of 336 people. Surgery looked better as "90 live" than as "10 die." The shift was not smaller for the physicians, or for students trained in statistics, than for the patients.

The life-expectancy wording, and labeling the options "surgery" and "radiation" rather than leaving them unnamed, also made surgery more attractive. A name carries a history. "Surgery" sounds like the serious treatment. A bare pair of survival tables does not. The frame and the label were doing work that the five-year counts, read alone, would not have done.

This is the version of framing that shows up on a consent form. The same operation can be described as a 90 percent chance of getting through it or a 10 percent chance of dying on the table, and the second sentence weighs more, including for the physician reading it. A patient choosing, or a doctor recommending, can ask for both wordings before deciding. McNeil and her colleagues' suggestion was modest: if the people in the room know that the wording moves them, the choice gets harder to rush.
