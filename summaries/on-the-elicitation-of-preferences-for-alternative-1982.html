Background

The Asian disease problem had shown, on paper, that a gain frame and a loss frame can reverse a choice between identical programmes. Barbara McNeil, Stephen Pauker, Harold Sox and Amos Tversky asked whether the same reversal would appear when the disease was real, the numbers were a physician's numbers, and the people choosing included the physicians themselves. Their 1982 paper in the New England Journal of Medicine, On the Elicitation of Preferences for Alternative Therapies, is the clinical companion to the 1981 Science article. Folio's framing card cites it for a specific finding. Physicians, patients and graduate students all preferred surgery to radiation for lung cancer more often when the outcomes were framed as survival than when the same outcomes were framed as mortality.

The shift from a hypothetical outbreak to lung cancer changes the stakes of the claim. A framing effect on a classroom vignette can be filed as a puzzle about wording. A framing effect on the treatment a doctor recommends, or a patient accepts, is a fact about consent. The paper's ambition is to show that the puzzle survives the change of room.

The question

When a treatment has an early risk and a later benefit, does the description of that trade decide the preference? Surgery for lung cancer, as presented in this literature, offers a better chance of living for years and a real chance of dying from the operation itself. Radiation avoids much of that early danger and offers less long-run survival. The statistics of the two treatments can be told as the proportion of people alive after a given interval, or as the proportion who have died. Those are not two bodies of evidence. They are one body of evidence read from opposite ends. The question is whether patients, doctors and students, each group supposedly closer to the decision than a volunteer reading about an imaginary epidemic, would still be moved by which end the speaker started from.

How the study was set up

The instrument was a set of treatment descriptions for lung cancer, built from the same underlying outcomes and written in two frames. In the survival frame, the results of surgery and of radiation appear as the chances of being alive at successive points in time. In the mortality frame, the identical results appear as the chances of being dead, including the deaths that come early with surgery. Nothing was added in one version and removed in the other except the direction of the count. A person who translated freely between living and dying would choose the same treatment under both descriptions.

Three groups of respondents faced the descriptions: patients, physicians, and graduate students. The design matters because each group supplies a different excuse in advance. Patients might be frightened and numerically unpractised. Students might be clever and uninvolved. Physicians might be protected by training, by habit, or by having seen the trade-off before. If all three groups shift in the same direction, the excuse that framing is only a layman's error loses its best case. The preference asked for was a treatment preference, surgery or radiation, which is the choice the elicitation was built to draw out.

What they found

All three groups preferred surgery more often under the survival frame than under the mortality frame. The early deaths associated with surgery loomed larger when they were stated as mortality, and they receded when the same facts were stated as the complement, a chance of having survived the operation and then lived. Radiation, which looks comparatively gentle in a mortality frame because it avoids that early toll, looked less attractive once the description dwelt on who was still alive later. The direction is the direction prospect theory suggests. A frame that makes outcomes feel like losses encourages avoidance of the sure-looking early loss. A frame that makes outcomes feel like lives retained encourages the option that retains more of them in the long run.

The paper's point is the agreement across roles, not a league table of who was wisest. Physicians were not immune. Training in medicine had not installed a habit of recoding every survival curve into a mortality curve, or the reverse, before the preference was felt. Patients were not uniquely suggestible. The frame moved the people who would recommend the treatment and the people who would undergo it. An elicitation of preference had turned out to be, in part, a choice of frame by the person who wrote the page.

How the authors read the results

McNeil, Pauker, Sox and Tversky read the result as a warning about the interview, not as a discovery that one treatment is secretly better. The data do not decide between surgery and radiation. They show that the data, as spoken, decide more of the preference than a theory of stable wants would allow. If a clinic describes outcomes only in survival, it will collect more preferences for the treatment whose advantage is long-run survival. If it describes them only in mortality, it will collect more preferences for the treatment whose advantage is the avoidance of early death. A patient who has heard only one of those clinics has not yet met the choice.

They connect the finding to the psychology of the previous year. The reference point is doing the work. Death is a loss from the reference point of being alive. Survival is a gain against the reference point of the disease. Because losses loom larger, the early surgical deaths, once named as deaths, weigh heavily enough to change the modal leaning. The authors' practical conclusion is about elicitation as a craft. Anyone who asks for a preference over therapies is responsible for the frame in which the outcomes are shown, and showing both frames is a way of giving the patient a chance to notice that the preference is frame-bound.

Limits

The study elicits stated preferences from descriptions. It does not follow patients through a decision made in a clinic, with a particular tumour, a particular surgeon and a family in the room. Stated preference is the right object for a study of elicitation, and it is not the whole of medical choice. A reader should not treat the paper as a finding about which treatment extends life. The outcomes in the descriptions are inputs. The output is the sensitivity of the choice to their wording.

There is a limit of generality that the design itself suggests. Lung cancer, with a sharp early risk from surgery and a longer horizon on which surgery pulls ahead, is a case built for this contrast. A pair of treatments whose curves do not cross in that way, or a decision in which one option dominates on every description, will not produce a framing effect of this kind, because there is no trade to be redescribed. The paper shows that when the trade exists, the frame can pick the winner among people who understand the trade professionally. It is not a claim that every sentence in medicine is a hidden nudge of equal force.

Replications and critiques

The result entered the framing literature as the existence proof that expertise does not repeal the effect. Later reviews, Kühberger's among them, treat medical and monetary frames as members of one family, reliable and also dependent on whether the two wordings really are the same choice. That dependence is a reason to be careful with slogans. A brochure that adds a vivid adjective has not automatically reproduced this experiment. This experiment changed the origin of the count, from lives retained to deaths incurred, and left the numbers standing. Criticisms that people in the original disease vignette were confused by an imaginary outbreak do not touch the physicians in this one, who shifted all the same.

A fair criticism is about remedy. Showing both frames is attractive, and it is not free of frames of its own. Order, emphasis and the sheer presence of an early death on the page will still weight the conversation. The paper leaves that problem open. Its contribution is to make the problem visible in the place where preference elicitation had looked most like a neutral instrument.

Why it matters here

Folio's framing card uses this paper to take the Asian disease problem out of the hypothetical. The 1981 article shows that a gain frame and a loss frame can reverse a choice between programmes that are mathematically the same. McNeil and colleagues show that a real therapeutic trade, told as survival or as mortality, moves patients, students and doctors in the same direction. The card's lesson, that preferences are often constructed at the moment of choice, is a lesson about clinics as well as about questionnaires. Read the 1981 paper for the logic and the classic problem. Read this one for the demonstration that the logic reaches the people who already know the medicine.
