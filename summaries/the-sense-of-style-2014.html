The experiment Steven Pinker uses for the curse of knowledge is Elizabeth Newton's, from her 1990 Stanford dissertation. Forty tappers each chose three well-known songs from a list of twenty-five and tapped the rhythm on a table. Forty listeners sat with their backs turned and tried to name the tune. Asked how often their own listener would get it, the tappers' estimates averaged 50 percent, with guesses running from 10 percent to 95. Asked about an audience of a hundred, they averaged 51 percent. Listeners identified 3 songs out of 120, a rate of 2.5 percent, outside the whole range of those guesses. People who were told the song's name and then asked to predict, without having tapped or listened, also guessed about 50 percent. The tapper hears the melody. Everyone else hears taps.

Pinker's claim in The Sense of Style is that this is the main reason prose goes opaque. The writer has the definition, the middle step, and the scene, and it does not occur to them that the reader has none of those. Jargon stays in. A logical leap gets skipped because it feels too obvious to write down. The cure he recommends is the classic style described by Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner: prose that points at a thing in the world and lets the reader look at it, instead of prose that displays the writer's membership in a specialty. Showing the object is how you stop tapping a song only you can hear.

Anyone who has explained a job to a new colleague has been the tapper. The steps that feel fused into one motion for you are separate, unnamed events for them. A doctor's note, a product page, or a methods section fails in the same place, at the fact the writer no longer notices they know. Pinker's advice is practical and unsentimental. Assume the reader is competent and uninformed, put the concrete thing on the page, and check the sentence against what a person without the melody could actually reconstruct.
