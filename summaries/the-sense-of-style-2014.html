A style book from a psychology of reading

The Sense of Style, published in 2014, is Steven Pinker's book about writing in English, written by a psychologist who studies language. It is a style guide in the sense that it gives advice a writer can use on a paragraph. It is unlike a traditional handbook in where the advice comes from. Pinker treats bad prose as a predictable outcome of how minds work, and good prose as a set of repairs that take those workings seriously. The reader he imagines is an expert, often an academic or a professional, who writes badly while knowing a great deal. The diagnosis he offers is the curse of knowledge. Once you know a thing, you are a poor judge of what it is like not to know it. You over-use abbreviations, you skip the example, you stack nouns that are transparent to you and opaque to the person you most want to reach.

He takes the name of the curse from economics, from the experiments in which better-informed people could not set their extra knowledge aside when they tried to predict the judgements of the less informed. He takes the literary remedy from Francis-Noël Thomas and Mark Turner. Classic style, in their sense, is a stance: the writer has seen something in the world and directs the reader's gaze towards it, in a conversation between equals. Pinker's book is what happens when that stance is restated with the findings of cognitive science under it. The early chapters make the stance attractive, by reading passages that work and saying what they are doing. The later chapters become more technical, because the stance has to survive contact with syntax, memory, and the real rules of English as opposed to the folk rules of the schoolroom.

The curse, and the tune you can hear

The chapter on the curse of knowledge is the centre of the book for Folio's purposes, and it is concrete. Experts write in the language in which they think, and the language in which they think has had the examples compressed out of it. A term that took them a year to learn now feels like a simple pointer. Pinker lists the symptoms: jargon left undefined, abstractions with no instance attached, acronyms, and a habit of writing as if the reader were in the room when the research was done. He also names a more structural symptom. Because you already know the point, you cannot feel which sentence fails to establish it. The prose seems clear on the pass you give it, the pass of the person who knows.

The demonstration he retells, from Elizabeth Newton's work, has become the folk experiment of the idea. Tappers knock out the rhythm of a well-known song and predict how often a listener will name it. They can hear the melody over the knocks, so they expect success a large share of the time, around half in the familiar telling. Listeners hear knocks, and they name the song rarely, the figure usually given as a few per cent. The gap is the curse. The tapper's knowledge is real and private, and it disguises the poverty of the signal. Pinker thinks specialist prose is often a tapping. The writer hears the argument. The reader hears the nouns. The cure he trusts most is social, a reader who does not share the knowledge and is willing to say where the melody dropped out. Self-editing helps when it simulates that reader, and it fails when the simulation is too kind.

Sentences as trees, paragraphs as arcs

The middle of the book is a lesson in why some sentences are hard, drawn from psycholinguistics. Readers parse as they go. They hold a limited amount of structure in mind while they wait for the verb or for the point of a clause. A sentence that opens with a long noun stack, or that embeds a clause inside a clause inside a clause, asks for more hold than a reader has. Pinker explains the trouble with trees, the structures linguists draw under sentences, and with the linear string the eyes actually see. A writer who can see the tree of her own sentence is a writer who can see why a reader will stall. He is a defender of grammar in this sense, the sense in which grammar is the shared system that makes anticipation possible. He is an opponent of grammar superstitions that have no basis in that system.

Coherence gets a similar treatment. A paragraph works when its sentences stand in a stable relation, often with familiar information early and new information late, so that each line connects to a topic the reader is already holding. Pinker calls this an arc. Connective words can help, and they can also fake the arc, a row of howevers and moreovers with no actual topic underneath. He spends time on the given-new contract because it gives the curse a syntactic address. The writer, knowing everything, treats all of it as given. The reader needed half of it to be introduced. Repairing the contract is a practical way of stepping outside your own knowledge: mark what the reader has already been handed, and then hand the next piece.

Which rules he keeps

The closing movement sorts usage rules into those that prevent real misunderstanding and those that function as class passwords. Split infinitives, stranded prepositions, and a number of other schoolroom bans fail his test. They forbid constructions that English speakers use for clarity, and they distract writers from the choices that actually lose readers. The passive voice is his clearest disagreement with a certain reading of Orwell. A passive is the right choice when the topic of the sentence is the thing acted on, and when the agent is unknown or irrelevant. A ban on the passive produces contortions and does nothing, by itself, about the curse of knowledge. Pinker would rather a writer ask what the topic is, and then choose the construction that keeps the topic in view.

Classic style remains his recommended stance through these technical chapters. It tells you what the syntax is for. You are pointing at a world you and the reader can, in principle, share. If a sentence cannot be parsed, the pointing fails. If a paragraph treats your private knowledge as common, the pointing fails. If a usage rule makes you prouder of a prohibition than of a picture, the pointing has been replaced by a performance of membership. The Sense of Style is a long argument for the picture. It includes enough real linguistics to be more than a mood, and enough rewritten passages to be more than a theory. Its limitation is the limitation of classic style itself. Some truths arrive by confession, or by ritual, or by a difficulty that should not be smoothed, and a book in love with the clear window will be a partial guide to those kinds of writing. For the prose that experts inflict on colleagues and on the public, the partial guide is the one most needed.

Why it matters here

The card on the curse of knowledge and classic style is a reading of this book together with its two sources. Camerer, Loewenstein, and Weber supply the experimental name, the finding that informed people cannot un-know what they know even for money. Thomas and Turner supply the stance Pinker recommends, the window and the equal conversation. The Sense of Style is the place where those inheritances become a writing book: symptoms, the tapping demonstration, the syntax of difficult sentences, the arc of a paragraph, and a sorting of real rules from folk ones. Read it when Orwell's prohibitions have started to feel like the whole of style. Pinker keeps the demand for clarity and changes the reason. The reason is a mind that forgets what it was like to learn, and a reader who should not have to tap the melody back into existence alone.
