Habit as the enemy of experience

Viktor Shklovsky's essay Art as Device, also translated as Art as Technique, appeared in 1917 and became a founding text of Russian Formalism. The argument begins not with a poem but with a diagnosis of ordinary perception. Habit economises. Once an action has been performed often enough, we carry it out automatically, and the object of the action drops out of awareness. Walking, speaking, the furniture of a familiar room, even the faces of people we love, are recognised rather than seen. Recognition is fast and cheap, and it is how a life gets lived at a practical speed. Shklovsky's claim is that the saving has a cost. A life lived entirely by recognition is a life that was not felt as it passed. The things were there. The sensation of them was not. Art exists, on his account, to recover the sensation of life, to make the stone stony again. The usual English for that aim has become part of the essay's afterlife, and it is the right aim to keep in view, because everything technical in the essay serves it.

The method is ostranenie, a word usually translated as defamiliarisation or estrangement. The technique presents a familiar thing in an unfamiliar way, so that perception, which had been shortened into recognition, is lengthened and made difficult. Difficulty is not an obstacle to the aesthetic effect. Difficulty is the effect, because the time perception takes is the time during which the thing is actually experienced. A work that delivered its object instantly would be a work that had saved us the trouble of seeing, which is the trouble art is for. Shklovsky is willing to define art by this function rather than by a repertory of beautiful subjects. The same object can be unfelt in life and felt in art, and the difference is the device that slowed us down. The essay's title is the theory. Art is device. The device is what makes perception take time.

Tolstoy's refusals to name

His examples come chiefly from Tolstoy, and they are chosen because Tolstoy repeatedly describes a familiar institution as if he did not know its name. In the story Kholstomer, the narrator is a horse. The horse hears people call him someone's horse, and he observes that the person does not ride him or feed him. Property, seen from this position, is a set of sentences people utter about things they do not use. The device is the change of narrator, and the change of narrator is what makes an everyday legal relation look like a peculiar custom. Shklovsky's interest is not only in the moral, though the moral is real. His interest is in the technique that produces the moral by blocking the usual word. Once the word property is allowed to do the work, the reader recognises the topic and stops seeing it. The horse has no such word. The reader has to assemble the institution out of the practices, and the assembling is the recovered sensation.

In War and Peace, an opera is described from a point of view that does not grant the conventions. Painted cardboard, people singing strangely, gestures that would be embarrassing in a room, are what remains when the name opera is withheld. The scene is funny, and the humour is the sound of a habit breaking. Natasha, or a similarly uninitiated gaze, supplies a perception that regular opera-goers have automated away. Shklovsky reads this as Tolstoy's characteristic move, applied to war, to law, to sex, to private property, to punishment. Flogging, described without the vocabulary that makes flogging a normal part of discipline, becomes a fact about bodies that the social word had protected the reader from seeing. The moral shock and the formal device are the same event. Critics who praise Tolstoy for his moral seriousness and critics who praise him for his technique are, on this account, pointing at one gesture: the refusal to let the accepted name finish the reader's work.

Device as the unit of literary study

From the examples Shklovsky draws a methodological conclusion that founded a school. If art is the sum of devices by which perception is delayed and renewed, then the study of literature should study devices, not the author's biography, not the political message as a message, and not the reader's daydream of being a better person. Russian Formalism, in the work of Shklovsky, Jakobson, Tynyanov, Eikhenbaum and others, took the device, the priem, as the unit. A plot is a device. A point of view is a device. A rhythm that roughens ordinary language is a device, because poetic language is itself a making-strange of practical language, a way of making the sound and the word take longer than communication requires. Folk riddles, euphemisms, and erotic descriptions that avoid the plain name are, for Shklovsky, popular forms of the same art. The essay is a manifesto in the precise sense. It tells a generation of critics what to look at, and it tells them that looking at the author's life is a way of not doing the work.

The manifesto has a politics of its own, even though it postpones politics. To treat literature as device is to refuse the idea that a work is a window onto a soul or a statement of a party. That refusal was liberating in a culture of biographical criticism, and it became dangerous in a culture that later demanded that art be judged by its political utility. Formalism was suppressed in the Soviet Union. Shklovsky survived, recanted in places, and wrote differently under the pressure. The essay of 1917 should be read as the unpressured claim. Art has a function, the restoration of sensation, and a means, the device that estranges. A criticism that begins somewhere else will praise or condemn a work for what it says and never notice how it makes the saying take time. The claim is austere enough to be useful, and broad enough to explain why a description of an opera and a line of verse can be the same kind of event.

Brecht, and the difference inside a similarity

The afterlife of ostranenie runs through the theatre, and the important name is Bertolt Brecht's Verfremdungseffekt, the alienation or distancing effect, developed in the 1930s. Brecht wanted the stage to make the familiar strange so that an audience would think, rather than identify and weep. Songs that interrupt, placards that announce the outcome, acting that shows a character instead of becoming one, are devices for keeping the spectator's perception from automating into empathy. The kinship with Shklovsky is real, and the card on defamiliarisation is right to say so. Brecht had contact with Russian avant-garde ideas. The words are neighbours. A history of the technique that omitted him would be too tidy, and a history that collapsed the two would be too smooth.

The difference is the desired response. Shklovsky wants the stone to be stony. The end is sensation, the recovery of a perception that habit had cheapened, and the sensation can include moral shock of the Tolstoyan kind without being defined as a political lesson. Brecht wants the audience to judge a social arrangement that the play has made visible by refusing the usual theatrical spell. Identification, for him, is the enemy, because identification spends the energy that should go into criticism. For Shklovsky, a certain intense identification with a slowed-down perception is the aesthetic experience itself. Both men make things strange. They do it for ends that can conflict. A production that estranges in Shklovsky's sense, in order that a gesture be felt as if for the first time, may be a production Brecht would accuse of a refined enjoyment. A production that estranges in Brecht's sense, in order that enjoyment be interrupted by analysis, may be a production Shklovsky would find too eager to replace perception with a conclusion. Keeping the two apart is how the essay stays itself rather than becoming a general blessing on any art that is odd.

What the essay does not explain

The essay also brackets history. Devices have careers. A technique that was estranging in 1812 can be the most habitual thing in the world by 1917, which is why Formalism needed, and later developed, a sense of evolution, the wearing out of devices and the invention of new ones. The 1917 essay states the function more clearly than it states the history. Reading it beside the later formalist work makes the function look like a starting point rather than a whole system. Reading it beside Tolstoy makes it look like what it is at its best, a precise account of why a moral novelist keeps describing things as if he had never been told what they were called. The stone is stony because someone refused the name. That sentence is enough to carry a great deal of practical criticism, and it is the sentence the essay earns.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Defamiliarisation: art makes the stone stony, is a close reading of this essay. Habit dulls perception. Art recovers the sensation of life by ostranenie, by making the familiar difficult to perceive quickly. The horse in Kholstomer, who cannot make sense of my horse, and the opera in War and Peace, seen as painted cardboard and strange singing, are the card's examples and Shklovsky's. The card places him as a leading figure of Russian Formalism, the turn towards devices rather than biography or politics, and it notes the anticipation of Brecht's alienation effect, with the caution that the similarity of terms is not a coincidence and not an identity. Read the essay for the function and the method, stated before the school had a shelf of commentaries. The function is the return of sensation. The method is the device that slows recognition. Everything else in the card, the translations of the title, the later theatre, the fate of the movement, is context. The argument is the stone.
