Viktor Shklovsky, in the 1917 essay "Art as Device," says Tolstoy makes a familiar thing strange by refusing its usual name. In the piece "Shame," flogging is not called punishment and left there. It is "to strip people who have broken the law, to hurl them to the floor, and to rap on their bottoms with switches," and then "to lash about on the naked buttocks." Tolstoy asks why this stupid, savage way of causing pain, and not needles in the shoulder, or a vise on the hands. The question is unfair on purpose. Once the accepted name is gone, the act has to be seen as a procedure done to a body. Shklovsky's point is not only that flogging is cruel. The cruelty was already in the word, safely, for anyone who had stopped looking. The description makes the looking take time.

He takes a second case from Tolstoy's story "Kholstomer," which is told by a horse. The horse understands talk of whipping and of Christianity well enough. He cannot understand "his own" or "his colt," or why people call him a man's property. "My horse," said of a living animal, seems as strange to him as "my land," "my air," or "my water." People, the horse decides, are guided by words they have agreed on, and the one who can say "mine" about the largest number of things is counted the happiest. Many of those who called the horse their own never rode him. Property, seen from the stall, is a sentence, not a use. Shklovsky piles up this kind of example, from war, law, and sex, to get to a sentence of his own. Habit devours "works, clothes, furniture, one's wife, and the fear of war." Art exists so that a person may recover the sensation of life, "to make the stone stony." The technique is to make the object unfamiliar, the form difficult, the perception long. The delay is the effect.

The essay is a practical warning about smooth language. If you write "punishment," "property," or "operation" and move on, you have handed the reader a token they already know how to spend without seeing. If you describe the switches, or you let a horse fail to understand the pronoun, the token does not spend. The same habit eats ordinary days. A face, a room, a job, recognized a thousand times, is no longer perceived. Shklovsky wants the device that slows recognition down. Bertolt Brecht, a little later, built a theatre on a cousin of this idea, the Verfremdungseffekt, but he wanted the slowed moment to turn into a judgment about a social arrangement. Shklovsky's end, in 1917, is the sensation itself. The stone should feel like a stone again, even before anyone decides what to do about it.
