In New Guinea, a politician named Yali asked Jared Diamond a blunt question: "Why is it that you white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, but we black people had little cargo of our own?" By cargo he meant steel tools and the rest of the manufactured goods Europeans arrived with. Diamond refuses the answer that Europeans were smarter. His answer is geographic. The peoples of Eurasia lived on the continent that happened to hold more of the wild plants and animals that can be farmed, and on a continent shaped so those farms could spread.

He counts the animals. Of the world's 148 big wild land mammals that eat plants and weigh over 100 pounds, only 14 were domesticated before the twentieth century. Five of those fourteen became important almost everywhere people could keep them: the cow, the sheep, the goat, the pig, and the horse. Their wild ancestors lived in Eurasia and North Africa. The Americas had the llama and the alpaca and no beast of burden to match the horse. Australia and New Guinea had none of the fourteen. Diamond's rule is that a domesticable animal has to pass many tests at once, diet, growth, breeding in captivity, temperament, and a social order humans can take over. Failing one test is enough to stay wild. Zebras failed. Sheep passed. The head start was a difference in raw material, not a difference in effort.

The shape of the land then compounded the luck. Eurasia runs broadly east to west, so a crop domesticated at one place can be tried at a similar latitude, with a similar day length and a similar season. The Americas and Africa run north to south. A crop moving from one zone to the next meets a different climate and often fails. Food production, on this account, came earlier and more completely in Eurasia. Dense populations, specialists, states, writing, and the crowd diseases that jump from herds to people followed. Guns and steel were late products of that accumulation. Germs did much of the killing when Europeans met the peoples of the Americas.

The practical point is easy to misuse. A head start in wheat, sheep, and east-west roads helps explain why Eurasia had ships, steel, and smallpox in 1492. It does not tell you why, among the societies Europe later colonized, some are rich now and some are poor. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson, in a 2001 study of 64 former European colonies, found that once they accounted for a modern measure of protection against the seizure of property, countries in Africa and countries closer to the equator were not poorer. Geography sorted the deep past. It is a bad substitute for the rules people live under now.
