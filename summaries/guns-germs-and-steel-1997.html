Yali's question, and the answer Diamond refuses

Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, published in 1997, begins with a question put to him in New Guinea by a local politician named Yali. Why did Europeans end up with so much of the material power, the cargo of goods and tools, and New Guineans with so little? Diamond treats the question as the right one and the usual answers as unacceptable. He will not explain the imbalance by differences of intelligence or of racial character. His opening claim is geographic. The peoples of Eurasia were not wiser. They lived on a continent that happened to contain more of the wild plants and animals that could be domesticated, and that was shaped so those domesticates could spread. Food production came earlier and more completely. Dense populations, specialists, states, writing, and epidemic diseases followed. Steel weapons, firearms, and germs were the proximate reasons a small European force could overthrow large American empires. Geography was the ultimate reason Europeans had the weapons and the germs and the Inca emperor did not.

The book is a long argument from prehistory to the early modern world, written by a physiologist and biogeographer who wants a non-specialist to see the whole chain. It won the Pulitzer Prize and became the default popular account of why the continents diverged. It has also been argued with ever since, often by historians who think a geographic ultimate cause has been asked to do the work of politics, culture, and the actual decisions of conquerors and the conquered.

Domestication, and why it was uneven

The first major case is the origin of food production. Diamond argues that only a few regions domesticated plants and animals independently, and that the Fertile Crescent had an extraordinary starter set. Wheat and barley were already productive wild grasses. Several large mammals, sheep, goats, cattle, and pigs, lived there and had the temperaments and the social structures that make domestication possible. He uses what he calls the Anna Karenina principle. A domesticable animal has to get many things right, diet, growth rate, breeding in captivity, disposition, and a social hierarchy humans can take over. Failing one test is enough to remain wild. Eurasia had the candidates that passed. Sub-Saharan Africa had spectacular mammals that mostly failed. The Americas had the llama and the alpaca and little else of comparable use, and no beast of burden to match the horse. Australia had still fewer. The head start was not a small difference in effort. It was a difference in raw material.

He then asks why the head start compounded. Eurasia lies broadly east to west. Places at the same latitude share day length and, roughly, climate, so a crop domesticated in one place can be tried in another without a total reinvention. The Americas and Africa lie north to south. A crop moving from one zone to another meets different seasons and different diseases, and the transfer is slower and more often fails. Diamond treats this axis as one of the great unnoticed facts of world history. The same geographic accident that provided the domesticates also laid out a highway for them across Eurasia, and denied that highway to the other continents. China, in his account, received and contributed to the same broad pool. The Americas, cut off by oceans and divided by latitude, developed brilliant agricultures, maize above all, later and in greater isolation.

From food to guns, germs, and steel

Food production is the hinge. Farmers support more people per unit of land than foragers. More people mean more inventors, more soldiers, and more surplus to feed people who do not farm. Surplus and density are the conditions under which specialists, kings, and scribes become possible. Diamond does not claim that every farming society built a state or an alphabet. He claims that the societies which did were drawn from the farming pool, and that the pool was filled earlier in Eurasia. Writing arises in these dense societies because someone has a reason to record tax, trade, or the king's deeds. Technology accumulates because there are more makers and because a useful device can spread along the same east-west routes as the crops. The title's guns and steel are late products of a technological accumulation that began with farmers and smiths, not with a European spark in the Renaissance.

Germs are the chapter that readers remember, and it is the one most tightly tied to animals. Diamond argues that the crowd diseases of the Old World, among them smallpox and measles, evolved from the infections of domestic animals in dense populations. Eurasians who survived childhood carried a grim inherited experience of these diseases. The peoples of the Americas, Australia, and the Pacific did not, because they had not lived for thousands of years beside the same herds. When contact came, the germs travelled ahead of, and alongside, the conquerors. Diamond's account of the Spanish conquest of the Inca and the Aztec empires gives guns, horses, and steel their place and then argues that disease did still more of the killing. The proximate causes of conquest are real. They are also, in his chain, the latest chapter of a biogeographic story that neither Pizarro nor Atahualpa chose.

Around the world, and the conclusion he wants

The later chapters apply the framework beyond the famous collision. Polynesia is his natural experiment. Related peoples settled islands with very different climates and resources and developed very different societies, from the complex chiefdoms of Hawaii to the smaller communities of more marginal islands, which he reads as geography sorting social forms in a shorter time. Africa gets a long treatment. He emphasises the north-south axis, the difficulty of moving Mediterranean crops and animals across the Sahara and the equatorial forest, the tsetse fly's constraint on livestock, and the Bantu expansion as a farming dispersal that the environment shaped. China is his example of a different geographic consequence. An early, relatively connected civilisation unified early, which sped the first spread of innovations and later allowed a single political decision to halt oceanic voyaging. Europe's fragmented geography, many peninsulas and a stubborn set of rival states, made a single decision to stop impossible, and competition among states drove the overseas expansion. The conclusion repeats the opening refusal. The people who met at Cajamarca differed in power because of where their ancestors had farmed, and because of what had farmed with them, not because of a difference in their minds.

The evidence is drawn from archaeology, linguistics, genetics as it stood in the 1990s, epidemiology, and the narrative of the conquests. Diamond is synthesising fields he does not all professionally inhabit. The synthesis is the book's value and its exposure. Where a specialist consensus existed, he is often a clear guide. Where he has chosen one side of a live argument, a reader who knows only this book will think the argument is over.

What the criticism has stuck on

Historians and anthropologists have objected to the geographic determinism. A theory that runs from seeds to steel leaves little for political institutions, religious ideas, or individual and collective action to do, except to play out a script written by the map. The conquest of the Americas involved strategy, alliance with local enemies of the Aztec and Inca states, and a particular European appetite for plunder and mission. Germs were devastating. They do not make those choices unimportant, and they do not explain why Europe, rather than China or the Ottoman empire, was the power that arrived. Critics also argue that Diamond underplays the last five centuries of colonial violence, slavery, and institutional design, which have their own effects on who is rich now, effects that a Neolithic head start does not fix. The east-west axis, memorable as it is, simplifies climates that do not actually form neat bands, and simplifies the real history of how crops moved.

The sharpest organised reply, for readers of this path, is the institutions argument associated with Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson. They contend that the way societies are ruled explains modern income differences better than latitude or disease ecology alone, and their colonial evidence is a direct challenge to any reading of Diamond in which geography still determines outcomes after 1500. Diamond's answer, which he has given in exchanges around that work, is that geography shaped which institutions were possible in the first place. David Graeber and David Wengrow have attacked the book from another side, rejecting the ladder from foraging to farming to the state on which the chain depends. A fair user of Guns, Germs, and Steel can keep the domesticates, the germs, and the continental axes as real advantages in the deep past, and refuse the implication that the political history of the last millennium is merely geography working itself out.

Why it matters here

The card diamond-geography is this book. Yali's question, the Fertile Crescent's plants and animals, the link from livestock to crowd diseases, the east-west axis, and the explicit refusal of a racial answer are the card's contents, and they are Diamond's contents. The card records both the influence and the charge of geographic determinism. It points onward to Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, who put colonial institutions against geography as an explanation of modern incomes, and to Why Nations Fail and The Dawn of Everything as further challenges. Folio uses Guns, Germs, and Steel as the strongest popular statement of the geographic head start. The rest of the path exists so that the head start is not mistaken for a complete theory of why nations are rich or poor now.
