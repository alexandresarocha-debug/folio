Airstrip One, on a cold April day

Nineteen Eighty-Four, published in 1949, is George Orwell's novel of a Britain absorbed into Oceania, a super-state permanently at war with one or the other of the remaining two. The war's enemy changes, and the records change with it. Winston Smith, a member of the Outer Party, works at the Ministry of Truth in London, called Airstrip One, where his job is to alter back numbers of the Times so that the past agrees with this week's line. He lives in Victory Mansions under a telescreen that can be turned down and never off. The stairwell smells of boiled cabbage. A poster in the street carries an enormous face and the caption that Big Brother is watching you. The opening is famous because it is ordinary. Tyranny arrives as housing, a canteen, a job in an archive, and a screen.

From the first pages Winston is in a small, private revolt. He buys a blank book and begins a diary, which is already a crime, and he writes it to the future, to whoever might read. He is also a man with a varicose ulcer, a bad cough, and a hunger for sensory pleasure the Party has pauperised: real coffee, real sugar, the feel of good paper. Orwell builds the political argument out of these appetites. The Party does not only forbid opinions. It starves the capacity for comparison. If you cannot remember that the chocolate ration went down, because the record now says it went up and your memory has been trained to agree, you cannot say that you are poorer. Winston's work is the industrial form of that training. His diary is a bet that a single unrevised page might be enough to break it.

Julia, the room, and the paperweight

The plot tightens when two people seem to answer the bet. Julia, who works in the Fiction Department on the machines that produce rubbish novels for the proles, passes Winston a note in three words. They meet in the country, then in a room above a junk-shop kept by Mr Charrington, an old man who appears to remember songs and a past the Party has abolished. The room becomes the novel's counter-world: a bed, a private dark, a coral paperweight Winston buys because it holds a small solid beauty that does not mean anything official. Orwell writes these chapters as a real love story, with pleasure, secrecy, and the comedy of two people trying to be ordinary. The tenderness is load-bearing. When the room is destroyed, the loss has to be the loss of a life, a physical one, or the later torture would be an abstract debate.

O'Brien, an Inner Party intellectual Winston has hoped might be a secret ally, completes the trap. He invites Winston into what looks like a conspiracy, the Brotherhood of the enemy Goldstein, and he gives him a book, The Theory and Practice of Oligarchical Collectivism, which explains the strategic logic of perpetual war, the three-class structure, and the reason the Party wants power. Winston reads it in the room, in the happiness that comes just before Charrington is revealed as an officer and the telescreen behind the picture is revealed as having been there all along. The explanation and the arrest are one movement. Understanding the system is not a step towards defeating it. In this novel, the system can hand you a true description and still own the room in which you read it. O'Brien will later tell Winston that he helped to write the book. The truth of the analysis and the treachery of its distribution are both allowed to stand.

Newspeak, doublethink, and the ministries

Around the plot Orwell builds a linguistics. The ministries are named by inversion: Truth handles lies, Peace handles war, Love handles torture, Plenty handles scarcity. The Party's slogans set contradictions side by side and demand that both sides be believed. Doublethink is the mental act that makes the demand survivable, the habit of holding two contradictory beliefs and accepting both, of forgetting whatever it is useful to forget and then forgetting the forgetting. Newspeak is the long project that would make doublethink unnecessary by making the contradictory thought inexpressible. Syme, a philologist in the canteen, explains the beauty of the destruction with too much intelligence, and Winston understands that Syme will be vapourised. Enthusiasm for the method is already a kind of excess thought.

The appendix, The Principles of Newspeak, is written as a scholarly account in the past tense, describing the language as a project of the regime and explaining its grammar of abbreviation, its political vocabulary, and its aim of shrinking the range of consciousness. Readers have noticed the tense and wondered whether it implies a later world in which the project can be described historically. The novel itself does not narrate that later world. What the appendix does inside the book is to finish the argument of Orwell's 1946 essay in fictional form. If political language works by killing the picture, a state might try to kill the words that make pictures. The essay asks a writer to choose the concrete term. The novel imagines a dictionary from which the concrete choice has been removed, so that a thoughtcrime cannot be assembled because the parts are no longer stocked.

Room 101, and the last loyalty

The Ministry of Love occupies the last movement. Winston and Julia are separated. O'Brien becomes the torturer who also wants to be the teacher. The pain is detailed, and it is paired with a philosophical bullying: the Party will make reality, two and two will be whatever it says, the mind will learn to see the fingers O'Brien chooses. Winston's resistance gathers on one point, his feeling for Julia, which he experiences as the last private fact. Room 101 is the place where that point is broken by the particular terror that is his and not a generic prisoner's, rats. He betrays her. The betrayal is the novel's most horrible invention because it uses love as the instrument. Afterward, in the Chestnut Tree Café, Winston drinks, traces slogans, and watches himself become the citizen the posters required. The book ends with his love for Big Brother. The future reader he wrote the diary towards has been replaced by the present face on the wall.

Proles, throughout, are the residue of hope Winston cannot quite abandon and the novel will not confirm. They are the majority, they sing, they quarrel, they are not worth the Party's closest surveillance because they are not granted a politics. Orwell leaves them vivid and unrisen. The effect is to refuse the consolation of an inevitable uprising. Nineteen Eighty-Four is a warning in the shape of a completed defeat. Its language theme is the part that ties it to the essay: euphemism perfected, history edited, the vocabulary reduced, the picture prevented. Its human theme is what that perfection costs one body in one rented room. Both are required. A reader who takes only the slogans has a poster. A reader who takes Winston, Julia, the paperweight, and the rats has the reason the poster was worth fearing.

Why it matters here

The card on Orwell's rules points to this novel because Newspeak is the 1946 argument given institutions, a ministry, a canteen philologist, and an appendix. Pacification and the elimination of unreliable elements, the essay's examples of phrases that hide acts, become a whole language policy whose purpose is to make the acts hard to think. Doublethink is the psychological partner of those phrases, the trained ability to accept the phrase and the fact on alternate days. Read the novel after the essay and the essay's sixth rule changes temperature. Breaking a stylistic rule to avoid a barbarous sentence is a small freedom. The society of Nineteen Eighty-Four is one in which that freedom has been designed out of the dictionary, and in which Winston's last, broken love is what remains when the design succeeds.
