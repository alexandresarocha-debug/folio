On a bright cold day in April, the clocks strike thirteen, and Winston Smith goes to work. He is thirty-nine. He lives in Victory Mansions, under a telescreen that can be turned down and never off, and his job at the Ministry of Truth is to rewrite back numbers of the Times so that the past agrees with this week's line. A poster in the street says that Big Brother is watching you. He buys a blank book in a junk shop and starts a diary, which is already a crime, addressed to a future reader who may never exist. The work has a simple purpose. If today's record says the chocolate ration went up, and you have been trained to remember it that way, you cannot say that you have less than you had.

Julia, who works the machines that turn out rubbish novels, passes him a note: "I love you." They meet in a room above the junk shop. Winston buys a glass paperweight with a piece of coral inside it, a small solid thing that means nothing official. The room is a trap. The shopkeeper is an officer, and a telescreen has been behind the picture the whole time. In the Ministry of Love, O'Brien, whom Winston had hoped was an ally, tortures him. The last private fact Winston is holding is Julia. In Room 101 the instrument is the fear that is his in particular, a cage of rats, and he shouts for it to be done to her instead. The book ends at the Chestnut Tree Café. "He loved Big Brother."

The ministries are named by inversion: Truth handles lies, Peace handles war, Love handles torture, Plenty handles scarcity. An appendix, "The Principles of Newspeak," describes a language project in the past tense, whose aim is to shrink the vocabulary until a heretical thought cannot be assembled. That is the 1946 essay given a building and a job. Pacification, in the essay, is a phrase that hides a raid. In the novel it is Winston's morning, one corrected article at a time, and the cost of the correction is a rented room, a paperweight, and a betrayal he cannot take back.
