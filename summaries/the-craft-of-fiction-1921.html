The novel catching up with the drama

Percy Lubbock's The Craft of Fiction, published in 1921, is the book that taught a generation of readers and writers to treat the novel as a craft of presentation rather than as a loose report of life. Lubbock takes his cue from Henry James's prefaces, and he generalises them. The novel, he argues, becomes an art at the point where the story is placed before the reader's imagination as a scene, something shown, rather than being told as a summary the reader must take on credit. The distinction between showing and telling, which later advice would harden into a slogan, is here a distinction between scene and picture, between the dramatised occasion and the panoramic account. Lubbock is not embarrassed to rank them. The scene is the higher intensity. The picture, the narrator's retrospective survey, is often necessary and is usually a falling-off. The history of the novel, in his telling, is the history of a form learning to do what the theatre already knew how to do, and then learning to do it from inside a single consciousness, which the theatre cannot.

Point of view is the technical centre of this history. Once a novelist chooses the mind through which a stretch of the story will be seen, the choice is a discipline. The book cannot conveniently know what that mind cannot know, and it should not break the discipline for a fact that would be convenient. Lubbock treats the holding of a point of view as the thing that gives a large novel the unity a play gets from the stage. Without it, the novelist is a host who wanders in and out of the room, pointing, explaining, and spoiling the guests' own encounter with events. With it, the reader lives the events under a limitation that feels like life, because life, too, is seen from somewhere. The argument is Jamesian in its pride. Difficulty, limitation, and the refusal of the god's-eye shortcut are not sacrifices. They are the conditions under which fiction stops being a report and starts being a made experience.

Scene, picture, and the reader's share

Lubbock's account of scene is more specific than the classroom phrase "show, don't tell". A scene, for him, is a continuous occasion, usually in dialogue and action, which the reader seems to attend. Time on the page and time in the room run together. Nobody summarises the outcome before the occasion has produced it. A picture, by contrast, is the narrator's composed view of a period, a person, or a social fact, delivered as something already understood. Pictures are how novels cross years, introduce a family, and compress a war. Lubbock grants them their use and then asks the novelist to recognise the drop in intensity when a picture replaces a scene. The art, as far as art can be talked about as a procedure, lies in knowing which moments deserve the expensive present tense of a scene, and in building the pictures so that they serve the scenes rather than replace them. A novel that is all picture is a chronicle with opinions. A novel that is all scene, he is less worried about, because he thinks the scene is where the reader's imagination finally has something to do.

The reader's share is the moral of the method. When the author tells, the reader receives a conclusion. When the author shows, the reader makes the conclusion, and the making is the pleasure and the life of reading. Lubbock is severe with novelists who will not trust that making. They step in front of the picture. They tell us what to think of a character we were in the middle of understanding. Thackeray is his recurring case of a great talent that keeps interrupting itself, a narrator who will not stay behind the curtain. Flaubert, in Madame Bovary, is a case of the method carried through, the author refinished out of the language, the scenes left to do the judging. James, particularly in a novel as tightly limited as The Ambassadors, is the method conscious of itself, a whole book lived from one centre of vision so that the drama of understanding is the drama of the book. These readings are Lubbock's evidence. He works by describing how particular novels are conducted, and the descriptions are the theory.

What the discipline made visible

The value of the book, even for a reader who will finally side with Booth against it, is the quality of the attention it demands. Before Lubbock, and before James's prefaces reached a wide workshop audience, it was possible to talk about novels almost entirely in terms of their people, their morals, and their likeness to life. Lubbock insists that likeness to life is an effect produced by choices of form, and that those choices can be named. Who sees this chapter? Is this a scene or a summary? Has the point of view been broken for the sake of a fact the chosen mind could not possess? Does the narrator know the end while pretending to discover it with us? These questions do not settle a novel's worth. They stop a criticism that cannot tell a flashback from a confession, or a limited witness from an omniscient historian, from believing it has described the book. The craft tradition that follows, including the writers who rebel against Lubbock's ranking, is using his questions even when it rejects his scores.

His ranking is nonetheless a real narrowing, and it is the narrowing Booth would open back up. By treating the author's visible intelligence as an interruption, Lubbock makes it hard to praise the very different art of a narrator whose commentary is the pleasure. He also makes it hard to see free indirect style clearly, that middle voice in which the narrator's grammar carries the character's bias, because his preferred contrast is between a retired author and a dramatised scene. The middle voice is neither a lecture nor a play. It is a telling that has absorbed a showing. A theory built on the drama as the novel's destination will always be a little suspicious of it. Lubbock's suspicion produced a cleaner technical vocabulary than English criticism had possessed. It also produced the slogan, once the vocabulary left his careful rankings and entered the classroom, that good writing shows and bad writing tells. He is more nuanced than the slogan. He still built the scale on which the slogan would be measured.

A standard and its proper use

Read as a standard rather than as a law, The Craft of Fiction remains a sharp instrument. It asks a writer to justify every retreat from the scene into the summary, and to justify every claim to know more than the chosen consciousness knows. A great many slack novels fail these tests in ways a reader can feel and could not, before this tradition, easily name. The panoramic chapter that exists because the writer has not imagined the scene, the sudden access to a stranger's secret so that a plot can be explained, the narrator's adjective that does the work the dialogue was supposed to do: Lubbock gives you the terms for the disappointment. He also gives you a way to admire difficulty. A limited viewpoint sustained under pressure is an achievement, not a lack of range. The achievement is James's, and Lubbock is at his best as James's expositor, showing why a novel that seems to do less is doing something harder.

The proper use stops short of his conclusion that the harder thing is always the better thing. Compression, moral analysis, the long view of a society, the joke that only a narrator can make: these are novelistic powers the stage does not have, and a craft theory that treats them as the residue of incomplete dramatisation will train writers to apologise for the powers of their own form. Later handbooks, including Le Guin's, take this correction as given and send the writer back to practise both scene and summary on purpose. Lubbock's book is the reason the correction was necessary. It stated, with real analytical force, the case for the shown story, the held point of view, and the reader as the maker of the meaning. The case is half of the truth about fiction. It is the half that the twentieth century learned first, and learned so well that it needed Booth, and then the working novelists, to remember the other half.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading is the advice to show rather than tell, together with the reasons for sometimes ignoring it. This book is where the advice became a craft, before it became a cliché. Lubbock, reading James's practice as a general method, argues that fiction turns into an art when the story is shown as a scene the reader attends, and that panoramic telling is a lower intensity a novelist should use sparingly and with its eyes open. Point of view, consistently held, is his principle of form. The argument is the one Wayne Booth would challenge forty years later, by insisting that telling is itself a rhetoric and that the author's intelligence need not be a flaw. It is also the argument Ursula K. Le Guin would put back into the workshop as one exercise among others, not as a ban. Read Lubbock for the strongest version of the scenic demand, stated with the novels in view and without the later slogan's crudity. The demand is still the right question to ask of a scene that has gone lifeless. It is the wrong law to impose on a form that can also think aloud.
