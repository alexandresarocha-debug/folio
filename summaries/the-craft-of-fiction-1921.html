Percy Lubbock, in The Craft of Fiction (1921), uses Madame Bovary as the book in which you can watch a novelist decide how a story should meet the reader. Flaubert, he says, knew the subject before he wrote it. The characters were defined, the small incidents set in order, and the remaining question was presentation. Lubbock then corrects his own word "telling." "The art of fiction does not begin until the novelist thinks of his story as a matter to be shown, to be so exhibited that it will tell itself." Handing the reader the facts as information is only the argument of the book, the ground the novelist builds on. "The book is not a row of facts, it is a single image." Defoe, on this account, can lean on the claim that his tales are historically true. A novel cannot. It has to look true, and plain statement will not make it look true. Flaubert still writes sentences, one after another. The art is in the path he chooses among them, so that a description of Emma, or of one of her moods, or of something she does, arrives as a scene rather than as a report.

Lubbock's other long case is Henry James's The Ambassadors. There the reader stays with Lambert Strether, and Strether's slow understanding of Chad and of Paris is the story. What Strether does not yet see, the reader does not get by a side door. The scene acts on one mind, and that mind's revision of the scene is the plot. Lubbock treats this as the novel grown up: dramatic where an older novel would have summarized, and bound to a point of view where an older novel would have wandered among minds. Picture and drama are his two poles. Summary has a place, but the place is lower. The effect he admires is the one that seems to be happening, rather than the one that is being recounted.

The advice is still the default in a writing class, and it is good advice against the paragraph that informs the reader of a quarrel instead of letting the quarrel occur. It becomes a muddle when "show" is taken to mean that nobody may ever summarize, judge, or tell you what a person wants. Lubbock himself keeps saying that the novelist must state, must narrate, must set down assertions. The difference he cares about is the path, not a ban on sentences that explain. Wayne Booth, in The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961), argued that Lubbock's preference had hardened into just such a ban, and that telling is one of the novelist's legitimate tools. A scene can be the right tool for Emma's humiliation. A sentence of telling can be the right tool for the year that made her ready to be humiliated. The craft is knowing which job you are doing.
