A book about continuity, written by a man who feared rupture

Alexis de Tocqueville’s The Old Regime and the Revolution, published in 1856, is the book in which a theorist of democracy turned back to France and tried to explain why a revolution that announced a new world had built a state so like the one it destroyed. He had already, in Democracy in America, described a society born equal and then worried about the new kinds of tutelage equality might accept. The French book asks how a country with a deep feudal past arrived at a centralised democracy that reminded him, in its habits of administration, of the monarchy. His answer has two parts, and both of them cut against the revolution’s account of itself. The first is that the revolution’s most striking political achievement, the construction of a powerful, uniform, central state, was largely the completion of the old regime’s own work. The second, which is the part Folio’s card names, is that the revolution came not at the moment of greatest misery but in a period of improvement, when reform had begun and when the remaining abuses had lost the justification of inevitability.

He did not live to write the history of the revolution year by year. What he published is a study of the regime that produced it, based on a kind of research that was unusual for a French political writer of his class. He read the records of intendants, the administrators who were the monarchy’s hands in the provinces, and he worked especially closely on the papers of the intendancy of Tours. The method matters because his paradoxes are empirical. He is not deducing the revolution from the idea of equality. He is looking at who paid which dues, who met whom, which provinces still had living local institutions, and which had already been reduced to the supervision of a man sent from Paris. The book is organised in three movements: a demonstration that the revolution continued the old regime, an anatomy of how the old regime had already destroyed the intermediate bodies that might have taught the French to be free, and an account of how, in the reign of Louis XVI, reform itself became the accelerator.

The revolution as completion

The opening movement is comparative. Tocqueville asks what was singular about France, given that feudal burdens, clerical privilege, and royal administration existed across Europe. His answer is that France had gone further than its neighbours in destroying the political content of feudalism while retaining its irritating shell. Lords had lost the powers that once made them governors, judges, and protectors of a locality. They had kept their exemptions, their honorific rights, and a set of dues that were now simply extractions, cash payments for functions no one performed. The peasant who met his lord met a man who was no longer his ruler and was still his creditor. Tocqueville thought this ghostly privilege more explosive than a privilege still wrapped in responsibility. Where feudalism remained a system of government, it could still be endured as government. Where it survived only as inequality, it was intolerable the moment anyone proposed that inequalities required reasons.

Centralisation, on this account, is not Napoleon’s invention and not the Committee of Public Safety’s. The Bourbon monarchy had already drawn power out of provinces, estates, and municipalities, and had replaced them with intendants answering to the king’s council. The revolutionaries inherited a country that had been trained to look to the centre, and they used that training to finish the job: uniform laws, uniform administration, the abolition of the privileges that had obstructed uniformity. Napoleon stabilised the result. Tocqueville’s dry conclusion is that a Frenchman of the old regime, set down in the administration of the nineteenth century, would have recognised the machine. He would have been surprised mainly by its efficiency. This is the argument that later historians of the French state have had to confirm, qualify, or periodise. It is also the argument that makes the book more than a theory of rising expectations. Tocqueville is interested in what the revolution conserved, under a rhetoric of total break.

Classes that no longer shared a life

The middle of the book is a sociology of isolation. Tocqueville argues that the old regime had sorted the French into small, mutually suspicious groups and then forbidden them the shared institutions in which they might have learned public liberty. Nobility, bourgeoisie, and peasantry were not simply richer and poorer versions of one community. They were separated by tax exemptions, by rights of office, by the purchase of municipal and judicial posts, and by a style of life that had become a caste mark. The nobility had been drawn to court and away from its estates, which meant away from the only role that could have made its privileges intelligible. The bourgeoisie bought its way into petty privilege and then defended the privilege against the people below. The peasant, increasingly a small proprietor, was left alone with his taxes and his suspicion. When the crisis came, these groups did not know how to act together, because they had not been allowed to act together on anything smaller.

Writers stepped into the gap. Tocqueville’s chapter on men of letters is one of the most original things in the book, and one of the easiest to misread as mere anti-intellectualism. His point is structural. In a country where public questions could not be practised, they were written about. A literary class, fluent in general ideas and innocent of the compromises of administration, became the unofficial opposition, and the educated public learned politics from books that preferred principles to cases. The revolution’s abstract language, its willingness to remake a society from a definition, had been rehearsed in a public sphere that was allowed to think everything and to govern nothing. He draws the contrast with England, where the same century’s upper classes mixed in institutions that had real if limited power, and where political writing stayed closer to political practice. One need not accept the contrast at his valuation to see the mechanism. A monopoly of administration and a liberty of speculation is a dangerous pairing. It produces both a brittle state and a radical literature, each convinced the other is the whole problem.

The paradox of reform

The third movement contains the observation that later social science named after him. Louis XVI’s reign was not the depths of the old regime. It was a period in which the economy had been expanding, in which writers and ministers spoke openly about abuses, and in which the government itself attempted reforms: of labour services, of the grain trade, of local assemblies, of the worst confusions of the law. Tocqueville’s claim is that this improvement made revolution more likely, not less. Abuses that had seemed like the weather became choices, once a reformer had admitted they could be changed. A grievance patiently borne while it seems inevitable becomes shameful the moment a neighbouring province, or a neighbouring year, has been relieved of it. The most dangerous moment for a bad government, in the formulation associated with this book, is the moment it begins to mend. He supports the claim with the geography of the revolution itself. It was often fiercest not where feudal burdens were heaviest and peasants most beaten down, but where conditions had been improving, where peasants were already proprietors, and where the remaining seigneurial rights were most obviously relics.

There is a second, related observation about the government’s language. The monarchy did not only reform. It denounced, in its own edicts, the irrationality of the arrangements it was struggling to modify. It taught the country the vocabulary of its own indictment. When reform then stalled, blocked by the privileged bodies whose consent the monarchy still needed, the indictment was left in the air without the relief. Tocqueville is describing a trap. A reforming absolutism weakens the mystique that absolutism depends on, without yet possessing the consent that a constitutional regime depends on. It creates a public that expects change and an administration that cannot deliver it against the very corps it has spent a century hollowing out and a century preserving.

Liberty, equality, and what he did not finish

Under both arguments runs Tocqueville’s standing preoccupation, the rivalry between liberty and equality. The old regime had already accustomed the French to equality of the unfree, everyone equally subject to an intendant, and had denied them liberty of the local and corporate kind that would have made central power negotiable. The revolution, in his reading, seized equality with passion and treated liberty as a means, to be set aside whenever it obstructed the general will or the emergency. The authoritarian outcome was therefore not an accident that betrayed the revolution. It was one of the revolution’s possible completions, and in the event the one that lasted. He did not think this inevitable in the strong sense. He did think the old regime had made it the probable path, by destroying, before 1789, the habits that make free institutions work: the habit of compromise in a local body, the habit of looking at a neighbour as a fellow citizen rather than as a rival claimant on the state.

The book’s limits are the limits of its unfinished design and of its author. It is a study of causes more than of the revolution itself, and the notes he left towards a continuation are not the book. It is centred on France, and the English contrast is sometimes a moral rather than a fully researched one.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on the Tocqueville paradox is this book’s second argument, stated in the form later political science found useful: revolutions are especially likely not where oppression is worst and most static, but where things have begun to improve and expectations have pulled ahead of conditions. The card is right about the geography in Tocqueville’s evidence, the prospering and reforming regions as the intense ones, and right about the companion claim that is easier to forget: the revolution continued a centralisation the monarchy had started.
