Alexis de Tocqueville sat with the old administrative papers of the Île-de-France, the region around Paris, and compared them with the country downstream. In the districts near Paris, he wrote in L'Ancien Régime et la Révolution (1856), the old order had already been repaired the earliest and the deepest. Peasants were freer and more secure. The personal corvée, forced labor on the roads, had disappeared long before 1789. A regulation of 1772 had made the taille, the main direct tax, more regular, more moderate, and more evenly assessed: commissioners came to the parish, the community met, property was valued in public. Along the lower Loire, in the marshes of Poitou and the moors of Brittany, the old order had lasted best. That was where civil war caught and held, and where people fought the Revolution longest and hardest. The French, he said, seemed to find their position less bearable the better it became.

From that contrast he drew the sentence the book is remembered for. It is not always by going from bad to worse that a people falls into revolution. A people that has endured the most crushing laws without complaint, as if it did not feel them, often throws those laws off violently once the weight is lightened. The regime a revolution destroys is almost always better than the one just before it, and experience teaches that the most dangerous moment for a bad government is usually when it begins to reform. He gives the feeling a local shape. Feudalism at full strength had not made the French hate it as they hated it at the moment it was about to disappear. The smallest arbitrary acts of Louis XVI seemed harder to bear than the whole despotism of Louis XIV. A short imprisonment of Beaumarchais stirred Paris more than the dragonnades, Louis XIV's persecution of Protestants, had done.

The other finding in the same archives is that the Revolution did not invent the centralized state. The intendant, the king's man in the province, was already running local life. A village that wanted to repair a presbytery entered a chain of permissions that could take months. Nobles were exempt from the taille and had largely withdrawn from the work of governing their own districts. In England, Tocqueville argues, the gentry still taxed itself and still did that work, so a central office did not have to replace it. French revolutionaries and then Napoleon completed a machine the monarchy had already built.

When a revolt is read as a meter of misery, this book is the caution. The places that burned were not the places left worst. A reform can raise the temperature, because what remains of an abuse now looks chosen rather than inevitable. The papers also warn against treating 1789 as a clean break. A great deal of the state that came out of the Revolution was the old administration with a new name.

Charles Tilly's The Vendée (1964) tangled the map. Studying southern Anjou, he found the fiercest counterrevolution in the Mauges, districts being pulled into markets and rural textile work, not simply in the poorest country where old burdens sat heaviest. Who fought the Revolution does not line up in one straight path from "life got better" to "people revolted."
