Alexis de Tocqueville read the papers of the old royal administrators and came back with a map that runs the wrong way. In the généralité of the Île-de-France, around Paris, the personal corvée was already gone long before 1789, and a regulation of 1772 had made the assessment of the taille more regular, more moderate, and less arbitrary than in the généralités next door. Along the lower Loire, in the marshes of Poitou and the moors of Brittany, the old order had been left in place. That is where the civil war against the Revolution caught and lasted. His sentence, from the book he published in 1856, is the conclusion he drew: it is not always by going from bad to worse that a people falls into revolution, and the most dangerous moment for a bad government is usually the one when it begins to reform.

He put figures on the improvement. In the papers of the intendancy he found 40,000 livres distributed in the généralité of Tours in 1784 for charity workshops and the poor, 80,000 in Haute-Guyenne in 1779, and 48,000 in Normandy in 1787. The lease of the tax farms in 1786 brought in 14 million livres more than the lease of 1780. He quotes Arthur Young that in 1788 Bordeaux did more trade than Liverpool, and that French sea trade had doubled in twenty years. A short imprisonment of Beaumarchais, he writes, stirred Paris more than Louis XIV's dragonnades had. The abuse was lighter. People had been shown that it could be lifted, and what remained felt sharper.

The use of the book, when someone is deciding why 1789 happened, is to ask where the grievance was actually heaviest. Tocqueville's archives say the fiercest break came in places where the crown had already started to ease the load, and during a reign, Louis XVI's, in which trade and population were rising. He died in 1859 without writing the year-by-year history. The 1856 book is the study of the regime that produced the break.

The western civil war he points to also had later triggers. The rising in the Vendée broke out in March 1793, after the Revolution had attacked the church and ordered a levy of 300,000 men. His contrast between a reforming Île-de-France and a more intact west still matches the map. The war itself was not a simple vote by the places that had been left poorest in 1788.
