Alexis de Tocqueville did not write L'Ancien Régime et la Révolution (1856) from the memoirs of the men who made the French Revolution. He sat in the files of the administration they inherited. He says he spent a long time on what survived of it, in Paris and in several provinces, above all the archives of the intendancy of Tours, which he calls very complete. That generality sat in the center of France and held about a million people. The young archivist in charge was M. Grandmaison. What Tocqueville found there was a state already deep in private life. The royal intendant helped, blocked, and permitted. He had a great deal to promise and a great deal to give, and because the work was not public, families brought him their secret troubles. Centralization, on this evidence, was not a machine Napoleon invented after 1799. The monarchy had already built it, and the Revolution kept it.

The paradox is a map, not only a sentence. In the papers of the generality of the Île-de-France he found that the old regime had been reformed earliest, and most deeply, in the countryside around Paris. Along the lower Loire, in the marshes of Poitou and the moors of Brittany, it had been conserved. That is where the civil war against the Revolution burned longest. He draws the moral in one place. People do not always fall into revolution by going from bad to worse. A people that has borne crushing laws as if it could not feel them throws them off once the weight lightens. The regime a revolution destroys is almost always better than the one just before it, "et l'expérience apprend que le moment le plus dangereux pour un mauvais gouvernement est d'ordinaire celui où il commence à se réformer." The evil that was endured as inevitable becomes unbearable once someone has shown it can be removed. What is taken away uncovers what remains. Feudalism at full strength, he writes, never inspired in the French the hatred it inspired at the moment it was about to disappear.

The use of the book, when someone is deciding what 1789 was, is to separate the anger from a story of steady worsening. A government can be more hated in the decade it starts to lift old burdens than in the decades when the burdens were heavier. The remaining tax, the remaining privilege, the remaining arbitrary arrest, stands out because reform has made it look optional. Louis XVI's smallest acts of arbitrariness seemed harder to bear, Tocqueville says, than the whole despotism of Louis XIV. That is a claim about attention. It is also a claim about the archives: the state people rose against was already the centralized state the revolutionaries would enlarge.

Later writers kept the paradox and argued it was not enough. James C. Davies, in the American Sociological Review in 1962, redrew it as a curve: a long rise in what people get and what they expect, then a short reversal. Theda Skocpol, in States and Social Revolutions (1979), answered that a mood of that shape is too common to pick out France. A social revolution, on her evidence from France, Russia, and China, also takes a state that loses a fiscal or military contest, and rural communities able to revolt once its grip fails.
