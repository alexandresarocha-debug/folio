In 439 the Vandal king Geiseric took Carthage. North Africa was the western empire's grain supply and one of its richest tax bases. Peter Heather's The Fall of the Roman Empire (Macmillan, 2005) treats that loss as the blow the west did not recover from. In 468 the eastern and western governments made a last large attempt to take Africa back. Basiliscus led the fleet. It was defeated off Cape Bon. Ancient accounts agree that the expedition was extraordinarily expensive and disagree on the bill: one reckoning, from Candidus, is about 64,000 pounds of gold and 700,000 pounds of silver; Priscus and Procopius say 130,000 pounds of gold. Heather's conclusion is strategic. As late as 468, he argues, a victory would still have made a smaller western empire realistic, one holding Italy, Africa, southern Gaul, and Spain. The defeat closed that chance.

The other concrete case in his book is the start of the crisis, sixty years earlier. In 376 Goths crossed the Danube with the emperor Valens's permission, under pressure from the Huns. On 9 August 378 Valens was killed at Adrianople and the eastern field army with him. Heather reads this as an outside shock to a state that was still rich and still dangerous, not as the last symptom of a long internal decay. The Huns, on his account, pushed armed groups into the empire who could not be destroyed or fully absorbed. The west then lost the African revenues that paid for a full Roman army. The deposition of the last western emperor in 476 matters, in this telling, because by then the court could no longer fund the machine that had made the title worth holding.

The practical distinction is between a state that rotted and a state that lost its paycheck. Heather's west is the second. A fourth-century empire that could still move field armies is a different creature from the fifth-century court that could not replace Africa. Anyone deciding "when Rome fell" has to say whether they mean Adrianople, Carthage, the failed rescue of 468, or the empty western throne.

Guy Halsall's Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West (2007) uses the same dates and reverses the emphasis. In that reading the armed groups were largely produced by Roman civil war and Roman recruiting, and the external push is asked to explain too much. The capture of Carthage in 439 is a fact in both books. What it proves is the argument.
