A strong empire, not a dying one

Peter Heather's The Fall of the Roman Empire: A New History, published in 2005, is a narrative of the western collapse that starts by rejecting the premise it is usually given. The fourth-century empire, in his account, was not a hollow shell waiting for a moral explanation. It was large, taxed, and militarily serious. It could put great armies in the field. Its administration reached the provinces. Its problem was not that Romans had forgotten how to govern. Its problem, when it came, was a shock from outside that the political system then made worse. Heather writes against two habits at once: the Gibbonian story in which internal rot does most of the work, and the gentler late-antique story in which the fifth century is mostly a negotiation among partners.

The opening movement is therefore a portrait of strength. He describes the late Roman state as a going concern, with a professional army and a fiscal machinery designed to feed it. The Germanic societies beyond the Rhine and the Danube are part of this portrait rather than a mere cloud on the horizon. Centuries of trade, war, subsidy, and recruitment had changed them. Chiefs who could deliver warriors to Rome, or threaten the frontier, grew richer and more powerful than their ancestors. Heather's provocative continuation of this thought, developed further in later work, is that Rome helped to create the kind of neighbour that could eventually harm it. In this book the point is already present. The barbarians of the fifth century are not timeless tribes. They are products of a long frontier.

The Huns and the Gothic crisis

The mechanism of collapse, for Heather, begins on the steppe. In the 370s the Huns moved west and broke the political order of the Gothic groups north of the Black Sea and the Danube. Large numbers of Goths sought admission to the empire in 376. Rome let them across and then mishandled them. Officials exploited the refugees. The Goths rebelled. In 378, at Adrianople, they destroyed a Roman field army and killed the emperor Valens. Heather treats this defeat as a catastrophe the empire did not fully reverse. Theodosius fought, bargained, and settled Gothic forces inside the Roman system. He did not remove them. A first-class military power had been forced to accept an armed population it could not digest.

That settlement is the hinge between an external shock and an internal political problem. The Goths under leaders such as Alaric wanted land, pay, and a recognised place. Western politics, already prone to usurpation and to the use of barbarian troops against Roman rivals, gave them opportunities. Heather does not deny Roman mismanagement. He denies that mismanagement is the origin of the crisis. Without the Hunnic displacement, the Gothic army is not inside the empire in that form, at that moment. The domino starts outside.

A second wave, and the loss of Africa

A second crisis, in the years around 405 to 408, pushes the western government past recovery. Further groups, among them Vandals, Alans, and Sueves, cross the Rhine. Radagaisus invades Italy and is destroyed, at a cost. Alaric's Goths, after years of being hired and betrayed by court factions, sack Rome in 410. Britain slips out of imperial control amid its own usurpations. None of these events, taken alone, is presented as the end. Together they plant armed groups in Gaul and Spain who do not go home. They carve out territories and live off Roman tax and Roman land.

The decisive territorial loss, in Heather's fiscal story, is North Africa. The Vandals cross from Spain in 429 and take Carthage in 439. Africa had been the western empire's richest source of revenue and a major source of grain for Italy. Its loss is not a symbolic blow. It is a cut in the money that paid the army. A state that loses revenue loses soldiers. A state that loses soldiers loses more territory. Heather's phrase for the result is a spiral, and the spiral is the real explanation of the fifth century once the initial shock has landed. The failed attempts to retake Africa, including the great expedition of 468, matter because they show the centre still trying, and still able to gather resources, and still losing the one campaign that might have broken the spiral.

Why the west ended and the east did not

The last western decades, in this telling, are the arithmetic of that spiral. Generals such as Aetius manage coalitions, Attila's Huns extract and destroy and then vanish as a unified threat after his death, and the imperial office in Italy becomes a prize fought over by commanders who are often themselves of barbarian origin. In 476 the last western emperor is deposed. Heather does not need 476 to be a metaphysical end of Roman culture. He needs it to mark the end of a western Roman state that could tax and field an army of its own. The kingdoms that replaced it were real powers. They were not the empire.

The eastern empire is his control case. It faced Goths, Huns, and Persians, and it was no less Christian and no less late Roman than the west. It survived because it kept its richest provinces, because Constantinople was extraordinarily defensible, and because its rulers could still pay, bribe, and divide their enemies. When the east did commit a fortune to the recovery of Africa and failed, the failure hurt. It did not destroy the eastern tax base. The comparison is Heather's answer to every internal-cause theory. Whatever is said about decadence, bureaucracy, or the church has to explain why it killed one half and spared the other. An external shock concentrated on the west, followed by the loss of the western revenue that might have answered it, does explain the difference.

What he will and will not claim

Heather is explicit about what the narrative is not. It is not a return to the idea that ancient Germanic nations marched out of the north to claim a destiny. The groups involved were composite, their identities were in motion, and many of their leaders wanted a place inside the Roman order. It is also not a claim that the fifth century was a polite handover. Armed minorities took the provinces by war and by the threat of war, and the provincial population lived with the consequences. He uses the written sources, Ammianus above all for the beginning, and he uses them as evidence of a military and diplomatic crisis rather than as moral literature.

The argument has been resisted by historians, Guy Halsall among them, who think Roman civil war and the politics of accommodation explain more than the Hunnic push. That dispute is about weight. Heather does not say that Roman politicians acted wisely. He says that a rich and capable state was confronted with a migration crisis it had not generated by its own decadence, and that it lost the west by losing the fiscal means to finish the wars the crisis started. The book is a new history because it puts the outside shock back at the front of the story without restoring the old nationalist picture of the invaders.

Why it matters here

Two Folio cards use this book, and they use different parts of it. The card on the Hunnic domino is Heather's own thesis: the Huns displace the Goths, Adrianople follows, a second wave crosses the Rhine, the Vandals take Africa, and a shrinking tax base produces a death spiral. The west falls because of that external military shock and the fiscal feedback it starts, not because Romans became decadent. This walkthrough is that thesis in the order the book argues it, including the point the card depends on and a sentence can miss. The fourth-century empire has to be strong in the story, or the shock is not a shock. It is merely the last nudge to a corpse.

The card on pots and material collapse is the other link. Ward-Perkins shows specialised production and ordinary comfort coming apart in the west. Heather supplies a political sequence that can make that material story causal rather than atmospheric. Long-distance industry needed a state that could keep routes, customers, and demand intact. On Heather's account, the state lost Africa and then the means to restore itself, while the east, which kept its revenue, kept more of its complexity. The pots and the narrative answer different questions. One asks what households ceased to own. The other asks which wars and which lost taxes made that contraction likely. Read together, they describe a fall that is both military and material, and that does not require Gibbon's verdict on religion to do the explanatory work.
