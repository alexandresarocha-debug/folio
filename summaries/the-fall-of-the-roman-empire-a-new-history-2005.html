In the spring of 376, Goths gathered on the north bank of the Danube and asked to cross. They were running from the Huns. The emperor Valens let them in, hoping for recruits. Two years later, on 9 August 378, those Goths killed him at Adrianople and destroyed the army he had with him. Peter Heather's The Fall of the Roman Empire (2005) treats that defeat as the start of an outside shock. On his reading the fourth-century empire was large, taxed, and still able to fight. What it could not absorb was a chain of armed migrations set moving by the Huns, first in 376 and again in the opening years of the fifth century, when Vandals, Alans, and Sueves crossed the Rhine.

The break he isolates is Africa. On 19 October 439 the Vandal king Geiseric took Carthage. North Africa was the richest tax base the western government still had, and its grain fed Rome. In 441 a joint expedition with Constantinople, assembled to take the provinces back, was called off. Attila and his brother Bleda had attacked the eastern Balkans, and the eastern troops went home. The west had to accept Geiseric's hold on the African provinces. Heather's mechanism is fiscal. Lose that revenue and you cannot pay the field army at the old strength. A smaller army loses more territory, and the revenue falls again. By 476, when Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus, the western court had been living inside that spiral for a generation.

The argument tells you what to count. Luxury, bad emperors, and religious change can all be documented and still sit beside the point if a dated loss of tax, in 439, confirmed by a cancelled campaign in 441, is what made the army unaffordable. Guy Halsall's Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West (2007) reads the Danube crossing of 376 as a Roman administrative failure, and argues that the migrations followed the western collapse. The shared dates make the dispute checkable. One book has a functioning empire broken by a specific loss of African revenue. The other has a state already failing, and armed groups moving into the gap.
