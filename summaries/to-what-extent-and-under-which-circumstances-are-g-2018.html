The question in the title

In 2018 Victoria Sisk, Alexander Burgoyne, Jingze Sun, Jennifer Butler and Brooke Macnamara published two meta-analyses under a title that is itself the argument: To What Extent and Under Which Circumstances Are Growth Mind-Sets Important to Academic Achievement? The title refuses the yes-or-no on which the public discussion had settled. After Carol Dweck's Mindset became a bestseller, schools adopted the growth mindset as a programme, and critics replied that the programme was a fad. Sisk and her colleagues ask a more awkward pair of questions. How large is the relationship between holding a growth mindset and doing well academically, once the studies are gathered rather than cited one by one? And how large is the effect of interventions that try to instil a growth mindset, once those experiments are gathered too? Extent, and circumstances. The paper is organised as two separate quantitative reviews because those are different claims. A belief can correlate with grades without being easy to teach. An intervention can work in a laboratory and wash out in ordinary classrooms. Averaging them together would answer a question nobody needs answered.

A meta-analysis, in the form they use, takes many studies that measured roughly the same thing, puts their results on a common scale, and asks what the studies say in the aggregate and what features of the studies explain the scatter. It is not a new experiment with children. It is an argument about the literature that already exists. That is the right instrument for a claim that has become famous, because fame selects the striking studies. The unstriking ones remain in the journals, and a review that does not go and get them will reproduce the fame rather than test it.

The first meta-analysis: mindsets and achievement

The first review asks about the association. Researchers have measured students' mindsets, usually by asking how far they agree that intelligence is fixed or that it can be developed, and have recorded academic achievement, usually grades or test scores. If the strong popular claim were true, students who endorse a growth mindset would be substantially ahead of students who endorse a fixed one. The studies can be lined up and the average association inspected, and the association can be allowed to differ by age, by how the mindset was measured, and by the kind of achievement on the other side of the correlation.

What Sisk and colleagues find is that the overall relationship is weak. Students with more of a growth mindset do a little better on average, and the little is small enough that mindset, as measured, is not a major account of why some students achieve more than others. A weak association is still a real pattern. It is not a foundation for telling schools that beliefs about intelligence are the lever that moves attainment. The circumstances matter less here than the extent. Looking across the literature, the correlation does not become large in some obvious subgroup and negligible in another. It stays modest. That result is already enough to discipline the book's reception. A mechanism that sounds transformative in a case study can be a small slice of the variance in a correlation.

The second meta-analysis: interventions

The second review asks about the causal claim, which is the claim schools acted on. In an intervention study, some students are taught the growth-mindset message, often through readings, lessons, or writing exercises about the malleability of ability, and others are not. Achievement is measured afterwards. This is a different literature from the correlational one. A student may endorse a growth mindset because they are already doing well, which would produce a correlation without any effect of the belief. An experiment that assigns the lesson is the way to see whether the lesson does anything.

The average effect of these interventions on academic performance is very small. That is the paper's central blow to the programme as it was sold. It is not a finding of nothing, and the authors do not write it as a finding of nothing. It is a finding that the typical mindset programme, across the studies that measured achievement, shifts achievement by very little. The extent question has a discouraging answer. The circumstances question has a more interesting one. The effects look larger for students who are at risk of failing, and more generally for students whose academic situation gives a change of belief something to bite on. Even there the paper is cautious. Larger than very small is not the same as large. A benefit concentrated among students at risk is still a benefit worth understanding, and it is not the universal lift that a school-wide poster promises.

Why heterogeneity is the real result

The intellectual contribution of the paper is the demand that both answers be given at once. A reviewer who reported only the overall weakness would be right about the average and wrong about the literature, because the literature contains a hint of a subgroup. A reviewer who reported only the at-risk hint would be selling the hint as if it were the average, which is how the idea became a fad in the first place. Sisk and colleagues keep the two numbers in the same frame. Growth mindsets are, on the published evidence available to them, a weak correlate of achievement and a weak intervention on average, with a possible special role for students who have more to gain.

They also give reasons why an attractive mechanism can generate a small effect without anyone having fabricated the mechanism. Mindset measures are short questionnaires, and a short questionnaire is a noisy portrait of a belief. Interventions differ wildly in length, in skill, and in whether the surrounding classroom contradicts the lesson the moment the student leaves it. Achievement is a distal outcome. A belief might change a student's willingness to try a hard problem next Tuesday and still fail to move a grade that also depends on prior knowledge, teaching, attendance and the test. A small average is what one expects if the mechanism is real and the outcome is far downstream, and it is also what one expects if the mechanism has been oversold. The meta-analyses by themselves do not decide which of those readings is right. They decide that any reading must live with a small mean.

What the review cannot say

A meta-analysis inherits the studies it includes. If the field has published its successes more readily than its failures, the average will be too optimistic, and the authors treat publication bias as part of the problem rather than as a footnote. If the best interventions are rare and the mass of interventions are thin classroom exercises, the average describes the mass. It does not prove that a carefully built lesson, aimed at a chosen group of students, in a school whose norms support the behaviour the lesson encourages, would also be tiny. That more precise possibility is what a later national experiment was designed to test. Equally, the meta-analyses cannot show that mindset is an important cause of achievement that the questionnaires simply failed to capture. Unmeasured importance is not a finding. It is a hope.

The subsequent argument has been sharper than a dispute about one number. A further review led by Macnamara and Burgoyne has argued that the more positive results in this literature cluster in studies with methodological weaknesses or with financial conflicts of interest, and other researchers have disputed that interpretation. The 2018 paper does not have to win that later fight in order to stand. Its own claim is already limited. To a small extent, and perhaps more under circumstances of academic risk. Anyone quoting the paper as proof that mindset is worthless is adding a word the results do not contain. Anyone quoting Dweck's book as if this review did not exist is subtracting the denominator.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Growth mindset: big idea, small effects, depends on this paper for the phrase that follows the comma. The card says the overall relationship between mindsets and academic achievement was weak, and that mindset interventions had very small average effects on academic performance, though possibly larger effects for students at risk of failing. Those clauses are this paper. The card then sets beside them the 2019 national experiment, in which a brief lesson did help lower-achieving students under supportive norms, and Dweck's own warning against praising empty effort. The honest summary the card offers, a real but modest lever, is what you get if you refuse to let either the book or the meta-analyses speak alone. This review is the restraint. It tells a reader how far the early laboratory effects, and the bestseller that grew from them, had outrun the average evidence about grades.
