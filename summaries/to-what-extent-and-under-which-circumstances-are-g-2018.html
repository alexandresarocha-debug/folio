Victoria Sisk, Alexander Burgoyne, Jingze Sun, Jennifer Butler, and Brooke Macnamara published two meta-analyses in 2018 on the claim that a growth mindset raises grades. The first collected every eligible correlation between a mindset measure and an academic outcome: 273 effect sizes, 365,915 people. The average correlation was r = .10. Of those 273 effects, 157, or 58%, were not statistically different from zero. Sixteen, or 6%, went the other way, with a growth mindset accompanying worse achievement. The remaining 100 were positive and significant. Allowing for a typical test reliability of .80 would lift the average only to about r = .12.

The second meta-analysis asked whether a lesson that teaches a growth mindset raises achievement relative to a comparison group. Forty-three effect sizes, 57,155 people, averaged d = 0.08. Income changed the picture, inside a limit the authors state. For students from low-income households the intervention effect was d = 0.34, from seven effect sizes, with a confidence interval from 0.07 to 0.62. For middle- and upper-income students it was d = 0.03 and not significant. Student income was missing for 28 effect sizes, so that split uses a subset. Sisk and her colleagues warn that few studies and small samples sit under the low-income number.

A school buying a mindset program on the promise of large average gains is buying a result these figures do not show. A correlation of .10 will not sort a classroom. The interventions looked more useful among students who were already at an economic disadvantage, and that estimate rests on a handful of effects. A year later, a preregistered national experiment by David Yeager and colleagues found a gain of 0.10 grade points for lower-achieving ninth graders after an online lesson of less than an hour, and no clear grade gain for students already above their school's median. That is the same small size, concentrated where there was room to move.

Source. Sisk, V. F., Burgoyne, A. P., Sun, J., Butler, J. L., and Macnamara, B. N. (2018). To what extent and under which circumstances are growth mind-sets important to academic achievement? Two meta-analyses. Psychological Science, 29(4), 549–571. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797617739704
