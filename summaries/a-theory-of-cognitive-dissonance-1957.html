Leon Festinger’s 1957 book, A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance, starts from a rumor that should have been unwelcome. After the great earthquake in India in 1934, J. Prasad collected the stories circulating in places that had felt the shock and had not been badly damaged. The rumors that spread were not reassurances. They predicted worse disasters, soon. Festinger’s question, which he says in the foreword was the hunch that produced the theory, was why frightened people would pass along news that gave them more to fear. His answer was that the rumors were justifying fear the people already had. The ground had shaken, they were scared, and nothing in front of them was ruined. A story about a coming catastrophe made the fear fit the facts.

The same arithmetic, he argued, applies when two of a person’s own beliefs clash. A person who smokes and who has come to believe that smoking is dangerous holds two cognitions that do not sit together. The direct way out is to stop smoking. The routes people actually take are often cheaper. They decide the evidence is thin, they add a benefit (“it steadies me,” “it keeps my weight down”), or they shrink the danger by setting it next to something else that also kills. Festinger’s claim is that the discomfort pushes toward some resolution, and that changing the belief is often easier than changing the habit, the purchase, or the public statement already made.

In ordinary decisions this is the tug you feel after you have already acted. You buy the more expensive appliance, take the job, or agree to the operation, and then the reviews, the commute, or the side effects look worse than they did in the store. The pressure is to touch up the reasons: to talk up the features you chose and talk down the ones you passed over, so the decision and the belief match. A doctor who has started a treatment, or a manager who has announced a plan, is in the same spot. New information that says the choice was poor has to get past a person who now has a stake in the choice having been sound.

Later work narrowed the theory. Dissonance came to be treated as sharpest when the person chose the inconsistent act, which the earthquake rumors, felt by people who had not chosen the quake, do not show. And in 2024 a 39-lab study of 4,898 people failed to find the classic effect of high choice versus low choice on attitude after a counterattitudinal essay. The discomfort of a contradiction is still a useful description. The claim that it reliably rewrites the belief, on cue, is weaker than this book made it sound.
