The idea, stated as a theory

A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance, Leon Festinger's 1957 book, is the attempt to turn a field observation and a scattering of experiments into a general account of how people handle inconsistency. A cognition, in his usage, is any belief, knowledge or opinion a person holds about himself, about his behaviour, or about the world. Two cognitions are consonant if one follows from the other. They are dissonant if, considering them alone, the opposite of one would follow from the other. Smoking while believing that smoking harms you is his plain example, and it is the example Folio's card uses. Dissonance is the discomfort of that relation, and it is motivating in the way hunger is motivating. It pushes for reduction. The book is a map of the ways the push is resisted and the ways it is relieved. It is a theoretical monograph, so the walkthrough follows its argument part by part rather than trial by trial.

Magnitude, resistance and reduction

Festinger's first move is to say when dissonance is large. It is larger when the cognitions involved are important, and it is larger when dissonant cognitions are many and consonant ones are few, relative to each other. A trivial inconsistency among trivial beliefs hardly matters. A contradiction that touches a person's competence, morality or major commitment matters a great deal. This is why the theory is not the claim that people are tidy logicians. Most inconsistencies are ignored because they are cheap. The ones the theory is about are the ones that are expensive to leave in place.

Reduction has a small menu. A person can change a cognition, including a cognition about his own behaviour, which is the menu item called changing one's conduct. He can add new cognitions that are consonant with the side he is keeping, which is the menu item called rationalisation only when one dislikes it. He can reduce the importance of the whole conflict, so that the inconsistency is still there and no longer drives him. Which item he chooses depends on resistance to change. Behaviour is hard to change when it is pleasurable, public, or tied to addiction and habit. Beliefs are hard to change when they are anchored in reality, widely shared, or central to the self. The path taken is the path of least resistance. Festinger's smokers, if they cannot stop, are predicted to doubt the evidence, to add beliefs about filters and statistics and exceptions, or to decide that a long life is less important than they had thought. The theory's content is in that prediction's structure. People do not reduce dissonance at random. They reduce it where the cognition will give.

After a decision

The first large application is the consequence of having chosen. A decision between two attractive options creates dissonance by construction. The good features of the option one rejected, and the bad features of the option one chose, are now dissonant with the choice. Before the decision they were simply the terms of the problem. After it, they are an accusation. Festinger predicts the spreading of alternatives. Once the choice is made, the chosen option will be rated more favourably than it was just before, and the rejected option less favourably. Information will be sought and read in ways that support the spread. The harder and closer the decision, the larger the dissonance and the larger the spread one should see, provided the decision is hard to reverse. An easily reversed choice need not be justified, because it can be undone.

This chapter is the theory's account of post-decision rationalisation, which the card lists among the phenomena dissonance was asked to explain. It is also a claim about timing. The moment of commitment is the moment the motives change. Before it, a person should be relatively even-handed, because dissonance has not yet been created. After it, the same person has a reason to be biased. Festinger reviews experiments in which people rank appliances, or other close options, and then rank them again after a choice, and he takes the shift as the signature of the chapter. The practical sting is that a person who has just decided is a poor judge of the decision. The judgement is now doing the work of reducing discomfort.

Forced compliance

The second application is the one the one-dollar experiment made famous, and the book gives it the general form. If a person says or does something contrary to a private attitude, dissonance follows, and it is larger when the external pressure that produced the behaviour is only just sufficient. A huge reward, or a severe threat, justifies the compliance. The person can keep the attitude and point to the pressure. A barely adequate reward, or a mild threat that somehow still works, leaves the compliance unjustified, and the attitude is predicted to move into line with what was said or done. Festinger discusses both reward and punishment. The insufficient-punishment case is the twin of the insufficient-reward case. A child who refrains from playing with a toy under a mild threat is predicted to decide the toy is less attractive, because the restraint was real and the threat is too small to explain it. A severe threat explains the restraint, so the toy can stay attractive.

The chapter is careful about the boundary. Compliance that does not occur produces no dissonance of this kind. Pressure so heavy that anyone would have complied produces compliance without attitude change. The theoretically interesting band is the band in which the person could have refused and did not, and cannot quite tell himself why. Festinger treats that band as the place where public behaviour rewrites private belief. The 1959 experiment with Carlsmith, still in the future when the book was written, is the cleanest later occupant of the band. The book states the logic the experiment would use: hold the counter-attitudinal act constant, vary the justification, and look for attitude change where the justification is poor.

Information and other people

The third application is selective exposure. If dissonance is uncomfortable, information that would increase it is a threat, and information that would reduce it is a relief. Festinger predicts a preference for consonant information and an avoidance of dissonant information, with important qualifications he does not hide. A person who is confident of refuting the dissonant material may seek it out, because knocking it down would reduce dissonance further. A person whose dissonance is small may tolerate exposure that a person near the limit will not. The chapter is the theory's most easily abused prediction, since a blanket claim that nobody ever reads the other side is obviously false. Festinger's actual claim is comparative and conditional. Voluntary exposure is biased towards support when dissonance is present and when the person does not expect to be able to dismiss the challenge. Involuntary exposure, the leaflet through the door, is a different problem, and he treats the resulting dissonance as something that then has to be reduced by the usual menu.

The fourth application is social support, and it is the bridge back to When Prophecy Fails. Disagreement with people one respects is itself a source of dissonance, because their contrary belief is a cognition one has to live beside. Agreement is a reduction. Festinger lays out the ways a person can respond to disagreeing others: change one's own view, try to change theirs, add supporters, or reject the others as people whose views do not count as evidence. Groups, on this account, are machines for manufacturing consonance, and the rejection of deviates is one of the machine's operations. The failed-prophecy case is the illustration he wants the reader to have in mind. When reality will not agree with the group, the group can seek new members so that social reality will. The chapter makes that move a general prediction about social comparison, not a curiosity about saucers. It also says when disagreement will not be dissonant: when the other person is not a relevant comparison, when the issue is not important, and when one's own position is already heavily supported.

Why it matters here

Folio's card calls dissonance one of the most influential ideas in social psychology because the 1957 book gave it this range. Effort justification, post-decision rationalisation, the stickiness of sunk costs, and the rewriting of beliefs after public compliance are presented there as one process, differing only in which cognitions are in conflict and which of them will move. The card is right that changing the belief is often easier than changing the behaviour. That sentence is Festinger's resistance principle, said in plain language. The smoker, the buyer who has just chosen, and the person who has just spoken against his own view are all looking for the cognition that will give.

The card also records the dent. A 2024 multi-site registered replication of the induced-compliance paradigm did not find the classic attitude change, so the forced-compliance chapter is the part to hold most lightly, even though it is the part the one-dollar study made famous. The rest of the book is a framework rather than a single effect, and frameworks are not refuted by one failed replication. They are kept, trimmed, or replaced according to how well the whole menu continues to organise evidence. Tavris and Aronson's Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me), the card's readable modern companion, keeps the menu and carries it into law, memory, therapy and marriage. Festinger's book is the menu. Read it for the structure: magnitude, resistance, decision, compliance, information, social support. The structure is what the later arguments, including the arguments about replication, are arguments about.
