In 1583 the Inquisition arrested a miller from the village of Montereale, in Friuli, in the hills north of Venice. His name was Domenico Scandella, but everyone called him Menocchio. He was about 51. He had been telling anyone who would listen, for years, what he thought about God and the world. Carlo Ginzburg came across his file in 1963, while he was looking through records of witchcraft trials. He returned in 1970 to study the two trials properly, and published the book in 1976.

At his first hearing Menocchio explained how the world began. In the English translation he says: "all was chaos, that is, earth, air, water, and fire were mixed together; and out of that bulk a mass formed - just as cheese is made out of milk - and worms appeared in it, and these were the angels." God, he said, was created out of the same mass, one of the angels. He added that Jesus was born of a man and that the Pope had no power from God. He held that the only sin was to harm your neighbour. About Latin in the courts he said it was a betrayal of the poor, because they could not follow what was being said.

The trial records also tell us what he read. Several books were found at his arrest, and others come up in his testimony: the Bible in Italian, Il Fioretto della Bibbia, a translation of Mandeville's Travels, the Golden Legend, and later Boccaccio's Decameron and a book thought to be an Italian Qur'an. Many were lent to him. Ginzburg noticed that what Menocchio said about these books often differs from what they say. He was not copying. He was picking out sentences that fitted ideas he already had.

The sequence of events is unusually well documented. Menocchio was arrested in October 1583 and abjured in May 1584. He then spent another 20 months in prison, and was released in January 1586 on condition of wearing a garment marked with flaming crosses and staying in Montereale. He did not stop talking. After some 12 years he was arrested again, as a relapsed heretic, and burned at the stake in 1599, aged about 67. There is no record of the execution itself. The only trace is a notarial act of 16 August 1599 that calls his son Stefano the son of the late Domenico Scandella.

What this means in real life: most people in the past left no writing, and what we know of them often comes from the people who judged or ruled them. A heresy trial is a rare case where a miller's words were taken down, though always filtered through the inquisitor's questions. Ginzburg's lesson is to read such records closely, ask what the person did with the ideas they met, and not assume that ordinary people only absorbed what the elite handed down. His argument was that ideas travelled both ways, between book culture and village talk.

The big weak point is that Ginzburg admits the oldest part of his argument, a long oral peasant tradition behind Menocchio's cosmos, is unproven. Critics also say one unusual man cannot stand for a whole class.

Source. Ginzburg, C. (1976). Il formaggio e i vermi. Turin: Einaudi. English translation by John and Anne C. Tedeschi, The Cheese and the Worms: The Cosmos of a Sixteenth-Century Miller, Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press, 1980.
