Howard Wainer, a statistician at the National Board of Medical Examiners, nominated a candidate for the most dangerous equation: de Moivre's rule that the variation of an average shrinks with the square root of the sample size. It is not dangerous to use, he wrote, but ignoring it has been costly for centuries.

His first example is the Trial of the Pyx, the English check on newly minted coins that has run since the twelfth century. Samples of coins were weighed together, and the allowed error grew in proportion to the number of coins rather than with its square root, which left room for the mint to shave coins unnoticed.

His best-known example is a map of US counties by kidney-cancer rate. The counties in the lowest tenth tend to be very rural, Midwestern, Southern and Western, and it is tempting to credit clean country living. But the counties with the highest rates are also mostly rural. What the two groups share is a small population, and therefore rates that vary much more from chance alone.

He then turned to schools. In the late 1990s the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation began supporting small schools across the US, and by 2001 it had given grants to education projects totalling about $1.7 billion. Part of the case was that small schools were common among the top performers. With Harris Zwerling, Wainer looked at all 1,662 Pennsylvania schools reporting 5th-grade reading scores. Of the 50 top-scoring schools, six were among the smallest 3%, four times more than chance would give. But of the 50 lowest-scoring schools, nine were among the 50 smallest. Small schools were over-represented at both ends, as de Moivre's equation predicts, and overall the link between size and scores was essentially flat.

What this means in real life: before celebrating or worrying about a top or bottom ranking, check how small the unit is.

Caution: this is an essay, and the school and county analyses are illustrations rather than full studies. Small schools may have other effects that average scores do not capture.
