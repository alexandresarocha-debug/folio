If pigeons can build rituals around random food, do people do the same with random rewards? Koichi Ono tested this with university students.

Twenty undergraduates sat in a booth with three large levers, a counter and three coloured signal lights. A point on the counter, together with a red lamp and a brief buzzer, served as the reward. Points came on a timer, either at fixed intervals or at varying intervals, of 30 or 60 seconds, whatever the students did. The coloured signals came on with no consistent link to the points or to behaviour. The students were not asked to do anything in particular.

Three of the 20 students developed persistent superstitious behaviour. One settled into a pattern of a few short lever pulls followed by a long pull. A second ended up touching many things in the booth. A third showed what Ono called sensory superstition, responding in a biased way to the signals. Most of the other students did not show consistent superstitious behaviour.

Ono's conclusion was balanced. Rewards can shape human behaviour even when there is no link between the behaviour and the reward, and in some cases they produce stable superstitious patterns. But superstition is not a consistent outcome when people receive rewards that do not depend on what they do.

What this means in real life: random good luck can start a ritual in some people, but not in most, and the rituals that do form are often idiosyncratic.

Caution: the sample was small and the setting artificial. Students may have been trying to work out what the experimenter wanted, which is different from believing a ritual causes luck. This page relies on the paper's published abstract.
