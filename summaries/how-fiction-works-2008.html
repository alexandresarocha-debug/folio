A critic's account of the novel's instruments

James Wood's How Fiction Works, published in 2008, is a critic's book about the tools of fiction, written as a continuous argument rather than as a textbook with exercises. Its central claim is that the novel's distinctive instrument is free indirect style, the sentence that belongs grammatically to the narrator and idiomatically to the character. Around that claim he builds accounts of detail, of character, and of the kind of truth a novel can tell. He is not neutral among these tools. He thinks some of them are the living tradition of the form, running from Jane Austen and Gustave Flaubert into the present, and he thinks some workshop pieties flatten that tradition into rules. The book is therefore both a description and a defence. It describes how a sentence can carry two minds at once, and it defends the realism that uses that sentence against the idea that realism has been exposed as a set of tricks and ought to retire.

Free indirect style is easiest to see by setting three sentences beside one another, as demonstrations rather than as quotations from any novel. A narrator may report a thought from outside: she feared she would be late. A novelist may quote the thought: I am going to be late again, she thought. Or the narrative may take on the thought without marks and without a tag: she was going to be late again, of course. The third sentence is the narrator's in its person and its tense, and the character's in the impatience of "of course". Wood's argument is that this third way is the novel's great invention because it lets a book be inside a person and outside them in the same instant. Sympathy and irony are not two chapters. They are two readings of one line. The reader is given the character's language as the medium of the scene, and is given, by the framing intelligence of the narration, a way to hear that language as biased, self-loving, or simply wrong.

Austen, Flaubert, and the double voice

Wood takes Jane Austen as the English writer who brought this double voice to a high polish, especially in Emma. The reader spends long stretches inside Emma Woodhouse's confidence, seeing Harriet, Mr Elton, and Jane Fairfax as Emma sees them, and the prose does Emma the honour of using her own complacencies. At the same time the book is built so that those complacencies will be exposed. The exposure does not require a narrator to step on stage and scold. It requires the reader to notice that the rhythm of Emma's certainty is the rhythm of a mistake. Free indirect style is what makes that noticing intimate. A purely external narration would make Emma a specimen. A first-person confession would make her the only authority, or would have to plant clues against her in the manner of an unreliable diary. Austen's method does something less mechanical. It lets the mistake be felt from within at the moment it is committed, and lets the correction arrive as a change in how the same kind of sentence sounds.

Flaubert, for Wood as for so many critics, is the writer who made the method the default climate of a book rather than a heightening used at chosen moments. In Madame Bovary the world is continually rendered through Emma's appetites and boredoms, in sentences that no one in the novel speaks and that no neutral reporter would have chosen. The famous trouble this caused, when the book was prosecuted, is part of Wood's interest. If the sensuous noticing cannot be firmly assigned either to a judging author or to a deluded character, then a prosecutor who wants to know who is speaking has been defeated by the technique. Wood does not treat that defeat as a party trick. He treats it as the moral risk and the moral capacity of the novel. A form that can inhabit a desire it does not endorse is a form that can tell the truth about desire without either sermon or complicity. Later writers, from Henry James to the novelists of Wood's own day, inherit the risk whenever they let a sentence lean into a character and then lean back.

Detail, and the life of a phrase

The same double attention governs what Wood wants from detail. A detail can be a mere certificate of reality, an object named so that the reader will believe a room exists. Roland Barthes had called a version of this the reality effect, the barometer on the wall that serves no plot and signifies only that we are in the real world. Wood is interested in a stricter detail, the one that is both stubbornly itself and saturated with a particular consciousness. The right detail, on his account, is not a piece of inventory and not a symbol with a detachable meaning. It is a moment of noticing that could belong to this character and not to a general narrator of facts. Free indirect style is what makes that belonging possible. When the narration starts to see as the character sees, the choice of detail becomes a revelation of the chooser. A list of furnishings tells you there is a house. A single object, seized on for a private reason, tells you who is in the house and what they want.

This is why Wood is impatient with a flat rule that fiction should always show and never tell. Showing, if it means a camera-like record of behaviour, can be as conventional as any summary, and it can abandon the inner life that the novel is specially able to reach. Telling, if it means the narrator's intelligence selecting and judging, is one of the ways free indirect style works. The sentence "she was going to be late again, of course" shows a mind and tells a judgement at once. Wood's allegiance is to that fusion, not to a camp in the workshop war. He wants fiction that is free to think, and he thinks the thinking is most alive when it is pitched in a character's key. A novel of pure external behaviour and a novel of authorial lecture both give up the instrument he is describing.

Flatness, roundness, and what counts as life

On character, Wood picks a quarrel with the usual moral ranking of E. M. Forster's terms. Forster had called flat characters those built around a single idea, summarisable in a sentence, and round characters those capable of surprising us in a convincing way. The ranking that followed, in a great deal of teaching, treats flatness as a fault and roundness as the aim. Wood argues that flatness can be a power. A character organised around one magnificent insistence, a linguistic habit, a repeated desire, can be more alive on the page than a character dutifully supplied with contradictions. Dickens's people, so often offered as the examples of flatness, persist in memory precisely because they are not psychologically complete. They are concentrated. Life, in Wood's sense, is not a matter of how many traits a dossier would list. It is a matter of whether the representation has an irreplaceable presence, something that would be lost if the character were smoothed into a plausible person.

The argument connects back to style. A flat character, done well, is often a feat of the sentence, a way of speaking that cannot be confused with the narrator's neutral voice. A round character, done badly, is a set of attributes the narration tells us to believe. Free indirect style complicates the binary further, because a character who is given a rich inner voice can still be, in Forster's structural sense, organised around one mistake, as Emma is organised around her certainty that she understands other people. Wood's practical upshot is a permission. Writers and readers may stop using "flat" as a dismissal and start using it as a description of concentration. They may also stop assuming that the novel's highest achievement is to imitate the full mess of a real personality. The novel's achievement, as this book describes it, is to make a consciousness audible, with enough art that we hear both the consciousness and the art.

Why it matters here

Two ideas meet in this book. One is free indirect style, the novel's way of blending the narrator's grammar with the character's mind so that irony and intimacy share a sentence. Wood's essay is the modern critic's case that this blend, perfected in English by Austen and made pervasive by Flaubert, is the form's defining technique. The other idea is flat and round character. Forster's test, that a round character can surprise us convincingly, remains useful, and Wood's reply is the complication the idea needs: flatness can be alive, concentration can beat a fake completeness, and a character's life is often a matter of style rather than of how many traits they are given. Read him for both. The demonstrations are simple, the history runs through the novels Folio already points to, and the defence of a double voice is the reason the technique, once noticed, seems to be on almost every page of modern fiction.
