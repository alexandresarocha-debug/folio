James Wood, in How Fiction Works (2008), invents a sentence to show what free indirect style can do in a handful of words: "Ted watched the orchestra through stupid tears." Take out "stupid" and you have an ordinary report, a man crying at a concert. Put it back and someone has judged the tears. Wood asks whose word it is. It would be strange for the novelist to call a listener stupid for being moved by music. The word slides over to Ted. He is embarrassed. Turned into a direct thought, Wood says, the same moment becomes "'Stupid to be crying at this silly piece of Brahms,' he thought," which is longer and loses what he calls "the complicated presence of the author." In the short sentence the word belongs partly to Ted and partly to the narrator. "We inhabit omniscience and partiality at once."

He finds the same hinge in a sentence he did not invent, the opening of Joyce's "The Dead": "Lily, the caretaker's daughter, was literally run off her feet." Nobody is literally run off her feet. "Literally" is Lily's own exaggeration, the phrase she would use about the party she is serving, dropped into the narrator's grammar with no "she thought" and no quotation marks. The reader is in the hallway with her and also a half-step outside her, hearing the cliché as a cliché. That double place is the device. Austen uses it when Emma's certainty and Austen's knowledge occupy one sentence. Flaubert uses it for long stretches of Madame Bovary, so that Emma's way of seeing and the narrator's way of seeing share a line and do not become the same thing.

For anyone writing, the practical test is the single loaded word. If you write "she glanced at the cheap ring," you have to know whether "cheap" is your verdict or hers. If it is hers, the reader can love her and still see the snobbery, or ache for her and still see the shame, without a narrator stepping in to explain. If you never let a judgment belong to the character, the prose stays outside, pointing. If every judgment is only the character's, you lose the gap in which irony lives. Free indirect style is that gap, kept small on purpose. Wood's warning is that the gap is easy to miss and easy to fake. A word like "stupid" or "literally" has to be one the character could actually think. Drop in the author's clever adjective and the alchemy fails. The reader hears the writer, not Ted.
