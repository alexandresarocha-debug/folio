A map of how fiction presents a mind

Dorrit Cohn's Transparent Minds, published in 1978, is the book narratologists still use when they need to say precisely how a novel lets us know what a character is thinking. The title names the privilege. In life, other minds are opaque, inferred from what people say and do. In fiction, minds can be made transparent, and the techniques of transparency are not one technique. Cohn's argument is a classification with consequences. The way a thought is presented changes who is judging it, how close the reader stands, and whether irony is available. She is especially concerned to separate three third-person methods that ordinary talk collapses into "the writer went into the character's head", and then to describe the related choices open to a first-person narrator. The classification is the contribution. Once it is in hand, a page of Austen, of Flaubert, or of a modernist monologue can be described without the vague compliment that it is psychological.

Her name for the method most readers now call free indirect style is narrated monologue. She prefers the name because it says what the sentence is doing. A monologue, a stretch of the character's own mental language, is being narrated, carried by the narrator's person and tense. The result is easy to miss if you only have two categories, telling and showing, or thought report and quotation. Narrated monologue sits between them and does work that neither neighbour can do. Cohn's book is the patient demonstration of that in-between, with enough contrast against the neighbours that the in-between becomes visible as a choice rather than as a vague atmosphere of inwardness.

Three ways a third-person sentence can know

The first way is psycho-narration. The narrator reports the mind in the narrator's own language. A fear, a motive, or a half-formed reluctance is named, summarised, or analysed by a voice that knows more, or at least speaks more abstractly, than the character does. Psycho-narration can cover a long period in a clause, and it can say what the character could not say, including a self-deception the character is busy maintaining. Its power is intellectual. Its risk is distance. A mind continually translated into the narrator's concepts can feel managed, and the reader may start to trust the narrator's diagnosis more than the experience. Cohn treats this not as a fault but as a capacity, the capacity of fiction to understand a person better than the person understands herself, and to do it in the open.

The second way is quoted monologue. The thought appears in the character's first person and in the present tense of thinking, often with a tag, and in principle with the marks of quotation. At full stretch, with the tags dropped and the narration apparently gone, this becomes the autonomous monologue of high modernism, a mind alone on the page. Quoted monologue offers the strongest illusion of unmediated access. It also breaks the narrative tense and the narrative person. The book has to step out of its ordinary grammar, or else mark the step so clearly that we hear a quotation happening inside the story. The gain in immediacy is paid for by a change of gear. Cohn wants that gear-change noticed, because a criticism that calls every inward moment a stream of consciousness has lost the ability to say what is special about the moments that really abandon the narrator.

The third way, narrated monologue, keeps the narrator's third person and past tense and takes on the character's idiom, questions, exclamations, and orientation in time. Words like now, here, and tomorrow can belong to the character's moment even though the sentence says she and was. There is no "she thought" and no quotation mark. Consider, as a demonstration rather than as a line from a novel, the difference between a report, she believed the visit was unnecessary, and a narrated monologue, why go at all, when the visit would be useless. The second sentence asks the character's question in the narrator's grammar. Sympathy is built in, because we are hearing the mind's own cadence. Irony is built in too, because the narrator has not surrendered the sentence. A single clause can be warmer than a report and more suspicious than a quotation. That double temperature is why Cohn, like James Wood after her, treats the device as the novel's particular privilege, and why she spends her care on the grammatical tests that keep it from being confused with a lightly coloured narration on one side or a disguised quotation on the other.

First person, and the distance inside one life

Cohn refuses to treat first-person fiction as a single alternative called "I". A narrator who says "I" still chooses a distance from the self who lived the events. In what she calls dissonant self-narration, the telling self judges the experiencing self from later knowledge, with irony, regret, or moral superiority. The gap between them is the subject. In consonant self-narration, the telling stays close to the experience, withholding the later verdict, so that the reader inhabits the earlier confusion with very little help from hindsight. The distinction matters because unreliability and intimacy are often discussed as if first person produced them automatically. It does not. A first-person book can be a tribunal the narrator holds for a younger self, or it can be a refusal to hold the tribunal. Those are technical choices, as definite as the choice between psycho-narration and narrated monologue, and they change what the reader is entitled to feel sure about.

At the far edge of quoted inner speech she places the monologue that has become independent of any narrating frame, the mind presented as if we were overhearing thought itself with no one left to introduce it. The famous modern instances sit here, and Cohn's point in isolating them is to stop them from swallowing the rest of the map. Most of what readers call inwardness in the nineteenth-century novel is not a monologue of that kind. It is narrated monologue or psycho-narration, devices that keep a narrator in the grammar even while they give the character the words. Calling all of it stream of consciousness is a historical blur. Cohn's map restores the history. The modern monologue is a late, extreme choice. The double-voiced sentence is an older and more widespread one, and it has its own rules.

Why the classification is a way of reading

The ethical force of the book is easy to underestimate if one comes to it looking only for terms. To choose psycho-narration is to claim the right to name another person's state. To choose quoted monologue is to pretend, for a stretch, that the naming can stop and the thought can simply occur. To choose narrated monologue is to do both in a way that can tip, inside a line, from alliance to exposure. Novels that move among the three, as most ambitious novels do, are moving among stances towards their people. A critic who can say which stance a paragraph is in can say what the paragraph is doing to the reader, instead of saying only that the writing is close or distant. Cohn's terms have lasted because they are fine enough to be used on a passage and sturdy enough to be taught. They do not decide whether a given use is good. They decide what the use is, which is the prior question, and the one vague praise of psychological depth continually skips.

She is also a warning against the idea that transparency is a single effect that fiction either achieves or fails. A mind can be over-explained and therefore less present. A mind can be quoted at length and become a performance that hides the very muddle quotation was meant to catch. A narrated monologue can be so continuous that the reader forgets there was ever a narrator to disagree with, which is one way of describing the risk Flaubert ran and the risk his prosecutors stumbled on. Transparency is a set of bargains. Cohn's book itemises the bargains so that a reader can see which one a sentence has struck. That itemising is the scholarly version of a writer's question, asked on every page and usually answered by instinct. Whose language is this, and who is being asked to trust it?

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading calls free indirect style the novel's great invention, the blend of narrator and character without quotation marks or a tag such as she thought. Cohn's book is the classic survey that gives the invention a precise name, narrated monologue, and a place between two neighbours: psycho-narration, in which the narrator reports a mind in the narrator's language, and quoted monologue, in which the thought appears as the character's own words. The survey is what keeps the idea from becoming a mood. It shows the grammar, the third person and the past tense carrying a character's idiom, and it shows the sister choices in first person, where the narrator can stand far from the earlier self or stay consonant with it. James Wood's later essay is a critic's celebration of the same middle path. Cohn is the map. Read her when you want to know which kind of transparency a sentence is using, and what kind of irony that choice has already built in.
