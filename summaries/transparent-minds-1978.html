In A Portrait of the Artist as a Young Man, Stephen Dedalus waits to confess. The prose says: "God could see that he was sorry. He would tell all his sins." Dorrit Cohn, in Transparent Minds (1978), stops on those lines because they are not labeled as thought. The novel does not say that Stephen said to himself, "God can see that I am sorry. I will tell all my sins." The grammar stays in the narrator's third person and past tense, while the fear, the bargaining, and the repetition belong to the boy. Cohn's name for this is narrated monologue. Translate it back into first person and it sounds like a quotation. Leave it, and the words sit on the threshold of being spoken at all. A few lines later the narrator is reporting posture again, the clasped hands and the crucifix, with no seam where the mind ended and the body resumed.

She sets that mode beside two others, and the difference is easier to hear than to define. Psycho-narration is the narrator telling us what a mind is doing, in the narrator's own words, often with a reach the character does not have. Quoted monologue is the thought given directly, the old "she thought," with the person's own sentences inside the quotes. Narrated monologue borrows the person's words and keeps the narrator's tense. Cohn hears Austen doing it at a crisis in Emma: "How could she have been so deceived!" The exclamation is Emma's, the third-person story is not. The same chapter can move among all three. What changes is how much of the sentence the character seems to own.

The distinction matters as soon as you write a scene in which someone is wrong. If you report the mistake from outside, the reader is safe and a little bored. If you quote the whole panic, the page fills with italics and the world disappears. Narrated monologue lets the mistake sound like a fact for as long as the character believes it, then lets a plainer sentence take the room back. Readers do this switch constantly and rarely name it. Writers run into trouble when they do not notice which mode they are in, so that a narrator who should know better suddenly shares a character's illusion, or a character's voice is cleaned up until nobody on the page could have thought it. Cohn's terms are a way to hear that slip. They are not a ranking. Joyce needs all three, and so does a letter in which you tell a friend what you thought, what you said to yourself, and what you now see you were telling yourself.
