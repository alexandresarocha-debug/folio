Philip Dawid, a professor of statistics at University College London, worked on the Sally Clark case. In this lecture on Bayes's theorem and evidence in court, written in 2001, he uses the case to show how two respectable-sounding ways of reasoning can lead to opposite answers.

He describes the trial evidence: a professor of paediatrics testified that the probability of two cot deaths in a family like Sally Clark's was about 1 in 73 million, by squaring a carefully estimated figure of about one in 8,500 for a single death. Dawid calls the squaring extremely dubious, because it assumes the deaths are independent, but accepts it for the sake of argument.

First he shows a tempting rule: if two babies in a family both die of unexplained causes, decide that the mother murdered them. If she is innocent, the rule is wrong only if both died of cot death, which on the prosecution's figure happens 1 in 73 million times. The rule looks astonishingly accurate. But it ignores how rare the alternative is.

So he estimates that too. Office for National Statistics data for 1997 showed seven babies murdered in their first year out of 642,093 live births, about 1.1 in 100,000. Squaring that in the same dubious way gives about 1 in 8.4 billion for two murders. Comparing the two rare explanations, the odds come out at about 0.009, or over 100 to 1 in favour of cot death rather than murder, before any medical evidence is considered.

His point is not that these numbers are right. It is that a tiny probability of the evidence under innocence means nothing until it is set against its probability under guilt. Only a Bayesian comparison, he argues, takes proper account of all the evidence.

What this means in real life: when someone says an innocent explanation is very unlikely, ask how likely the guilty explanation is.

Caution: the numbers are deliberately crude illustrations, and real cases involve medical and other evidence that changes the comparison. The published version appeared in 2002; this page follows the 2001 preprint.
