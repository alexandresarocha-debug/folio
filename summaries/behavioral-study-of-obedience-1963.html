Background

In the early 1960s, at Yale, Stanley Milgram began a series of experiments on what he called obedience. The first full report, Behavioral study of obedience, appeared in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology in 1963. It is a short paper with a single, carefully staged condition, and it is the source of the figure everyone remembers. Ordinary men from New Haven, recruited to take part in a study of learning, were told to give electric shocks of increasing strength to another man whenever he made a mistake. The other man was a confederate. He was not being shocked. In the version published that year, twenty-six of the forty participants continued to the maximum on the board, 450 volts. Milgram connected the result to the Holocaust and to the trial of Adolf Eichmann. His claim was that ordinary people will harm others when an authority tells them the harm is required.

Folio's card on the obedience studies treats this paper as the origin and warns against treating it as the whole story. Later variations, the 1974 book, Burger's partial replication, and archival work on what the experimenter actually said, all change the picture. The walkthrough here stays with the 1963 study, so the famous result is clear before the complications are added.

The question

Milgram wanted to know how far a person would go in hurting a stranger when the instruction came from a legitimate authority and the setting was a scientific laboratory. He was not asking whether people enjoy cruelty. The design tries to separate obedience from sadism by making the shocks a requirement of the procedure, imposed on a participant who has come to help with research, and by recording the conflict in people who continue while visibly distressed. If they continue and suffer, the result looks like obedience against one's own feeling. If they continue and are calm, other explanations get stronger.

He also wanted a comparison with what experts expected. Before the study, he described the procedure to psychiatrists, students and colleagues and asked them to predict how many people would go to the end. The predictions clustered at the bottom. Only a pathological fringe, the judges thought, would deliver the maximum shock. The experiment is built to test that confidence. A result in which a majority go to the end is a result about the judges as well as about the participants.

How the study was set up

Forty men, aged between twenty and fifty and drawn from a range of occupations, were recruited through a newspaper advertisement and direct invitation in the New Haven area. They were paid a modest fee for coming, and they were told the fee was theirs whatever happened. At the laboratory they met the experimenter, a man in a lab coat, and another supposed volunteer, a mild middle-aged man who was in fact trained for the part. A rigged draw assigned the real participant to be the teacher and the confederate to be the learner. The cover story was a study of memory and punishment. The teacher watched the learner being strapped into a chair and heard that the shocks could be painful, though they would cause no lasting tissue damage.

The teacher read word pairs and, on each mistake, was told to deliver the next shock in the series. The learner's responses were a script. He did well at first, then protested, demanded to be released, cried out, and eventually stopped answering. The teacher was in a separate room, able to hear him and unable to see him. When the teacher hesitated, the experimenter used a fixed series of prods: a request to continue, a statement that the experiment required him to continue, a statement that it was essential he continue, and a final insistence that he had no choice and must go on. The prods were to be used in order, and only as needed. The session ended when the teacher refused to go on after the final prod, or when he had delivered the maximum three times. Afterwards the deception was explained.

What he found

Every participant went a long way up the board. The disagreements were about the top, not about whether a stranger could be shocked at all. Fourteen men, at various points in the upper range, refused to continue and were classified as defiant. Twenty-six went through to 450 volts and were classified as obedient. That is 65 per cent, the figure the card quotes, and it is the figure from this one condition, not an average across Milgram's later variations. The men who obeyed were not calm technicians. Milgram describes sweating, trembling, stuttering, nervous laughter, and participants who protested in one sentence and threw the next switch in the next. The conflict is part of the finding. They knew something was wrong by the standard of their own distress, and they continued because the experimenter told them the procedure required it.

The predictions were badly wrong, and Milgram uses the gap as evidence that the power of the situation is obscure from the outside. People who hear the procedure imagine they would stop, and imagine that almost everyone else would stop. The participants themselves, once inside the sequence, found stopping difficult. Each shock is only one step above the last, the learner's fate seems to be in the experimenter's hands, and the teacher has already agreed to play the part. Leaving means breaking a commitment, contradicting a scientist, and defining one's own earlier shocks as wrong. Staying means one more switch. The paper's claim is that this structure, not a hidden population of cruel men, produced the 65 per cent.

How Milgram read it

Milgram read the result as a demonstration of obedience to legitimate authority. The participants had come under their own steam, they were free to leave in the formal sense that nobody chained them down, and they inflicted what they believed to be serious pain on a protesting man. Most of them did it when a man with no power except his role said that the study required it. Milgram argued that the authority of science, the incremental nature of the task, and the absorption of responsibility by the experimenter were enough to get ordinary people to act against their own values. He offered the finding as a way to think about atrocities in which officials later say they were following orders. He did not claim that a laboratory hour is a death camp.

He was also careful, even in this short paper, about what he had not shown. This was one setting, at a famous university, with men only, and with the learner out of sight. The experimenter was physically present and unchallenged. Milgram already suspected, and later showed, that changing those features changed the rate. The 1963 paper is the baseline, written so that the baseline would be hard to forget. The theoretical vocabulary he would later use, the agentic state in particular, is only sketched here. The paper's own conclusion is empirical and moral at once. People who are sure they would defy an unjust order should look at how these forty men, who would have said the same, actually behaved.

Limits

The limits of this particular paper are the limits of a single dramatic condition. There is no comparison group inside the article. A reader cannot tell, from these pages alone, whether the rate would fall if the experimenter left the room, if the teacher had to force the learner's hand onto a plate, or if other teachers refused. Those comparisons exist in the programme, and they are the reason the card says the book is more nuanced than the textbook summary. Using the 1963 figure as "what people do" is a misuse of a paper that was designed as the start of a series.

There are also limits that Milgram could not see as clearly as later historians. Gina Perry's archival work, which the card flags, found that the experimenter sometimes went beyond the scripted prods, and that a substantial number of participants doubted the shocks were real. Doubt does not make the behaviour trivial. People who half-suspect a trick can still be frightened and still comply. It does weaken any claim that all twenty-six believers in the cover story chose, clear-eyed, to torture a man. The debriefing and the emotional aftercare have also been argued over. For a walkthrough of the 1963 paper, the honest statement is that the procedure as published already imposed serious stress on people who had not agreed to be distressed, and that the result is about the pressure they were under, not about a clean window onto evil.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Milgram's obedience studies: what they do and don't show, begins with this experiment and with the 65 per cent. The figure is real, and it comes from the condition just described: learner audible and unseen, experimenter present, prods in a series, forty men at Yale. What the paper shows is that in that condition a majority went to the end of the board, against their own visible distress, and that observers had expected nothing of the kind. What it does not show, because it did not try, is that orders of any kind will produce that rate, or that the rate is a constant of human nature.

The rest of the card is the reason not to stop on this page. The 1974 book reports the variations: obedience fell when the authority was distant, when the victim was close, and when peers refused. Burger's 2009 partial replication, stopping at 150 volts for ethical reasons, found a similar willingness to continue at the comparable point. Haslam and Reicher's reading, which the card prefers as a live alternative, says the men were engaged in a scientific cause, and notes that the prod which sounded most like a naked order was the one they typically rejected. Perry's archive complicates the script and the belief. Read the 1963 paper for the procedure and the famous condition. Read it with those companions, or the percentage becomes a myth.
