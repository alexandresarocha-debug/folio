In the summer of 1961, at Yale, Stanley Milgram paid forty men from New Haven and nearby towns $4.50 to come to a study of memory. The ad and the letter said the money was theirs for showing up. The men were between twenty and fifty. The sample included postal clerks, high school teachers, salesmen, engineers, and laborers, and schooling that ran from elementary school to professional degrees. Each man met an experimenter in a lab coat and a mild, middle-aged “learner,” who was in fact working with the experimenter. A rigged draw made the real participant the teacher. The teacher read word pairs and, for each mistake, was told to throw the next switch on a generator whose thirty levers ran from 15 volts to 450 volts, in steps of 15. The top of the board was marked past “Danger: Severe Shock.” The learner’s protests were a script. When the teacher hesitated, the experimenter answered from a short list of prods, the last of which was that the teacher had no choice and must go on.

Twenty-six of the forty men went to the last switch, 450 volts. Fourteen refused at some point after the learner protested and stopped answering. The earliest of those refusals were at 300 volts: five men stopped there. The men who continued were often in bad shape while they did it. Milgram describes sweating, trembling, stuttering, and nervous laughter that in some men turned into uncontrollable seizures. Before the study, fourteen Yale seniors, all psychology majors, were asked how one hundred ordinary men would behave. Every one of them predicted that only a small minority would go to the end. Their estimates ran from 0 to 3 percent.

The result is a warning about a particular kind of pressure. A legitimate-looking person is in the room. The task is described as science. Each new shock is only one step above the last, so the hard moment never arrives as a single decision to do something monstrous. It arrives as “one more.” People deciding whether to carry out a harmful order at work, in a ward, or in a unit can use that structure against itself: decide the stopping point before the first small step, and put the refusal where other people can see it.

The 65 percent figure is this one condition, at Yale, with the learner out of sight. In the 1974 book Milgram reported that the share who went to 450 volts fell to 40 percent when the learner sat in the same room, and to 30 percent when the teacher had to force the learner’s hand onto a shock plate. Jerry Burger’s 2009 partial rerun, which stopped the session at 150 volts, found that 28 of 40 people continued past the learner’s first demand to be released. In Milgram’s comparable condition, 33 of 40 had continued past that same point. Burger stopped the people who went on, so the rerun does not show whether they would have reached 450 volts.
