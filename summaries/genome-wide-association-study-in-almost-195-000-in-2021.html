If one DNA letter does most of the work, is eye colour a one-gene trait? Mark Simcoe, Pirro Hysi and a large international team set out to find everything else.

They studied 157,485 people of European descent from the genetic testing company 23andMe, who sorted their own eye colour into seven shades from blue to dark brown. They checked the results in another 35,501 Europeans. In total that was 192,986 people from 10 populations, the largest eye colour study to date.

They found 124 separate genetic signals in 61 regions of DNA. Fifty of those regions were new. Earlier studies had found signals in and around about 10 genes. Many of the new genes are involved in making pigment. Others are genes earlier linked to the structure of the iris, such as its furrows and small pits called crypts. The authors suggest structure may also affect eye colour. In 1,636 people from two Asian populations, the genetics looked similar, with smaller effects.

How much do the common DNA differences explain? In a British twin group, they explained almost all of the blue-versus-brown split, but only 38.5% of who has intermediate colours like green and hazel. Across all shades on the full scale, they explained 53.2%.

That is less than twin studies suggest, which put eye colour heritability near 0.9. The gap is a small version of the missing heritability puzzle seen in many traits. The authors think genes still unknown make up part of the gap.

What this means in real life: a DNA test can already tell blue from brown quite well. Forensic scientists use one based on six DNA letters. Green and hazel are much harder to predict, and these new genes may help.

Caution: eye colour was self-reported, and people describe shades differently. Almost all participants were of European descent. Genetic signals point to regions, not proven causes. The authors call for lab studies to show what each region actually does. The idea that blue eyes is a simple recessive trait is clearly out of date, but the full picture is still being filled in.
