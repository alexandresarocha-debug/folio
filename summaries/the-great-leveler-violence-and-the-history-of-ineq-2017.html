The challenge he sets

Walter Scheidel’s The Great Leveler, published in 2017, asks a question that sounds like a provocation and is meant as a research programme. Across the long run of agrarian and commercial societies, does economic inequality ever fall by peaceful means, on a scale that a historian can take seriously? His answer is bleak, and he states it early so the rest of the book can be a test rather than a mood. Inequality, he argues, tends to rise whenever a complex society is stable enough for elites to accumulate land, capital, offices, and the right to pass them on. The great reductions have come from violence and catastrophe. He sorts those reductions under four heads, the Four Horsemen of levelling: mass-mobilisation warfare, transformative revolution, state collapse, and lethal pandemic. The horsemen are not a metaphor he drops after the introduction. They are the table of contents. War, revolution, collapse, and plague each get a part. A further part asks whether reform, recession, democracy, development, or education has ever done similar work. A last part asks what that record implies once the horsemen have, for the moment, dismounted.

Scheidel is a historian of Rome who has spent years on ancient demography and on the scale of premodern economies, and the book uses that training. It is not a history of the twentieth century that gestures at the past. It starts from the appearance of heritable surplus, runs through ancient empires, and only then arrives at 1914, which he treats as the clearest modern case of a general pattern rather than as the pattern’s invention. He is explicit about the moral he does not want drawn. The finding that disaster has been the reliable leveller is not a recommendation of disaster. It is a constraint on anyone who hopes to compress inequality by methods that leave accumulation intact. If modern societies manage a large, peaceful compression, they will be doing something his evidence says is historically unusual.

A brief history, and the ceiling on inequality

The opening part is a history of inequality’s rise, written to deprive the reader of the idea that equality is the normal condition and inequality a recent sin. Scheidel begins with the limited hierarchies of foraging societies and with the way farming, herding, and stored grain made surplus both possible and inheritable. Once surplus can be held and transmitted, small advantages compound. He then surveys empires, in which land, slaves, tax farms, and official rank gave elites extraction rights on a new scale, and he looks at the ups and downs that are real but, in his reading, modest next to what the horsemen later achieve. The useful conceptual tool, developed with the work of Branko Milanovic and others, is the inequality possibility frontier. In a poor society, inequality cannot rise without limit, because if the rich took much more, the poor would die and there would be no one left to extract from. Measured inequality can therefore look moderate in a very poor place simply because the surplus is small. What Scheidel wants the reader to notice is how often premodern societies pushed towards that frontier. They were about as unequal as their poverty allowed.

This framing matters for everything that follows. A fall in a Gini coefficient, or in the share of wealth held at the top, is interesting only if one knows whether the society was bumping against its ceiling. And a peaceful reform that trims an elite in a rich modern economy is not automatically the same kind of event as a plague that changes the price of labour for a century. Scheidel’s later scepticism about land reform and democratic transitions is partly a scepticism about scale. He is looking for compressions that show up in the structure of wealth, persist for more than a political season, and are not reversed as soon as stability returns.

War and revolution

The war chapters distinguish kinds of war that do not level from the kind that does. Preindustrial warfare, including many civil wars, often redistributed plunder among elites or ruined a region without producing a new social settlement. Mass-mobilisation warfare in the twentieth century was different because it reached so far down and so far up at once. It conscripted labour, taxed income at rates agrarian states could not have administered, destroyed physical capital, inflated away debts and rentier fortunes, and left behind labour movements and welfare institutions with the political strength of societies that had put millions in uniform. The great compression of mid-century incomes, across industrial countries that fought on opposite sides and across some that did not, is his showcase. He does not treat it as the ordinary maturation of capitalism. He treats it as the shadow of the wars, prolonged by policies the wars had made thinkable, and reversible once the mobilisation society receded.

Revolution, the next horseman, is narrower than the word’s everyday use. Scheidel’s transformative cases are the communist revolutions, above all in Russia and China, which levelled by expropriation, the destruction of landlord and bourgeois property, and terror. The levelling was real. So was the human cost, which he refuses to launder. He also looks at revolutions before Lenin and finds them less reliable: a change of regime that leaves property relations standing does not, on his evidence, produce a lasting equalisation. The pairing with the war chapters is deliberate. Both horsemen work by breaking the property and inheritance rules that stability protects. Both have, in the cases where they most clearly levelled, been associated with states that then became powerful enough to hold the new distribution in place for a time. Levelling and liberty are not the same outcome. A reader looking for a defence of revolutionary equality as a moral project will not find it. A reader looking for a description of what happened to fortunes will.

Collapse and plague

State collapse is the horseman most native to Scheidel’s home field. When an imperial structure fails, as in the west of the Roman Empire, elite incomes that depended on imperial office, long-distance rents, slavery, and monetised tax lose their machinery. Villas shrink, literacy and monumental building recede, and the gap between the powerful and everyone else narrows partly because the powerful have less to be powerful with. He assembles comparable cases elsewhere, and he is careful about the ambiguity that makes collapse a difficult horseman to cheer for. The loss of an elite fortune can be a loss of coordination, security, and trade at the same time. Material simplicity is not the same thing as a good life. It can still be a compression of the distribution. Scheidel’s argument is that the compression is consistently a consequence of the breakdown, not a reform the breakdown happened to accompany.

Plague is the horseman with the clearest market mechanism. The Black Death, commonly estimated to have killed something like a third or more of Europe’s population in its first passage, made labour scarce and land relatively abundant. Real wages for the survivors rose and, in places, stayed high for generations, while the value of purely owning land came under pressure from tenants who could leave. Scheidel reviews the wage evidence and the long fade of that advantage as populations recovered and elites rebuilt their bargaining power. He then asks, in a wider chapter, when pandemics level and when they do not. A mortality crisis that kills without changing the ratio of people to resources, or that elites can shift onto the poor, is not automatically a leveller. The fourteenth-century pandemic levelled because of its scale and because of the agrarian economy it struck. Later epidemics with different age patterns and different institutions did not repeat the effect on command. Famine, which he treats alongside pandemic, is an even less reliable friend of the poor: it destroys the people with the thinnest reserves first.

Peaceful alternatives, and the future

The part on alternatives is the book’s answer to the obvious objection. Land reform, financial crisis, democracy, mass education, and economic development have all been asked to do the work of the horsemen, and Scheidel’s survey finds their effects usually modest, local, or temporary, unless they were riding on a violent shock already underway. He does not claim that the postwar welfare state was imaginary. He claims that the conditions under which it compressed inequality were not the normal conditions of peacetime capitalism, and that the compression has been partly undone where those conditions lapsed. Development can raise everyone’s income while widening gaps. Education can spread skills and still be outrun by the returns to capital and to the very top of the wage distribution. The counterfactual chapter is there to stop the reader from treating the absence of levelling as a mere failure of political will in the historical record. If the record is long and the exceptions are small, will is not a sufficient theory.

The closing part turns the argument towards the present. The violence that produced the twentieth century’s compression has receded, which Scheidel regards as a gain that should not be bargained away. The consequence, if his horsemen are the only reliable mechanism, is that large-scale levelling is unlikely to return on its own. He is not predicting a single path for the next generation of policy. He is saying that policy would have to accomplish, without mass death or the destruction of capital, something that in the past was accomplished by mass death and the destruction of capital. That is a high bar, and he thinks it should be described honestly rather than talked down.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on the Great Leveler is a compression of this architecture: the four horsemen, the Black Death’s wage effects, the wars of the twentieth century, communist expropriation, the fall of states, and the explicit refusal to treat the finding as advice. The card pairs Scheidel with Thomas Piketty’s Capital in the Twenty-First Century, and the pairing is exact where both of them look at 1914 to 1945. Piketty’s r greater than g is a theory of why inequality returns in peacetime.
