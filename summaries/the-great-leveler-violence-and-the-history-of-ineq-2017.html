On the eve of the First World War, Walter Scheidel writes, the richest tenth of households in England held 92 percent of all private wealth. By the time of his book, that share was a little more than half. He treats the drop as one case of a rule he has followed from the ancient world to the twentieth century. Long stretches of peace and stable government do not flatten inequality. They let it rebuild. The compressions that actually show up in the record follow disasters. He groups the disasters as four: mass-mobilization war, revolution, the collapse of states, and lethal pandemics.

The Black Death is his clearest premodern case, because the dying changed the price of labor without burning down the farms. Plague killed on the order of a third of the people in Europe and more in England. Afterward, in western Europe, real wages doubled or tripled. Laborers, he writes, dined on meat and beer, while landlords struggled to keep up appearances. Land was worth less because fewer people needed it, and the survivors could demand more for their work. The English Statute of Labourers tried to freeze wages and failed. The leveling lasted a few generations. As population grew back, wages fell, and over a couple of centuries inequality returned toward where it had been before 1348. The same logic, with better numbers, applies to the two world wars. Mass armies, very high taxes, inflation, and the physical destruction of capital cut the share of income and wealth held at the top. Scheidel calls that twentieth-century fall the Great Compression, and he calls it the model case because the evidence is dense enough to see the mechanism.

The uncomfortable implication is about policy that is not a catastrophe. Scheidel looks at land reform, democracy, education, and ordinary economic growth, and he does not find a peaceful tool that has compressed inequality by anything like the same amount. A tax, a union, or a land law can matter. On his reading, the ones that mattered on a historical scale arrived with war, revolution, state failure, or plague, and their effect faded once the shock faded. Anyone hoping that a normal election or a normal boom will, by itself, reproduce the mid-twentieth-century fall in the top wealth share is betting against the pattern he assembles.

The pattern depends on how the peaceful cases are classified. Thomas Piketty's series, which document the same European collapse in top wealth shares from 1910 to the 1950s and the rise since, treat progressive taxation and the welfare state as policies societies can keep in peacetime, not only as side effects of the fighting. If those policies did independent work after the guns stopped, Scheidel's "only violence levels" claim is too exclusive, even if the wars were what made the policies politically possible. He is explicit that the shocks fade. He is less persuasive if everything that followed them is counted as part of the violence.
