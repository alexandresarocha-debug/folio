Donald Redelmeier and Daniel Kahneman recorded pain minute by minute during real procedures at Wellesley Hospital in Toronto. One hundred fifty-four patients underwent colonoscopy. One hundred thirty-three underwent lithotripsy, the shock-wave treatment for kidney stones. Every 60 seconds a computer asked the patient to rate current pain on a handheld scale. For 53 of the colonoscopy patients an observer made those ratings instead, as a check that the act of rating was not itself changing the experience. Colonoscopies lasted from 4 to 67 minutes, about 23 minutes on average. Peak pain during colonoscopy averaged 7.7 on a 10-point scale, and 38% of those patients hit 10 at least once. Pain in the final three minutes of colonoscopy averaged 2.0. Lithotripsy ended harder than it began: pain in the last three minutes averaged 4.4, against 1.6 at the start.

In the recovery room, patients rated the pain of the procedure as a whole. For colonoscopy, that rating correlated 0.62 with the single worst moment and 0.50 with the pain at the end. The length of the procedure had no significant correlation with how bad patients remembered it to be. A longer colonoscopy was not remembered as a worse one. Physicians showed the same compression. Their ratings of the patient's pain, and their judgments about whether more anesthetic would have helped, tracked the peak and the end more closely than they tracked the clock.

The two procedures make the same point from opposite endings. Colonoscopy tended to finish milder than it started. Lithotripsy tended to finish worse, with the last three minutes at 4.4 against 1.6 at the beginning. In both cases the memory followed the worst moment and the finish, and the clock dropped out. A patient who remembers a 20-minute test and a 50-minute test as equally bad is using that rule, and so is the physician who watched it.

A patient deciding whether to come back for a recommended repeat, and a doctor deciding whether the last case was tolerable, are both consulting this shortened memory. The sum of the minutes, which is what the person actually went through, is a different number. Easing the worst moment is the main clinical job. Easing the final minutes is a separate lever on the memory that later guides the decision. This study is the evidence that the lever is already at work in ordinary procedures, before anyone has tried to pull it on purpose.
