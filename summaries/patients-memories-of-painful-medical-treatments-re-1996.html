From the laboratory basin to the procedure room

Patients' memories of painful medical treatments, by Donald Redelmeier and Daniel Kahneman, appeared in 1996 in the journal Pain. It takes the peak-end claim out of the cold-water basin and asks whether the same compression of memory shows up when the pain is a real treatment that a patient has agreed to undergo for a medical reason. The subtitle names the method: real-time and retrospective evaluations of two minimally invasive procedures. The paper's question is not whether patients dislike pain. It is whether the memory they carry away is a fair summary of the pain they reported while the procedure was going on, or whether that memory is edited in the particular way the laboratory result predicts.

The two procedures are colonoscopy and lithotripsy, the shock-wave treatment used to break up kidney stones. Both are unpleasant, both vary in length from patient to patient, and both have a shape: intensity rises and falls rather than sitting at one level. That variation is what the study needs. If every procedure were the same length and the same intensity, peak, end, and duration would be the same fact. Because patients differ, the investigators can ask which feature of a patient's own real-time record predicts that patient's later summary. The laboratory experiment manufactured the shape of the episode. This study measures shapes that clinical practice already produces.

Two records of the same procedure

The design produces two descriptions of one experience. During the procedure, patients give ongoing ratings of pain. Those ratings are the experiencing record. They can be inspected for the worst moment, for the level at the end, for the length of the procedure, and for a rough total, the accumulation of the ratings over time. After the procedure, patients evaluate it retrospectively: how bad it was, remembered as a whole. That second number is the memory. The statistical question is which parts of the first record line up with the second.

An adding-up view makes a clear prediction. The patient who reported more pain, for longer, should remember a worse procedure. Duration should matter, and the sum of the momentary ratings should matter, because both are ways of saying how much suffering occurred. A peak-end view makes a different prediction. The retrospective rating should track the most intense moment and the intensity at the end, and once those are taken into account, duration should add little. The procedure that ran longer need not be the procedure that is remembered as worse, if its peak was similar and its ending was gentler. The patient is not being asked which theory is true. The patient supplies both sets of numbers, and the theories are tested by which numbers agree.

What predicted the memory

The memories behaved like the cold-water memories. For these patients, the retrospective evaluation of pain was predicted by the peak intensity and by the intensity at the end, rather than by how long the procedure lasted. A long procedure was not remembered as worse merely for being long. A procedure that finished badly was remembered as worse even when the total time was not exceptional. The real-time record shows that duration was a real feature of what happened. The retrospective record shows that duration was a weak feature of what was kept. The editing is specific. It is not that patients forgot the procedure, or that they were unable to say that one session had gone on longer than another. It is that the summary they used, when they judged the episode as a whole, gave its weight to the worst moment and the last moment.

The same pattern appeared across two procedures that feel different to a patient and that last different amounts of time. That matters for the argument. A finding confined to colonoscopy might have been a quirk of that instrument, of bowel preparation, or of the way a particular clinic ended the examination. Lithotripsy is another kind of pain, another kind of machinery, another typical duration. The peak-end pattern in both suggests that the compression is a property of how aversive episodes are remembered, not a property of one treatment. Redelmeier and Kahneman are physicians and psychologists writing together, and the paper keeps both loyalties. It is a measurement paper about memory, and it is a paper about what clinicians are hearing when a patient later says the procedure was awful.

Memory as the thing that guides the next decision

The practical sting is that patients do not choose the next procedure by replaying the real-time tape. They choose, if they choose at all, by consulting the memory. A person deciding whether to return for a recommended repeat, or telling a relative what the test was like, is speaking from the retrospective evaluation. If that evaluation neglects duration and listens to the peak and the end, then two patients with the same total burden can leave with different intentions, and two patients with different totals can leave with the same intention. The 1996 study is observational on this point. It does not assign patients to different endings. It shows that, in the endings clinical practice already happens to produce, memory follows peak and end. The implication is a hypothesis for a trial: changing the ending, even by adding a short interval of milder discomfort, could improve the memory without improving the sum of pain, and the improved memory could change willingness to come back.

That hypothesis is easy to misunderstand, and the paper's logic is worth keeping straight. The claim is not that the end is all a humane clinician should care about. The experiencing patient still lives through every minute, and those minutes count morally whether or not they count in the memory. The claim is that the minute that counts in the memory has a disproportionate effect on later behaviour. A clinic that manages only the average, or only the clock, can still send people out with a memory dominated by a bad finish. A clinic that manages the finish can change the memory while the total suffering moves in a different direction. The 1993 experiment had already made that dissociation in a basin of water. This paper reports it in people who were not volunteering for psychology, but arriving for treatment.

Limits of a study that watches rather than intervenes

Because the study does not assign the shape of the procedure, it cannot by itself prove that a better end causes a better memory. Peak and end are predictors. Patients whose procedures finished at a lower intensity remembered less pain. Something else about those patients, or about the ease of the anatomy that produced a gentler finish, might also have made them more tolerant afterwards. A randomised comparison is the way to close that gap, and it is what the later colonoscopy trial attempts. The 1996 paper is the observational bridge: real procedures, real-time ratings, memories that fail to track duration.

The paper also inherits the limits of the peak-end rule itself. Not every kind of experience is remembered this way, and a medical procedure is a particular kind of episode, bounded in time, with a clear finish and a salient worst moment. Chronic pain, which has no natural end, is a different problem. So is a procedure whose beginning, rather than its end, carries the information the patient later needs. Redelmeier and Kahneman do not claim to have written a general psychology of suffering. They claim that for these two treatments the memory patients formed was the memory peak-end predicted. That is a narrower result than a slogan about endings, and it is the result the evidence supports.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the peak-end rule cites this study for a single, precise step in the argument. The laboratory preference for more pain is not only a preference about cold water. Patients' memories of colonoscopy and of lithotripsy were predicted by peak and end intensity rather than by duration. The card then moves on to the randomised trial, in which an ending is deliberately added. This 1996 paper is the middle term. Without it, the trial would be a clever application of a student experiment. With it, the trial is a test of a pattern already observed in the procedures being modified. Read the 1993 paper for the dissociation between total pain and choice. Read this one for the same dissociation in the memories of patients. Read the 2003 trial for what happens when the ending is changed on purpose.
