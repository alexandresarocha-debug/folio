Timur Kuran and Cass Sunstein named a pattern they called an availability cascade. Someone states a danger in a vivid way. Other people repeat it, partly because the story is now easy to picture and partly because disagreeing has started to look careless. Each repetition makes the danger feel more plausible to the next listener. The belief spreads because it is available, not because a new measurement arrived.

Their second case is the scare over Alar, a chemical sprayed on apples, sold as daminozide. On February 26, 1989, CBS's 60 Minutes ran a segment, "'A' Is for Apple," built on a Natural Resources Defense Council report about cancer risk to children. Schools pulled apples from cafeterias. Shoppers stopped buying. Uniroyal, the maker, took the chemical off the food market, and later that year the Environmental Protection Agency canceled its food uses. Kuran and Sunstein write that the EPA's own review after the broadcast put the risk far below the figure in the uproar: one cancer in 111,000 rather than one in 4,200. They call the public risk "vastly exaggerated."

They set Love Canal beside it. In 1978 residents of a neighborhood in Niagara Falls, New York, learned that homes and a school sat next to a buried chemical dump. The pictures and the illnesses that were reported became a national story, and in 1980 Congress passed the Superfund law to clean up hazardous sites. Kuran and Sunstein's claim is not that the dump was imaginary. It is that once a harm is easy to retell, the demand for action outruns the evidence, and a public official who says "smaller than you think" pays a reputational price for saying it.

The same loop runs on a smaller scale whenever a risk is discussed in public. A single graphic case, a product recall, a side effect, a video, gets repeated until the chance of harm feels like the typical case. People then want the thing banned, pulled from the shelf, or kept out of the school. Kuran and Sunstein's practical warning is for the person who has to decide after the story has already spread: ask for the rate, not the picture, and notice that agreement has become the safe thing to express. They argue for putting a written comparison of risks in front of regulators before a cascade hardens into a rule.
