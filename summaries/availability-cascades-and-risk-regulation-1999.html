Background

Availability began as an account of an individual mind. A person judges a risk by how easily examples come to mind, and news, drama and personal experience all feed that ease. Timur Kuran and Cass Sunstein, writing in the Stanford Law Review in 1999, asked what happens when that individual shortcut is placed in public. Expressed beliefs are heard by other people. What is heard becomes easier to imagine. What is easier to imagine is more likely to be expressed again. Under the right conditions the loop runs away, and a society comes to treat a modest or poorly understood risk as an emergency, or to neglect a large one that nobody is talking about.

They called the runaway loop an availability cascade. The paper is not a laboratory report. It is a theory of risk regulation, built out of cognitive psychology, the economics of information, and a set of public controversies. Folio's availability card points to it for the social half of the idea: media attention and public concern reinforcing each other.

The question

Why do some hazards seize the agenda of regulators, journalists and legislators, while others that kill more people remain background noise? Kuran and Sunstein did not think the answer was simply that the public is ignorant, or simply that industry captures the state. Their question was how a perception, once it is voiced, can generate the very availability that makes it seem more and more solid. They wanted a mechanism that could produce rapid, widely shared belief, and policy to match, without a conspiracy and without a proportionate change in the underlying evidence.

How the argument is set up

The paper distinguishes two engines, which can run together. An informational cascade occurs when people infer from others' statements, especially under uncertainty. If the people one trusts are treating a chemical, a waste site or a kind of accident as dangerous, their public concern is itself data. A person who has no private measurements may rationally adopt the going view, and by adopting it may become further evidence for the next person. Nothing in that step requires panic. It requires only that private information is weak and that other people's expressed beliefs are visible.

A reputational cascade occurs when dissent is socially expensive. People who doubt the emerging story keep quiet, because contradiction looks callous, captured, or naive. Silence is then misread as agreement. The pool of public examples becomes still more one-sided, which is an availability effect produced by the fear of looking wrong rather than by new evidence. Kuran and Sunstein add a third role, the availability entrepreneur: an official, a journalist, an activist or a firm that works to make a particular instance salient, knowing that salience will be mistaken for frequency or for proof. The entrepreneur need not be lying. A true and vivid case is often the most effective seed.

They illustrate the machinery with controversies in which a salient harm became the symbol of a much larger claimed danger, and in which regulation followed the symbol. Love Canal, the Alar scare over a chemical used on apples, and episodes of intense concern about toxic waste and certain well-publicised accidents are used as narratives of this shape. The paper's method is to retell these episodes as cascades, separating the triggering event, the spread of belief through information and reputation, and the regulatory response. It is history used as a theory's examples, not a set of controlled studies.

What the account claims

The claim is not that the public invents harm from nothing. Triggering events are often real. Children were made ill, a product was frightening, a site was contaminated, a crash was fatal. The claim is that the jump from a vivid case to a general probability, and from that probability to a large regulatory programme, is amplified by social availability. Once the case is everywhere, the harm feels common and the opposing evidence feels heartless. Policy made in that climate tends to chase the available risk. It can spend heavily to reduce a small or uncertain danger, while larger, duller risks, the ones with no entrepreneur and no footage, remain under-attended.

Kuran and Sunstein draw an institutional moral rather than a personal one. Individual awareness of the heuristic is not enough, because the cascade is a property of what is sayable in public. They argue for procedures that slow the translation of salience into law: a demand for base rates, a separation between the dramatic case and the exposure of the population, and a willingness to compare the risk being regulated with the risks that regulation itself can create, including the risk of frightening people away from something safer. Cost-benefit analysis, in their telling, is partly a device for making unavailable numbers visible again.

How to read the argument

Read as psychology, the paper is an application of Tversky and Kahneman to a group. The individual substitutes ease for frequency. The group manufactures ease, by repeating the same examples until they are the ones everyone can recall. Read as political economy, the paper is a warning about how agendas form. The risks that get institutions are not a clean function of expected harm. They are a function of expected harm filtered through what can be communicated, moralised and punished. Availability entrepreneurs understand this filter better than the average citizen does, which is why the cascade is not a purely accidental process.

The argument also explains neglect, not only panic. A hazard with no picture, no villain and no early speaker can stay unavailable, however large the quiet toll. The cascade is a theory of distortion in both directions. It predicts overreaction to the salient and underreaction to the statistical, and it treats those as two faces of the same public psychology.

Limits

Because the paper argues from cases, it is exposed to the charge it levels at everyone else. The episodes it finds most available are the ones that fit a cascade. A reader can ask whether Love Canal or Alar, told by a different historian, would look more like a reasonable response to hidden danger and corporate secrecy than like a cognitive accident. Case narratives do not include the control, a similar trigger that did not cascade, so the theory is better at organising episodes that did run away than at predicting which spark will catch. Where the scientific record was genuinely uncertain at the time, calling the outcome a cascade can be hindsight.

There is a related limit of sympathy. The reputational engine is real, and so is the temptation to treat every frightened public as a failed statistician. People sometimes demand regulation because the harm is inequitably distributed, involuntary, or imposed on children, even when the expected death count is modest. Those are moral reasons, not availability errors. A theory that folds all of them into cognitive bias will recommend the wrong reforms. Kuran and Sunstein know the difference in principle. Their examples do not always keep it sharp, and a regulator armed only with this paper could dismiss a constituency by calling it a cascade.

Replications and critiques

This is not a result that replicates in the laboratory sense. What followed was argument. Sunstein developed the picture in later books on risk and fear, pressing the case that democratic governments mishandle danger when they regulate by headline. Critics answered that the cascade story underweights genuine uncertainty, that experts have cascades of their own, and that a call for cost-benefit analysis can be captured by the industries being regulated. Others accepted the mechanism and disputed the morals of particular cases. The psychological piece, social proof plus the rising ease of a repeated example, is the part that has travelled most quietly into later work on rumours, financial panics and online attention, usually without the original legal programme attached.

A useful test, which the paper itself does not run, is whether a cascade reverses when base rates are made as available as the story. Sometimes publicity of the numbers does deflate a scare. Sometimes it does not, because the disagreement was never about the numbers. Where moral outrage or distrust of institutions is the driver, more statistics will not function as the antidote the theory hopes for. That limit does not make the mechanism imaginary. It marks where availability stops and legitimacy begins.

Why it matters here

The availability card moves from a person recalling words and personal episodes to a public that buys flood insurance after a flood and then lets the policy lapse, and to risks that dominate the news out of proportion to their toll. Kuran and Sunstein supply the missing middle. Individual ease of recall does not stay individual once cameras, campaigns and official statements start choosing which examples everyone will have. The card's advice, to ask how often a thing actually happens, is the private version of the discipline they want from regulators. Read this paper as the account of how a heuristic becomes a policy, and read it with Slovic alongside, so that a duller risk is not mistaken for the only risk that counts.
