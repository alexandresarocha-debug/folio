The 2008 paper showed that a history of earlier changes made the citrate trait possible. It did not say what those changes were. In 2012 Zachary Blount, Jeffrey Barrick, Carla J. Davidson and Richard Lenski published a genome study in Nature to find out. They analysed the genomes of bacteria from the Ara-3 line, including clones from before and after the change.

Their account has three stages. The first is potentiation: earlier mutations that created a genetic background in which the new trait could appear and survive. The second is actualization: the mutation that actually gave the bacteria the ability to use citrate in oxygen. The third is refinement: later changes that improved how well they used it.

The key mutation is a good example of how new functions arise. E. coli already has a gene, called citT, for a transporter that brings citrate into the cell. But that gene is switched on only when oxygen is absent, so it is silent in the flask. The paper found that part of the DNA, including citT, had been duplicated, so that a copy sat next to a promoter that is active when oxygen is present. A promoter is the switch that turns a gene on. The new arrangement put citT under the control of a switch that works in oxygen. Nothing entirely new was invented; an old part was rewired.

The first version of the trait was not very good. The refinement stage increased the number of copies of the new arrangement, which improved the use of citrate. This fits the idea that a major innovation is often first a weak, rough solution that is then improved.

What this means in real life: big changes may be built from small ordinary steps, such as copying a part and attaching it to a different control. It also explains why a trait might not appear for a long time, then spread fast, once the first rough version is useful.

Criticism: later work has gone on to identify particular enabling mutations, and the interpretation of the delay as contingency has been questioned in commentary. The core finding, that the trait came from a duplication placing citT under a promoter active in oxygen, is the established part.

Source. Blount, Z. D., Barrick, J. E., Davidson, C. J., and Lenski, R. E. (2012). Genomic analysis of a key innovation in an experimental Escherichia coli population. Nature, 489(7417), 513-518. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11514
