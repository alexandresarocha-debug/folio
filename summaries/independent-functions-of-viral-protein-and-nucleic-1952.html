Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase, at the Carnegie Institution's Department of Genetics at Cold Spring Harbor, worked with T2, a virus that infects the bacterium E. coli. A phage particle is made of protein and DNA. Protein contains sulfur but almost no phosphorus; DNA contains phosphorus but no sulfur. So they grew one batch of phage with radioactive sulfur and another with radioactive phosphorus, which let them follow each part separately.

If the protein coat stays outside the bacterium while the DNA goes in, it should be possible to knock the empty coats off. To test this, they let labelled phage attach to bacteria and then spun the suspension in a Waring blender to shear the empty coats off. The blender released about 75% of the phage sulfur from the cells but only about 15% of the phosphorus. Infected cells from which 80% of the parental sulfur had been stripped still went on to produce new phage.

They then looked at the next generation. The new phage particles contained less than 1% of the parental sulfur, but 30% or more of the parental phosphorus. The DNA, not the coat protein, was what passed into the cell and on to the offspring.

The authors drew a careful conclusion. The sulfur-containing protein forms a protective coat that attaches the phage to the bacterium and acts as an instrument for injecting the DNA, and it probably has no function in the growth of the phage inside the cell. The DNA has some function. They added: 'Further chemical inferences should not be drawn from the experiments presented.'

What this means in real life: the experiment became the textbook demonstration that genes are made of DNA, and labelling molecules with radioactive tracers became a standard way of following them inside cells.

Caution: the separation was not clean. About a quarter of the sulfur label could not be sheared off, and the authors stated that they had not determined whether sulfur-free material other than DNA also enters the cell. On its own the experiment showed where the DNA goes, not that DNA alone carries the genes.

Source. Hershey, A. D., and Chase, M. (1952). Independent functions of viral protein and nucleic acid in growth of bacteriophage. Journal of General Physiology, 36(1), 39-56. doi:10.1085/jgp.36.1.39. Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2147348/
