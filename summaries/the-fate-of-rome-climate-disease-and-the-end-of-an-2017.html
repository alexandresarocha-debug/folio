Kyle Harper opens the comparison that organizes The Fate of Rome (Princeton University Press, 2017) with two numbers. The Antonine plague, which began in AD 165 and was probably smallpox, "carried off perhaps seven million victims." At Adrianople in 378, the worst day on a Roman battlefield in the imperial period, "at most twenty thousand Roman lives were lost." His sentence is, "germs are far deadlier than Germans." He puts the population of the empire, at its mid-second-century peak, near seventy-five million. He is explicit that other historians have put the Antonine death toll anywhere from about 2 percent of the population to more than a third, a range of roughly 1.5 to 25 million. Seven million is his own middle estimate, not a census.

The second case is the plague that reached the Mediterranean in 541. It arrived at Pelusium, in Egypt, then Alexandria and Constantinople. Harper follows contemporary witnesses. Procopius, in the capital in 542, describes the daily toll rising from five thousand deaths to ten thousand and beyond. John of Ephesus gives a running count that climbed through five, seven, twelve, and sixteen thousand a day, and says the dead were numbered up to 230,000 and then brought out uncounted, with more than 300,000 dead in all. Harper sets a figure of about 250,000 to 300,000 against a city he takes to have held about half a million people, in the same range as the worst local death rates of the Black Death. Justinian himself caught the disease and survived. An edict of 1 March 542 already speaks of death having reached every place. Harper's larger claim is that this pandemic, arriving in a cooler and less stable climate after the volcanic dimming of the sun in the 530s, stopped a Roman recovery that Justinian's conquests had begun.

The numbers decide what kind of story the fall becomes. If germs killed millions and armies killed thousands, then a history made only of battles is missing the larger mortality. Harper wants climate and disease in the explanation beside politics. The difficulty is that the ancient tallies are literary, and they were written by men who thought they were watching the end of the world.

Lee Mordechai and colleagues, in a 2019 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, argued that those tallies are not supported by the other evidence. Among 505 Egyptian papyri dated to individual years from 520 to 570, they found no drop in documents, no abandoned land, and no tax collapse, and the estates of the Apion family show revenue up by about 30 percent between the 540s and the 580s. Pollen from the eastern Mediterranean shows no sixth-century crash in cereal farming of the kind visible in parts of Europe after the Black Death. The bacterium was real. A death toll of tens of millions is what the argument is now about.
