An empire as an ecological system

Kyle Harper's The Fate of Rome: Climate, Disease, and the End of an Empire, published in 2017, retells the Roman story with two actors that older political histories left at the edge. The climate set the conditions of harvest, and microbes moved along the same roads and sea lanes that carried grain, soldiers, and taxes. Harper does not ask the reader to throw out emperors and armies. He asks the reader to see the empire as a biological and environmental system, whose connectivity was a source of wealth and a source of danger. The fate in the title is not a metaphor for bad decisions alone. It is the result of human institutions meeting natural forces they could not see and could only partly manage.

The opening claim is about timing and advantage. The centuries in which Rome expanded and then held the Mediterranean coincided with a period scientists call the Roman Climate Optimum, a long stretch of relatively warm, stable, and, in much of the basin, favourable moisture. Harper treats this as a tailwind for an agrarian empire. More reliable harvests supported population, cities, and the surplus that taxation captured. He does not say the weather made the legions. He says the legions and the tax state grew inside a climate that made their food supply easier than it would later be.

Connectivity, and the first pandemic blows

The same integration that moved Egyptian grain to Rome moved pathogens. Dense cities, a standing army, and regular shipping created a disease environment different from a world of isolated villages. Harper's first great shock is the Antonine plague, beginning in the mid-160s, which he takes to be smallpox, on the basis of the medical descriptions, above all Galen's, while treating the identification as probable rather than proven. The mortality, in his reading, was high enough to strain the army, the mines, and the fiscal system. The empire continued. It continued with a smaller population and with a demonstration that its network could carry death as efficiently as it carried goods.

A second blow, the Plague of Cyprian in the middle of the third century, falls in the decades when the imperial succession had already broken down and the frontiers were under attack. Harper is more cautious about the pathogen. The symptoms reported by contemporaries do not identify it with the confidence he allows for later plague. What he insists on is the conjunction. Political fragmentation, military crisis, and epidemic arrived together. The recovery under Diocletian and Constantine, which political historians rightly admire, took place in a harsher disease environment than the Antonine age. The restored state was real. It was rebuilt on a population that had been hit twice.

A cooler world and the Justinianic pandemic

The later chapters turn to the sixth century, where the scientific evidence is strongest and the narrative is most dramatic. Written sources describe a darkened sky and failed harvests beginning in 536. Climate science connects that dust veil, and a second major eruption around 540, to a long cold phase now called the Late Antique Little Ice Age, running from 536 to around 660. Harper uses tree-ring reconstructions and related proxies for this, and he sets the cold beside the agrarian needs of an empire that still depended on the Nile, the rains of the Levant, and the harvests of Anatolia.

In 541, plague arrived in Egypt and spread through the eastern empire and beyond. Ancient DNA from sixth-century burials has identified the bacterium as Yersinia pestis, the organism of bubonic plague, the same species that would cause the Black Death and a different outbreak of it. Harper treats the Justinianic plague as a demographic catastrophe, renewed in later waves across the sixth, seventh, and eighth centuries, and he places Justinian's wars, laws, and buildings inside that catastrophe. The attempt to recover the west, and the effort to sustain a great imperial capital, collided with a population and a climate that no longer supported the old margin of surplus. The eastern empire survived. Harper's point is that it survived in a reduced and altered form, and that nature had done part of the reducing.

Nature through society

The book's own caution is as important as its drama. Harper repeats that climate and disease acted through institutions. A different state, with different stores, different cities, and a different way of feeding the poor and the army, would have absorbed the same shocks differently. He is not offering a story in which weather replaces politics. He is offering a story in which politics is the way a society meets weather and infection. The Roman achievement, the roads and the cities and the grain fleets, was also the Roman vulnerability. When the climate stopped being so kind, and when new pathogens found the network, the institutions that had been a triumph became a conduit.

He is also limited by the evidence, and he says so in the way he ranks his plagues. The Justinianic identification rests on genomes. The Antonine identification rests on symptoms and on the pattern of spread. The Cyprianic identification is weaker still. Mortality at the level of the whole empire is not a figure one can read off a tombstone. Harper argues from the convergence of texts, archaeology, and natural science, and the convergence is stronger for the sixth century than for the second. A reader can accept the ecological frame and still think a particular chapter overdraws the death toll.

What the argument leaves open

The debate that followed the book has concentrated on that last point. Some historians, looking at coins, documents, pollen, and the distribution of written references, argue that the Justinianic plague was locally terrible and imperially less decisive than the maximal reading claims. Harper's reply, in the book and after it, is that a pandemic can be uneven and still historically enormous, and that the biological identification of the pathogen has raised the burden on anyone who wants to treat the literary accounts as mere rhetoric. The argument is not settled by the book. The book is what made the argument central.

Where the older fall narratives asked about barbarism, virtue, and the army, Harper asks about harvests, ports, and immunity. The western loss of the fifth century still has its military history. The eastern transformation of the sixth and seventh has, in his account, an environmental one. The end of the ancient world becomes a conjuncture: political and military crisis, a less favourable climate, and a disease regime the classical city had not evolved to survive. None of the three, in the book, is decorative.

Why it matters here

The Folio card harper-climate-plague is a summary of this book, and the summary is only as good as the distinction Harper himself draws. The Roman Climate Optimum, the Antonine plague, the Plague of Cyprian, the cold that begins in 536, and the Justinianic plague identified as Yersinia pestis are the sequence the card relies on. The card is right that Harper's claim is not a replacement of political history by nature. It is the claim that these shocks repeatedly knocked the empire down, and that the empire's own connectivity decided how far the knock travelled.

The card also points at the scientific papers and at the critics, and the book is where those belong in one argument. Tree rings and genomes are not illustrations added at the end. They are the reason a historian can now talk about 536 and about the bacterium of 541 with a kind of evidence Gibbon did not have. The critics who think the death toll is overstated are part of the card's warning, and they are a warning about this book in particular. Reading Harper is how the card's double caution becomes concrete. Nature is a real actor in the fall and the survival of Rome, and the size of that actor's role, plague by plague, is still something the evidence has to earn.
