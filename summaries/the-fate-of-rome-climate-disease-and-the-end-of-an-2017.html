Kyle Harper's The Fate of Rome (Princeton University Press, 2017) puts a pandemic at the turn of Roman history. In 165 a disease he identifies as smallpox, the Antonine plague, broke out in the empire, carried back with the army from a Parthian war. His estimate is that it killed perhaps seven million people, and he notes that some scholars have put the toll higher. Behind the plague he sets climate. He divides the centuries into a Roman Climate Optimum, about 200 BC to AD 150, which he describes as warm, wet, and stable across the Mediterranean heartland; a less steady Roman Transitional Period, about 150 to 450; and a Late Antique Little Ice Age, about 450 to 700. The Romans, he writes, were "in planetary perspective, lucky," and then the luck ran out.

The second shock is the one ancient writers saw. In 536 the sun dimmed. Procopius, who lived through it, wrote that for a whole year the sun gave its light without brightness, like the moon. Ice cores and tree rings put large volcanic eruptions in 536 and 540. In 541 plague reached the Egyptian port of Pelusium, and in 542 it was in Constantinople. This pathogen was Yersinia pestis, bubonic plague, and it returned in waves into the eighth century. Harper argues that these blows, landing on dense cities and long grain routes, did work that court politics cannot explain on their own. The same roads and ships that moved grain moved germs. A different set of institutions, he allows, might have absorbed the deaths differently.

The seven million is his calculation, not a census, and the smallpox identification is a reading of Galen's case notes, not a recovered genome. On the later plague the contrary evidence is a set of counts. Lee Mordechai and colleagues, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2019, found no mid-sixth-century break in the yearly number of Egyptian papyri, in cereal pollen from the eastern Mediterranean, or in the income of the Apion estates, which rose by about 30 percent between the 540s and the 580s. A death toll of a quarter or a half of the Mediterranean population, the figure their paper says had become the consensus, should have shown up in those records. Harper's account of the 536 dust veil rests on the physical evidence. The scale of the Justinianic dying is the part that evidence has been used to challenge.
