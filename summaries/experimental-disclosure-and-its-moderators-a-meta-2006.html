After the 1986 study, many researchers tried versions of the writing task: people write for a few sessions about a stressful experience, and the researchers compare them with others who write about something neutral. By the early 2000s two small reviews had suggested that this experimental disclosure, as it is called, works. Joanne Frattaroli, a psychologist, thought they were limited. Both had used a fixed effects approach, which treats all the studies as if they measured one single true effect, and many more studies had been done since.

Her paper in Psychological Bulletin in 2006 gathered 146 randomised studies. She used random effects analyses, which allow the true effect to differ between studies. The headline is that experimental disclosure is effective, with a positive and significant average effect of r = .075.

That number needs reading carefully. A correlation of .075 is small. Squared, it means that the writing explains about half of one percent of the differences in outcomes between people. It is real on average, but it will not be obvious in any one person. The paper also identified a number of moderators, which are features of the studies that change how large the effect is. Summaries of the paper list things such as who took part, what they were told to write, the topic, the format of the sessions and the follow-up time.

What this means in real life: expressive writing is a cheap and low-risk exercise, so a small average benefit may be worth a try, especially if it feels useful to you. But it should not be oversold. It is not a substitute for treatment when someone is struggling, and the benefits are not guaranteed.

Criticism and limits: meta-analyses can only be as good as their input, and many outcomes in this field are self-reported. A small effect that varies this much between studies is a reason to be modest about promises. The paper's own value is that it replaced a single exciting 1986 result with a sober average over the whole body of work.
