Most earlier studies of the 'screen inferiority effect' used desktop or laptop computers. Tablets and e-readers are designed to feel more like books, so Ladislao Salmerón and colleagues asked whether the paper advantage survives on handheld devices, which are now common in schools.

Their search, completed in June 2022, yielded 1,845 records. The final sample had 49 studies and 63 effect sizes, analysed separately for designs comparing different people and designs where the same people read on both media.

Both analyses found a significant but small advantage for print: g = -0.113 for between-participant studies and g = -0.103 for within-participant studies. The authors note that only a few individual studies found significant differences on their own.

Some moderators mattered. The screen disadvantage was larger for undergraduates than for primary and secondary school pupils, and larger when people were tested individually rather than in groups. Unlike the earlier meta-analysis, this one did not find that time limits or publication year changed the size of the effect, and none of the text features analysed, including genre, length and the way readers navigated the text, made a significant difference.

What this means in real life: tablets in classrooms are not a disaster for reading, but print still gives a slight edge for comprehension, so the authors recommend continuing to foster print reading in schools alongside thoughtful use of devices.

Caution: the effect is small and varied across studies, and many questions, such as how long-term habits of digital reading affect comprehension, remain open. Moderator analyses in a meta-analysis are correlational and can be influenced by the particular studies that happen to exist.
