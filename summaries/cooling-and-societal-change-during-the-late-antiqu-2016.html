Ulf Büntgen and his colleagues measured the rings of 660 Siberian larches from five high sites in the Russian Altai and Sayan mountains: 152 living trees and 508 dead ones, 234,399 individual ring widths in all. Summer temperature is what limits growth at those elevations, so a narrow ring is a cold summer. In a 2016 paper in Nature Geoscience they set this record beside a tree-ring reconstruction from the Austrian Alps, about 7,600 kilometers away. The two move together, with a correlation that is statistically very strong. Both show a long cold phase opening in AD 536, after a cluster of large volcanic eruptions in 536, 540, and 547. Büntgen's group named the interval from 536 to about 660 the Late Antique Little Ice Age, and they judged it harsher than the later medieval Little Ice Age. In their reconstructions, decades inside it rank among the ten coldest decades of the Common Era.

They then place that cold next to a list of political and biological events: the Justinianic plague from 541, the wars that reshaped the eastern Roman empire, the collapse of the Sasanian empire, movements off the Asian steppe, the spread of Slavic-speaking peoples, and upheaval in China. The climate claim and the historical claim are different sizes. The rings and the eruptions are measurements. The suggestion that this cold helped cause each of those changes is a correlation across a century and a quarter, offered as an extra factor, not as a dated mechanism for any one battle or migration.

A person using 536 as an explanation of the seventh century needs the duration to be real, and needs the cold to have reached the place in question. A cold decade in the Alps can wreck a harvest there. It does not, by itself, move an army in Arabia or empty a Chinese province. The useful discipline is to ask which record is cold, for which years, and which event actually sits inside those years.

That test was applied quickly. In 2017 Samuli Helama, Phil Jones, and Keith Briffa replied in the same journal. Averaging the extratropical Northern Hemisphere records they could use, they found a cold interval from 536 into the 570s. The continuation all the way toward the 660s was clear in the Alps and the Altai, the two regions Büntgen had relied on, and much less clear elsewhere. Of the thirteen series they screened, only a limited number showed a cold spell of the full length proposed. Large-scale Eurasian cooling after the eruptions, on their reading, lasted until the 570s at the latest. Büntgen's group answered that glaciers and the combined historical evidence still supported a longer cold phase, and that several of the contradicting records were weak. The sharp cooling in the middle of the sixth century is the part both sides accept.
