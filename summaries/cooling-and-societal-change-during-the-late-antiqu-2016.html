Background

Writers in the sixth century said that the sun dimmed, that summer felt like winter, and that harvests failed. Procopius and Cassiodorus are the names most often cited. For a long time those passages sat in the category of marvels, interesting to historians of mentality and easy to leave out of a political narrative. Volcanic explanations were proposed in the late twentieth century and then strengthened by sulphate in polar ice and by narrow rings in trees. By the time Ulf Büntgen and his colleagues wrote, the question was no longer whether 536 had been a bad year for sunlight. It was whether the cold lasted, how wide it was, and whether a climate historian had any business setting it beside the political history of Eurasia.

The sixth century is crowded with events that invite environmental stories and punish careless ones. The Justinianic plague begins in 541. The eastern Roman empire fights Persia and then loses vast provinces to the Arab conquests in the following century. The Sasanian empire collapses. Slavic-speaking communities expand across much of eastern and central Europe. Steppe politics shift. A cold decade can be made to explain all of this, which is a reason to be strict about what the climate record actually shows.

The question

The paper asks two linked questions. First, was there a prolonged, spatially coherent cooling in late antiquity, and what forced it? Second, does that cooling line up with the societal changes historians already group in these centuries, closely enough that climate should be counted as one of their causes? The authors answer the first question with a reconstruction. They answer the second more tentatively, and the difference between the two answers is the difference between a result and a suggestion.

How the study was built

The reconstruction uses tree-ring chronologies from the Russian Altai and the European Alps, two regions far apart where tree growth is sensitive to summer temperature. Rings can be dated to the year. A long overlap between living trees and older wood lets the authors build summer-temperature histories that run across two millennia, so that the sixth century can be compared with what came before and after, rather than described in isolation. They set this tree-ring evidence beside other signs of a wide northern-hemisphere cold phase, and beside the volcanic chronology. Ice-core and related work had identified a cluster of large eruptions in 536, 540, and 547. The design is comparative across space. A cold spell that appears in both the Altai and the Alps is harder to dismiss as a local accident of one forest.

The societal half of the paper is built differently. It is not a statistical model of harvests and revolts. It is a historical alignment. The authors place their cold interval next to events and processes already dated by historians and archaeologists, and they ask the reader to consider the cold as an additional factor. That second method is only as strong as the dating of the events and the plausibility of the mechanisms, which they sketch rather than demonstrate case by case.

What was found

The tree rings show a sharp cooling from 536, reinforced by the eruptions that follow, and a run of unusually cold summers that is long rather than momentary. The authors identify the interval from 536 to about 660 as a distinct phase and give it a name, the Late Antique Little Ice Age. They argue that the cold was not confined to one mountain range. It spans a large part of the Northern Hemisphere. Volcanic eruptions are the trigger they emphasise. They also point to ocean and sea-ice feedbacks, and to a solar minimum, as reasons the cold could persist after the initial dust had fallen out of the air. Persistence is the finding that changes the historical conversation. A single year without a summer is a disaster. A cold phase lasting more than a century is a different kind of context, even if individual decades inside it varied.

What the authors make of it

They suggest that this cold phase be treated as one environmental factor among the causes of a list of changes: the establishment of the Justinianic plague, the transformation of the eastern Roman empire, the collapse of the Sasanian empire, movements of peoples out of the Asian steppe and the Arabian peninsula, the spread of Slavic-speaking peoples, and political upheaval in China. The verb they choose, in substance, is contribute. They do not claim that cooling founded the plague or toppled an empire by itself. They claim that historians of those changes are looking at societies under an unusual climatic stress, and that the stress lasted long enough to matter for more than one harvest.

The mechanism they have in mind is the ordinary one. Colder summers reduce yields. Failed yields produce famine, movement, and political strain. A population already hungry may be more vulnerable to epidemic disease. A state at war has less surplus to lose. None of this is exotic. What was missing, before a dated temperature reconstruction, was a reason to prefer it to a story told entirely in palaces.

Limits and later criticism

Tree rings of this kind record the growing-season temperature at the sites where the trees stood. The Alps are not the Nile valley, and the Altai is not the Syrian steppe. A hemispheric cold phase can still hide regional exceptions, and agriculture depends on rainfall and on the timing of seasons, not only on summer warmth in the mountains. The name Late Antique Little Ice Age, and the end date around 660, are interpretive frames placed on a reconstruction. Other researchers can draw the boundaries differently without disputing the cold of the 540s.

The societal list is the part that has drawn the sharpest historical criticism, and it deserves it. A long cold period overlaps many events. Overlap is not a cause. Some societies in the same centuries consolidated, migrated successfully, or built new political orders, which means cold was not a sentence. The paper is strongest as a climate reconstruction with a clearly named volcanic trigger, and most exposed where it offers one climate series as a contributing factor to half of Eurasia. Later work has continued to refine the dating of the eruptions and the spatial pattern of the cold. The historical argument has been absorbed, in a more cautious form, by historians such as Kyle Harper, and disputed by historians who think the political record of the sixth and seventh centuries has its own momentum. The right use of the paper is to keep those two assessments separate.

Why it matters here

The Folio card harper-climate-plague cites this study for a specific fact: tree rings reconstruct a cold period from 536 to about 660, the Late Antique Little Ice Age, beginning with the volcanic dimming of the sun and overlapping the Justinianic plague. That fact is what the paper established. The card's larger claim, taken from Harper, is that climate and disease knocked the Roman empire down repeatedly, acting through society rather than instead of it. Büntgen and his colleagues supply the climate half of the sixth-century case, and they supply it in a form a historian can date.

The connection is also a warning the card needs. The paper's own last step, from a temperature curve to a list of political transformations, is the step where environmental history either stays honest or becomes a universal explanation. The card says nature acted through institutions, and that the scale of particular plagues is still debated. This reconstruction supports the first of those sentences by giving the institutions a real climatic setting. It does not settle the second. A reader of the card who treats the Late Antique Little Ice Age as the cause of the end of the ancient world has gone further than the tree rings go. A reader who treats it as the best evidence that the sixth century was unusually cold, for a long time, across a very wide area, has understood what this paper added.
