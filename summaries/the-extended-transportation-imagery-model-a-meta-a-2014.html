Tom van Laer, Ko de Ruyter, Luca M. Visconti, and Martin Wetzels pooled 132 effect sizes from 76 articles on narrative transportation, the state in which a person enters a world a story evokes. Stories with characters a reader could pick out were more transporting, ρ = .20. Plots people could picture were more transporting, ρ = .29. Events that seemed possible were more transporting, ρ = .27. On the reader's side, attention correlated at ρ = .29, and being the sort of person who is easily carried into stories correlated at ρ = .30. Familiarity with the genre was ρ = .21. Sex was ρ = .15, and education ρ = .10. Age was ρ = .00.

Once people were transported, feelings moved the most, ρ = .57. Attitudes moved at ρ = .44, intentions at ρ = .31, and story-consistent beliefs at ρ = .26. Critical thoughts went down, ρ = −.20. The measuring stick mattered. Studies that used Green and Brock's transportation scale produced a smaller effect, ρ = .34, than studies that used some other scale, ρ = .56. The authors note that most of those other scales asked about positive feelings first, which can tug the later answers upward.

For an advertisement, a health leaflet, or a product page, the reliable part is the scene. A person you can picture, in events you can imagine and believe, shifts how you feel and what you plan to do more readily than it rewrites a factual belief. One study they draw on found that transportation into an ad whose character discovers she has skin cancer went with a greater willingness to use sunscreen. Another found the grimmer version of the same mechanism: identifiable characters who smoked were linked with a higher intention to smoke. The meta-analysis says that kind of shift is typical and moderate. The same absorption that goes with buying sunscreen goes with a higher intention to smoke, which is a reason to be careful about a story that asks you to feel with its people. A warmer questionnaire can make either effect look larger than Green and Brock's scale does.

Source. Tom van Laer, Ko de Ruyter, Luca M. Visconti, and Martin Wetzels, "The Extended Transportation-Imagery Model: A Meta-Analysis of the Antecedents and Consequences of Consumers' Narrative Transportation," Journal of Consumer Research 40, no. 5 (2014): 797–817. https://doi.org/10.1086/673383
