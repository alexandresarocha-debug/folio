A model stretched across a literature

In 2014 Tom van Laer, Ko de Ruyter, Luca M. Visconti, and Martin Wetzels published a meta-analysis in the Journal of Consumer Research that tries to gather what two decades of transportation research had been measuring. The title names the product: an extended transportation-imagery model, covering the antecedents and the consequences of narrative transportation in consumers. Green and Brock had built a scale and shown, in their own experiments, that absorption tracked belief. A large number of later studies had used that scale, or close cousins of it, on stories about brands, health, news, and other messages. Van Laer and his colleagues argue that the field had accumulated findings faster than it had accumulated a map. Their paper draws the map and then tests its paths with the numbers the studies report.

The search behind the map is wide on purpose. They look across psychology, communication, marketing, and neighbouring fields for work in which narrative transportation is measured as a state of being carried into a story world. The quantitative test uses 132 effect sizes from 76 articles, published and unpublished. That pool is the reason the paper can speak more broadly than any one experiment. It is also the reason a reader has to keep the mix in mind. A story designed to sell a product, a story designed to change a health belief, and a literary narrative used in a psychology laboratory are all eligible if they measured transportation and related it to a cause or an outcome. The model claims that the same skeleton is visible under those different skins.

What is supposed to produce transportation

The antecedents fall into two groups, matching the earlier transportation-imagery picture of a story on one side and a receiver on the other. On the story side, the model highlights characters a reader can identify, a plot a reader can imagine, and verisimilitude, the feeling that the tale could be a world. On the receiver side, it highlights familiarity with the topic or the genre, attention, and transportability as a difference among persons, the tendency some people have to be carried off more easily than others. Education and gender appear among the receiver factors the literature had been able to code. The point of listing them is to insist that transportation is a joint product. A well-made story meets a particular mind on a particular day. Neither side, in the model, is a sufficient cause to be left unmeasured.

The meta-analysis then asks which of these proposed causes actually line up with transportation scores across studies. The story-side qualities, identifiable characters, imaginable plot, and verisimilitude, come through as positive predictors in their tests. Attention, familiarity, and transportability do work on the receiver side as well. The paper is more cautious where the demographic results are thin or mixed, and a reader should be too. A significant path in a meta-analysis of observational and experimental mixes is a claim about a literature, about the studies that chose to measure both ends of the path. It is evidence that the skeleton is load-bearing. It is a weak licence for predicting any one campaign, any one reader, or any one novel.

What transportation is supposed to produce

The consequences side is the side Folio's card cites. Across the pool, transportation is associated with stronger affective response, more thoughts of the kind the story invites, stronger story-consistent beliefs, more favourable attitudes, and stronger intentions. It is associated with less critical thought. That last path is the meta-analytic echo of Green and Brock's false-note finding: the more absorbed readers are, the less they pick the story apart. Van Laer and colleagues treat the whole set as the payoff of the extended model. A consumer who has been transported is a consumer whose feelings, beliefs, and plans have shifted towards the story, and whose objections have gone quieter. In a journal of consumer research, the implications for advertising and brand storytelling are left in plain sight. A story can do work that a list of product claims does poorly, because the story changes the state in which the claims are heard.

The sizes of these associations vary, and the paper's own tables are a warning against treating every path as equally secure. Some links rest on many studies, some on fewer. Affect, belief, attitude, and intention are the outcomes the card has in view when it says that a later meta-analysis confirmed the predictive power of transportation. Confirmed, in this context, means that the association reappears when many studies are combined. It means a reliable pattern in measured responses. It means something short of a universal mechanism with a known dial. The authors also discuss the scales themselves as possible moderators, because the way you ask about transportation can change the size of the relation you then discover. A literature that uses one instrument tends to find the world that instrument can see.

How to carry the model back to ordinary reading

There is a gap between a consumer's transportation into a branded story and a reader's transportation into a novel, and the paper does not pretend the gap is empty. Its ambition is the common structure. Characters you can stay with, a plot you can picture, a world that holds together, a reader who is able and willing to attend: these are intelligible demands to make of a novel, and the consequences, feeling, belief, a quieter critic in the head, are intelligible descriptions of what novels do. The risk of the consumer frame is instrumentalism. If transportation is valuable because it raises intentions, then the story is a tool, and the tool is judged by the intention. Literature has other jobs, including jobs that leave a reader more critical than she was when she sat down. A model built to explain persuasion will be best at explaining persuasion. Used as a complete aesthetics, it would be a trap.

Used as a psychology of influence, it is a sober piece of work. It tells you that the Green and Brock result was not a one-laboratory curiosity. It tells you which features of stories and of audiences the later studies have found themselves measuring again and again. It tells you that critical thought moves in the opposite direction from absorption, which is exactly why transportation is powerful and why it calls for a counter-habit. The counter-habit is not contempt for stories. It is the decision, sometimes, to read twice: once as a traveller, and once as the person who has come back and can ask what beliefs arrived in the luggage.

Why it matters here

The card on narrative transportation pairs Green and Brock's original experiments with this wider test. Van Laer and colleagues are the wider test. They assemble an extended model, story features and receiver features in, feelings and beliefs and intentions out, critical thought diminished, and they find that the pattern holds across a large set of effect sizes in consumer and communication research. The card's practical sentence follows: a good story teaches and a good story manipulates, and transportation is a reason both can happen. Read the meta-analysis for the scope, the antecedents, and the modesty hidden in a large number of studies. The scope is real. The effect is an association in measured responses, strong enough to take seriously, and aimed, in this particular paper, at the world of consumers.
