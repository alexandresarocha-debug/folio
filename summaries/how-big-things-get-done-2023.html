The Sydney Opera House is the case Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner use for a project that started before anyone knew how to build it. Jørn Utzon won the competition with a sketch of the shells. Construction began before the engineering was solved, because the New South Wales government wanted enough concrete in the ground that a later government could not cancel the job. Work that had been built on the wrong assumptions later had to be blasted out. Utzon was pushed off the project before the interior was his to finish. Flyvbjerg puts the cost overrun at 1,400 percent and the delay at about ten years. The finished building became a landmark, and he argues that it has earned Sydney more than it cost. The years of construction were still a wreck, and they wrecked the architect's career. The book calls the sequence by its reverse: they thought fast and then were forced to act slowly.

The numbers behind the book come from a database of about 16,000 projects, of different sizes, drawn from many countries. In that record, 47.9 percent finished on budget. Only 8.5 percent finished on budget and on time. Half of one percent finished on budget, on time, and with the benefits that had been promised. Flyvbjerg's name for the pattern is the iron law: over budget, over time, under benefits, over and over again. The contrast in the book is a project planned in detail before anyone hurried. The publisher's account of the Empire State Building, which the book uses as one such case, is that it went from a sketch to the New York skyline in 21 months. Frank Gehry's Guggenheim in Bilbao is the other comparison: a long, iterative design, then a build that held.

The advice is to plan slowly and act fast. The time to argue, build a model, and look up what similar jobs actually cost is before the commitment is hard to undo. Once the work is underway, a short schedule is safer than a long one, because a long build is where the surprises accumulate. A kitchen, a software migration, and a rail line share that split. A vague start followed by a rush to get something in the ground is how the Opera House bought a decade of trouble. A finished plan, a class of comparable projects, and a short execution is the other order.
