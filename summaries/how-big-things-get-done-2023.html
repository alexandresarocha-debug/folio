Background

How Big Things Get Done, written by Bent Flyvbjerg with Dan Gardner and published in 2023, is the public statement of a research programme that had lived for years in planning journals. The programme's empirical core is a long record of megaprojects that cost more and take longer than their sponsors promised. Its practical core is the outside view: forecast from the class of similar projects, not from the story of this one. The book keeps both, and it adds a set of habits about how a project should be planned and how it should be built. Folio's card on the planning fallacy cites it for a recommendation that is easy to remember and harder to follow. Plan slowly. Deliver quickly. Prefer designs that have been repeated over designs that are unique.

A book of this kind is a synthesis with a teacher attached. It is not a new database, and it is not a novel. The walkthrough's job is to say which ideas are doing the work, and which temptations of the genre, the triumphant example, the villainous overrun, the reader should meet with the same outside view the book recommends.

The question

Why do big projects so often miss, and what do the ones that do not miss have in common? Flyvbjerg and Gardner are not short of mechanisms. The planning fallacy supplies the sincere error, a team in love with its schedule. Strategic misrepresentation supplies the insincere one, a number chosen because it will be approved. Politics supplies the deadline that arrives before the design is understood. The book's question is what a positive pattern looks like once those mechanisms are granted. If the great majority of megaprojects overrun, the interesting objects are the procedures that put a project in the minority, and the procedures that keep a society from betting as if the minority were the norm.

How the book is organised

The early chapters teach the outside view in the form a non-specialist can use. Your project is not a unique snowflake until the record says so. Other people have attempted things with your shape, and their outcomes are a better first forecast than your team's confidence. Reference-class forecasting, the method of the 2006 paper, appears here as a habit rather than as a formula: name the class, face its distribution, and let the inside view speak only afterwards. The book is frank that this habit feels like an insult. The inside view is where expertise lives, and being told to start elsewhere sounds like being told that expertise does not matter. The reply is that expertise matters most in choosing the class and in knowing which differences are real.

Later chapters turn from the forecast to the shape of the work. Think slow, act fast is the organising contrast. Planning should be long, cheap to revise, and full of experiments, because a mistake on paper is a different object from a mistake in concrete. Delivery should be short, because a long delivery window is a long opportunity for the world to change, for politics to shift and for the black swan the schedule forgot to arrive. A project that is slow to commit and quick to build spends its uncertainty in the phase where uncertainty is still editable. A project that commits in a weekend and builds for a decade does the reverse.

What the book claims about design

The design claim is modularity. Things that can be built from repeated units accumulate a reference class as they go. A module has been tried, revised and tried again. The next module inherits the learning. Projects composed of such units, in the book's account, have a better record than projects that are one-off monuments, bespoke from the ground up, with no neighbour to learn from and no piece that can fail without taking the whole with it. The psychological link is direct. A unique monument has no usable outside view, or has one the sponsors refuse to claim, because claiming it would mean admitting the monument is a member of a class of troubled monuments. A modular project generates its own outside view by repetition. The preference for uniqueness, which feels like ambition, is on this account a preference for being unforecastable.

Experience is treated similarly. Teams that have done the thing before are not morally superior. They are carriers of a reference class in the form of scar tissue. The book is suspicious of the heroic first attempt, the organisation that has never built a tunnel and is sure this tunnel will be the exception. The suspicion is the planning fallacy aimed at institutions. An institution's inside view is its org chart and its intentions. Its outside view is the record of institutions with that org chart, attempting that kind of work for the first time. The record is the one to open.

How the advice relates to the psychology

Think slow, act fast can be heard as a productivity slogan. In this book it is a corollary of the fallacy. Slow planning is the phase in which the outside view can still change the design, because the design is not yet a promise shouted in public. Fast delivery is the phase in which the inside view's optimism does the least damage, because there is less time for the neglected risks to accumulate, and because a modular build has fewer novel risks for them to be. The sequence is the opposite of the sequence megaprojects often follow, in which the decision is fast, the announcement is confident, and the building is a long education in everything the announcement left out.

The book also keeps strategic misrepresentation in the picture, so that the advice is not only therapeutic. A team can know the reference class and still publish a prettier number, if the prettier number is what gets the project approved and the overrun will be someone else's problem. Thinking slow helps only if the slow thought is allowed to reach the decision. The authors' institutional moral is that the class has to be demanded by the people who can say no, not merely contemplated by the people who want the project to live. That moral is continuous with the 2006 paper. The book adds the design habits that make a truthful class less frightening, because a modular, experienced, quickly delivered project is one a truthful class can actually smile on.

Limits

Examples in a book of advice are chosen to teach, and the choosing is a kind of inside view at one remove. A successful modular project and a disastrous bespoke one make a memorable pair. They do not, by themselves, establish the frequency of either outcome. The frequencies live in the research the book is popularising, and a reader who wants the distribution rather than the illustration should treat the 2006 paper and Flyvbjerg's empirical work as the source, and the book as the interpretation. Where the book says that the great majority of big projects overrun, it is leaning on that research. Where it tells a particular story, it is teaching. The two are easy to blur, and the blur flatters the prose.

There is a limit of fit. Not every valuable undertaking can be modular. Some works are singular by nature, and the book's advice for them is not a hidden recipe for pretending they are solar farms. It is the stricter demand that their uniqueness be priced, using the gloomiest honest class, and that the planning absorb the singularity before the concrete is poured. A reader in search of permission to build a monument without a reference class will not find it. A reader in search of a claim that monuments are never worth it will not find that either. The claim is about the order of operations.

What the book is for

The book is for taking a laboratory finding and a specialist procedure and making them into a standard of taste. The laboratory finding is Buehler, Griffin and Ross: people forecast from the plan and neglect the record, even when the record is their own. The specialist procedure is reference-class forecasting. The standard of taste is the new part, and it is what a trade book can add. Uniqueness is expensive. Speed of announcement is not a virtue. Repetition is a form of intelligence. Experience is a forecast. None of these sentences is a statistic. All of them are the book's attempt to make the outside view feel like craft rather than like pessimism.

Used that way, the book is a companion to the card rather than a replacement for the papers. It will not show you a think-aloud protocol or a regression. It will show you why a person who has understood the protocols might still approve the next unique, fast-decided, slow-built scheme, and what the alternative posture looks like when someone has practised it. The posture is the deliverable.

Why it matters here

Folio's card ends its path at this book for a reason. The thesis study shows the fallacy in a single student. The 2006 paper shows the forecasting rule that the fallacy requires once the project can hurt a budget. How Big Things Get Done shows the surrounding craft: slow planning, fast delivery, modular repetition, teams that have been here before. The psychology is unchanged from Kahneman and Tversky's outside view. What changes is the reader's ability to recognise the inside view when it arrives dressed as vision. Read the experiments for the mechanism, the 2006 paper for the reference class, and this book for the habits that keep a reference class from being a document everyone praises and nobody starts from.
