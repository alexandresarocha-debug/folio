Charles Clotfelter and Philip Cook, economists at Duke University, studied state lotteries for years. In this short paper they asked whether ordinary lottery players act on the gambler's fallacy, the belief that a chance event becomes less likely right after it has happened.

Their test was Maryland's daily three-digit numbers game, where players pick a number from 000 to 999. A straight bet wins only if all three digits come up in the right order, a 1 in 1,000 chance, and Maryland paid $500 on a $1 bet, in line with its policy of returning about half of the money bet as prizes. Because every number is equally likely every day, there is no reason to bet less on a number that has just won.

They took 52 consecutive winning numbers drawn in March and April 1988 and tracked how much money was bet on each, adjusted for the day's total betting, from the day before it won to 84 days after. On the day after a number was drawn, betting on it fell in 37 of the 52 cases. Three days later it had fallen in every case. The median ratio of betting to its level around the draw fell from 1.00 to 0.64 after three days, then crept back, reaching 0.93 after 84 days. Players behaved as if a winning number had been used up.

The data also showed strong favourites among numbers. Betting on a single day ranged from very low for some numbers to many times higher for others, with players preferring numbers such as triples, simple sequences and dates.

What this means in real life: avoiding last week's winning numbers does not improve your odds.

Caution: the sample is one game in one state over two months. The paper is based on aggregate betting, so it cannot say which individual players changed their choices or why. The figures quoted here come from the 1991 working paper, read as a scanned copy.
