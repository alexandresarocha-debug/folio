Between 1517 and 1520, thirty publications by Martin Luther probably sold well over 300,000 copies. Elizabeth Eisenstein does not offer that figure as her own count. She opens her account of the Reformation, in The Printing Press as an Agent of Change (1979), by quoting the historian A. G. Dickens, and then she stays with his conclusion. Lutheranism was from the start the child of the printed book. John Wyclif's followers and the Waldensians had preached dissent without a press. Their texts could be hunted down copy by copy, and each new copy could drift. Luther's German pamphlets, cartoons, and translations went out in batches that matched. A reader in one town and a reader in another could be answering the same sentences.

Eisenstein's name for what the press added is typographical fixity. A scribe does not only make errors. He also "improves" a text, and the next scribe improves the improvement, until a scholar cannot be sure which words were the author's. A printed edition is one setting of type, multiplied. It can still be wrong. She says ignorant printers multiplied error, and pirated editions multiplied it again. The difference is that an error in type can be seen against another copy and corrected in the next edition. Preservation and the chance to correct in public are, for her, one change.

She puts the same change under two books that left the press in 1543. Andreas Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica and Nicolaus Copernicus's De revolutionibus orbium coelestium were both large technical works, aimed at replacing a classical authority, Galen in one case and Ptolemy in the other. Anatomy and astronomy had been trying to recover ancient masters for centuries. What was new, Eisenstein argues, was that the next generation could hold the old book and the new book at the same time, including the pictures, and argue about a diagram that was the same diagram in Basel and in Padua. A corrected woodblock could then be printed again. Knowledge could accumulate because the last result did not have to be copied by hand before anyone else could use it.

The practical test is whether a claim refers to a text someone else can check. After the middle of the fifteenth century, a religious argument and a scientific diagram could become a public fight, because the page traveled and stayed put. Before that, "the" text was often whatever the nearest manuscript happened to say. Eisenstein's point is not that printing caused the Reformation or the new science by itself. It is that both were fought out in a world where copies matched.

Adrian Johns argued in The Nature of the Book (1998) that early print was nothing like that stable. Editions of the same work differed, printers pirated one another, and a reader had to decide which copy to trust. The trust, he said, was built by guilds and scientific societies. It was not a property of the machine. Eisenstein's reply, in the American Historical Review in 2002, was that she had always compared the handpress with the scribe, not with the later steam press, and that a messy London trade does not wipe out the European change.
