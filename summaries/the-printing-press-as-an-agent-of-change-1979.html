The press as a cause, not a backdrop

Elizabeth Eisenstein’s The Printing Press as an Agent of Change, published in 1979 in two volumes, asks historians to stop treating the press as a piece of scenery. The usual story, she argues, mentions Gutenberg, notes that books became cheaper, and then hurries on to the Reformation or the Scientific Revolution as if the medium in which those movements were conducted did not shape them. Eisenstein wants the shift from script to print described in its own right. Her title is a claim of causation. The press was an agent, not a neutral channel. She is careful about what that does not mean. Printing did not by itself produce Luther’s theology or Newton’s physics. It changed the conditions under which texts survived, travelled, and could be compared, and those conditions changed what scholars, preachers, and readers could do.

The book is a long essay in interpretation, built from the existing historical literature rather than from a new archive of one print shop. That choice is both its reach and its vulnerability. Eisenstein can talk about the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the new science in one frame. She can also be challenged by specialists who know one print shop better than her synthesis does. She presents the work as a hypothesis about a shift in communications, and she asks historians of ideas to take the hypothesis as seriously as they take doctrines.

What print did that copying did not

The conceptual core is a comparison between scribal culture and print culture. A manuscript tradition can preserve a text for centuries, and it can also lose it in a generation. Each copy differs from the last. Glosses accumulate, lines drop out, diagrams degrade, and a scholar in one city cannot be sure that a scholar in another is looking at the same passage. Eisenstein picks out features of print that, taken together, amount to a different regime. Dissemination: many more copies could exist, and could exist at the same time. Standardisation: the copies of an edition were alike enough that separated readers could refer to the same page, the same diagram, the same map, the same table. Fixity: a text, once printed in quantity, was much harder to lose, and a corpus of texts could accumulate instead of being repeatedly recovered and repeatedly corrupted.

She insists on the combination. Dissemination without standardisation would multiply chaos. Fixity without dissemination would preserve a text that almost nobody saw. Print’s power, in her account, is that the three features arrived together, and that they applied to images as well as to words. A botanical woodcut, a mathematical diagram, or a map could be reproduced with a fidelity no team of copyists could sustain. She also notes the reorganisation of the book itself: title pages, indexes, and the habit of correcting an edition and printing the correction. Fixity was never absolute. Errors were printed too, and printed errors travelled further than scribal ones. Her claim is comparative. Beside the manuscript, the printed edition was a new kind of stable object.

Renaissance and Reformation

Eisenstein uses that regime to reread two movements that predate any simple story of “print caused modernity”. The Renaissance, she argues, began in manuscript culture and was transformed once classical recovery passed into print. A scribal renaissance could revive a text and then lose it again. A print renaissance could make the recovery cumulative. Scholars across Europe could inhabit one library of editions rather than a scatter of unique copies. She does not deny the brilliance of the manuscript humanists. She denies that their achievement would have had the same historical shape without the press that came at its heels.

The Reformation is her clearest case of a movement that was a print event from early on. Luther’s theses and the sermons that followed became pamphlets, vernacular scripture, and woodcut images, produced at a speed and in numbers that no earlier heresy had commanded. Eisenstein’s comparison is with Wycliffe and Hus, movements that authorities did manage, with great violence, to contain. She does not argue that print made those earlier critiques less true, or Luther’s more true. She argues that a schism conducted through the press was much harder to silence. Catholic authorities used the same press, and built censorship and indexes of forbidden books, which in her account is further evidence that everyone involved understood the new conditions. The Reformation is not, on her telling, a proof that print favours Protestantism. It is a proof that print changed the scale on which a religious argument could be public.

Science and the cumulative page

The scientific revolution is the case where standardisation and fixity do the most work. Eisenstein links the new science to the possibility of reliable diagrams, star charts, anatomical images, and mathematical tables, and to a community of readers who could check a claim against a shared text. Errors could be found because the thing in which the error sat was duplicated. Observations could accumulate because the record was no longer at the mercy of a single copy in a single college. She is interested in the print shop itself as a social space, where scholars, artisans, correctors, and publishers had to cooperate. The commonwealth of learning, in this picture, is partly a commonwealth of printed objects.

She does not claim that printing was sufficient for science. Chinese and Korean printing, and European printing in places that did not have a scientific revolution, stop any such claim. Her argument is that the particular European conjunction, a competitive print market joined to a community that wanted replicable results, gave natural philosophy a communications system manuscripts could not provide. Readers who want a social history of one city’s book trade will find her thin. Readers who want to know why a diagram matters as much as a theorem will find the reason she wrote the book.

Limits

The criticisms gathered quickly and have become part of the subject. Adrian Johns, in The Nature of the Book, argued that early print was far less fixed and trustworthy than Eisenstein’s print culture implies. Editions varied, piracy was normal, and trust in a printed claim had to be built by institutions, the Stationers’ Company, the Royal Society, and the credit of particular shops, rather than by the press as such. Other historians pointed out that manuscripts remained central for centuries, that print multiplied error as well as truth, and that Eisenstein’s synthesis depends on secondary accounts of many different places. She answered that variation inside the printed world does not restore the scribal world, and that a shift of regime can be real even if the new regime was locally messy.

Both sides are now usable. Eisenstein is right that the press changed the scale and the durability of texts in a way that matters for the Reformation and for technical scholarship. Johns is right that “print culture” is too smooth a name for a trade full of unreliable books, and that readers had to learn which books to trust. A careful reading of Eisenstein takes her comparative claims and declines the impression, which her most enthusiastic pages can give, that the press was a single European mind.

Why it matters here

Two ideas in this reading meet in her book. One is that nations are imagined communities built with the help of print. Anderson’s printers, selling vernaculars to a growing public, need a press that can standardise a language and distribute a newspaper. Eisenstein is the account of how that kind of object became possible, and of what else it did along the way. The other idea is the press as an agent of change in its own right: standardisation, preservation, dissemination, and the consequences for reform and science. Jeremiah Dittmar later asked whether cities that adopted the press grew faster, which is a question her volumes pose and do not measure. Johns asked whether her agent was as reliable as she thought. Here, Eisenstein is the argument both neighbours depend on. Without a specified difference between copying and printing, the nation of readers and the cumulative sciences are just two more stories about ideas, and the press sinks back into the scenery she tried to pull it out of.
