Elizabeth Eisenstein asks what changed when a text stopped being a copy and became an edition. Her sharpest case is the early Reformation. She quotes the historian A. G. Dickens: between 1517 and 1520, Luther's thirty publications probably sold well over 300,000 copies. Lutheranism, Dickens wrote, and Eisenstein builds on the point, was from the start the child of the printed book. The Lollard and Waldensian heresies had moved by manuscript and by word of mouth, and authorities could hunt them down copy by copy. A German sermon set in type could be in many towns before anyone in authority had decided to ban it. Catholic printers used the same shops. The difference was the scale on which a religious argument could stay public.

She makes a second case out of scholarship that needed the page to match. Copernicus, she argues, was not a great observer. What print gave him was a shelf of records and reference books no earlier astronomer could have assembled in one place, and a way to put his own book, De revolutionibus of 1543, into other people's hands as the same text. A diagram, a star table, or a passage of Greek could be checked because the other reader had the same edition, not a cousin of it. Errors still got printed, and a printed error traveled farther than a scribe's slip. Her claim is comparative. Beside the manuscript, the edition was stable enough to argue over.

That is the question worth asking when someone says an idea "spread." Spread by what, in how many matching copies, and could the copies be called back? A movement that exists in three hundred thousand pamphlets is a different political fact from a movement that exists in a chest of sermons. The same test applies to a scientific claim. If the diagram is not the same diagram, the dispute is not yet about the thing in the picture.

Adrian Johns argued in 1998 that early print was a poor instrument of that stability. In London, a sheet could be corrected in the middle of a run, so two copies of one edition were not the same book, and pirated editions were an ordinary line of business. The quarrel is real. It limits any claim that type itself made a text trustworthy. It leaves the sales figure, and the comparison with earlier heresies, standing.
