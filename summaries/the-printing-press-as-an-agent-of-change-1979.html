Elizabeth Eisenstein's measure of the new press is a sales figure from the Reformation, not a story about a single machine in Mainz. Between 1517 and 1520, an estimate she quotes says, Luther's thirty publications probably sold well over 300,000 copies. Older dissent had no such channel. Wycliffe's Lollards and the Waldensians had moved by manuscript, sermon, and memory, and the authorities could hope to wear a heresy down by seizing the copies. Lutheranism, in the sentence she takes from that scholarship, was from the first the child of the printed book. A pamphlet set in type could be the same German sentences in Wittenberg, Nuremberg, and Basel, with the cartoon and the pamphlet selling alongside the treatise. She calls printing an unacknowledged revolution: everyone agreed it mattered, and historians still treated it as background.

The other half of her case is what happened to a text after the first edition. A manuscript lived in a few copies, and each scribe drifted a little. A printed edition lived in hundreds of copies of one setting of type. An error could be multiplied, and she says ignorant printers did multiply errors, and pirate editions multiplied them again. The same setting of type also meant that a correction, once made, could be kept, and that readers far apart could argue about the same page. She thinks that preservative power, which she distinguishes from mere sameness, is what let scholarship, law, and natural philosophy accumulate instead of starting over with every copy. Dissemination, standardization, and this fixity are the three properties she asks other historians to put back into the causes of the Reformation and of early modern science.

The practical consequence is that a public argument can have a shared object. People who will never meet can hold the same sentences and disagree about them. That is new relative to a world in which the authoritative copy is in one monastery and the others are approximations. It is also easy to overread. Eisenstein's own pages say that printing a text does not make it true. It makes a particular wording common, and it makes the next correction harder to lose.

Adrian Johns, in The Nature of the Book (1998), argued that fixity was not a property the machine delivered. In London, piracy was ordinary, editions of the same title differed, and a reader asked who had printed the sheets. His sharp case is the star catalogue of 1712. Edmond Halley, with Isaac Newton's authority, published John Flamsteed's observations before Flamsteed would accept them. Four hundred copies were printed. Flamsteed later got three hundred, kept the sheets he recognized as his, and burned the rest, calling the fire a sacrifice to truth. In the American Historical Review of February 2002 Eisenstein replied that Johns was comparing the handpress with later steam printing, while she was comparing it with copying by hand, and that she had never claimed the press made a text accurate by itself.
