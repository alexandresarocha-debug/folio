Kenneth Pomeranz's comparison is not Europe against China. It is Britain against the lower Yangzi delta, one of the richest farming regions in the eighteenth-century world. On the measures he assembles, living standards, markets, and farm productivity in the two places were close enough that a deep European superiority will not explain what happened next. The break, the "great divergence," comes around 1800. Britain got out of a shared ecological trap. The Yangzi did not. The two advantages he puts at the center were accidents of location: coal that sat near Britain's workshops, and the land of the Americas.

He tries to measure the American part in acres. Cotton, sugar, and timber imported into Britain stood in, on his estimate, for about 25 to 30 million acres of land that British farmers did not have to plant. He calls that overseas land ghost acreage. Sugar supplied calories. Cotton supplied fiber for the mills. Timber supplied wood that Britain had already cut thin. The fields that produced them were worked in large part by enslaved people, and the trade worked because American populations had already been shattered by Old World disease. Chinese settlers moving to their own frontiers did not play this role for the Yangzi. They became independent farmers, and their local crafts soon competed with the delta instead of feeding it a captive export market.

Coal is the other half, and it is local. Britain's industrial districts could burn coal instead of wood, first for heat and then for steam, because the seams were close. China's coal was real and large, and it was far from the lower Yangzi, too far to do the same job at the same cost. A region that is running out of forest and out of new farmland, and that cannot call on a buried fuel supply or a continent of ghost acres, hits a ceiling. A region that can call on both does not. Pomeranz's point is that the ceiling, not a permanent European talent for markets or machines, is what the eighteenth century was pressing against.

The claim that the two ends of Eurasia were still alike on the eve of industrialization has been hit hard with wage evidence. Robert C. Allen's 2009 reconstruction of prices argues that British labor was already expensive relative to capital, which is why a machine such as the spinning jenny paid in England around 1780 and did not pay in India. If British workers were already much better paid than workers in the Yangzi or in India, the economies were not interchangeable, and the divergence was underway before the ghost acres and the steam engine could be asked to explain it. Pomeranz's acreage arithmetic can still be right about the constraints on British land. It has a harder time if the similarity of living standards, the premise the arithmetic is meant to rescue, does not hold.
