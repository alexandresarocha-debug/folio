Compare the advanced regions, not the continents

Kenneth Pomeranz's The Great Divergence, published in 2000, asks why industrial growth began in Britain and not in the most advanced parts of China. The question had a standard answer of long standing. Europe, the answer ran, had been pulling ahead for centuries, because of superior institutions, superior science, a more commercial culture, or a more rational family system, and the industrial revolution was the moment when a deep advantage became obvious. Pomeranz argues that this answer compares the wrong units and misreads the timing. If one sets England beside the Yangzi Delta, rather than a vague Europe beside a vague China, the two societies in the middle of the eighteenth century look strikingly alike in the things that are supposed to mark a deep lead. Life expectancy, consumption, the intensity of markets, and the technical skill of farmers and artisans do not, on his reading of the evidence, put England in a different world from Jiangnan. The opening claim is that the great divergence was late, sudden, and contingent. It was not the unveiling of a European superiority that had been centuries in the making.

The book became the central text of what is called the California School of economic history, alongside the work of R. Bin Wong and others who had been arguing that Chinese institutions were more effective, and more market-like, than the older European story allowed. Pomeranz's particular contribution is ecological. He thinks the resemblance between the two advanced regions includes a shared problem, and that Britain escaped the problem by accidents of geology and of empire.

Two organic economies at a limit

Before the mineral economy, advanced societies were organic. Food, fuel, fibre, and building material all came, ultimately, from the amount of land that photosynthesis could serve. Pomeranz describes England and the Yangzi Delta as two regions that had pushed an organic economy very far. Agriculture was intensive. Labour was skilled. Trade in grain, cloth, and timber moved resources from where they were abundant to where they were scarce. Both regions were running into the constraint that the land could not be asked for more of everything at once. More people meant more demand for food. More industry meant more demand for fuel and for fibre. Forest that was cut for fuel was forest that was not available for timber or for the ecological services that kept soils and water in order. The usual European story, in which a uniquely efficient market or a uniquely inventive culture broke through this constraint, is, he thinks, unnecessary, because the Yangzi had efficient markets and a great deal of practical ingenuity and was facing the same kind of ceiling.

The comparison is the methodological heart of the book and the part that later scholars have worked hardest to test. Pomeranz is not saying that every province of China resembled every county of England. He is saying that the right test of a theory of European uniqueness is the comparison of the cores. A theory that explains why Lisbon was richer than a poor upland in the Chinese interior has not explained the industrial revolution. A theory that explains why Lancashire, rather than the Yangzi, became the first centre of coal-and-cotton industry has at least aimed at the right target. He assembles evidence on diet, on the spread of goods, on wages where they can be compared, and on the organisation of textile production to argue that the cores were in the same broad band of development. The argument is quantitative in ambition and, given the sources, often indirect. It is a brief for a late divergence, not a claim that the two regions were identical in every institution.

Coal and the ghost acres of the Americas

Britain's escape, in Pomeranz's account, had two parts, and neither of them was a moral quality. The first was coal. Britain had large deposits, and it had them near the places where industry and cities needed heat. Coal substituted for wood. It freed land that would otherwise have been devoted to fuel, and it made a mineral energy regime possible just as the organic ceiling was becoming tight. China also had coal. The deposits were far from the Yangzi Delta, and the political and transport conditions that would have made a Jiangnan coal economy natural were not the ones the empire presented. Location, not a European genius for burning rocks, is the variable. The second part was the Americas. Colonies supplied Britain with land-intensive products, sugar, cotton, timber, and other goods, that would have required a vast additional acreage at home. Pomeranz calls this the advantage of ghost acres. The land was not in England. The products arrived as if it were. A crucial share of that land was worked by enslaved people. The divergence, on this telling, is inseparable from coercion. An economic miracle that forgets the plantation has misunderstood where the acres came from.

The two escapes work together. Coal relieved the energy constraint. American land relieved the fibre and food constraint, and American silver and American demand shaped the trading system in which British industry grew. Pomeranz does not argue that Chinese science was incapable of industry, or that Chinese merchants were uninterested in profit. He argues that a region without conveniently located coal and without a captive transoceanic periphery did not get the same relief from the organic limit, and that the relief, not a prior cultural lead, is what opened the gap. The contingency is the point he wants to leave in the reader's hands. A world in which British coal had been located differently, or in which the New World had not been available on those terms, is a world in which the late eighteenth century need not have belonged to Lancashire.

What he keeps, and what he discards, from the older story

The conclusion is a reallocation of credit. European institutions and European science were not nothing. Pomeranz's claim is that they were not sufficient, and that they were not a unique endowment of a whole continent already far in front by 1700. Markets, property, and technical curiosity existed on both sides of the comparison and cannot by themselves answer the question he posed. Empire and energy can. The industrial revolution remains a European event in the narrow sense that it happened first in Britain. It ceases to be evidence for a long European exceptionalism running back through the Middle Ages. The book asks historians to stop using the nineteenth century as a searchlight that makes every earlier European institution look like a cause and every earlier Chinese institution look like a blockage.

The evidence is drawn from Chinese and European economic history, from studies of consumption, agriculture, demography, and technology, and from the record of colonial trade. Pomeranz is explicit about the difficulty of comparing prices and wages across such different sources, and he builds his case so that it does not depend on a single fragile number. The case does depend on the judgement that the similarities around the middle of the eighteenth century are more important than the differences which other historians put first. That judgement is the one the subsequent debate has contested.

The debate the book started

Quantitative work after 2000 has not left the late-divergence claim alone. Robert Allen's reconstruction of wages and prices, and the work of Stephen Broadberry and others on income, have been read as evidence that north-western Europe, and Britain in particular, had pulled ahead of the Yangzi in living standards well before the classic industrial revolution. If the wage gap was already large, Pomeranz's picture of two cores in the same band has to be revised, and an explanation that starts earlier than coal and colonies gains force. Allen's own explanation still gives coal a central role, and adds high wages as the reason British inventors spent effort on labour-saving machines. Joel Mokyr's explanation gives the decisive role to a culture of useful knowledge. Both are answers to Pomeranz even when they keep parts of his stage. The location of coal has proved more durable than the claim of full parity in 1750. Critics of the ghost-acre argument have asked whether American land was large enough, early enough, to be the binding constraint that broke, and whether Chinese regions had substitutes that the comparison understates.

There is a political edge to the reception that is worth stating plainly. A theory which says that Europe's lead was late and ecological, and that slavery and colonies were part of the escape, is an argument against a certain kind of civilisational self-congratulation. A theory which says that the lead was early and institutional is an argument for the long-run importance of European political and legal arrangements. The evidence can be discussed without choosing a side in advance. Pomeranz's achievement was to force the discussion onto comparative ground, with China as a peer case rather than as a foil. Even the historians who date the divergence earlier are, to that extent, working inside a question he reset.

Why it matters here

The card pomeranz-divergence is this book. The comparison of England with the Yangzi Delta, the claim of broad similarity around the middle of the eighteenth century, the shared ecological constraint of an organic economy, and the two escapes, nearby coal and the ghost acres of the Americas worked in part by enslaved labour, are the card's contents. The card records the California School and the later quantitative criticism from Allen, Broadberry, and others, which suggests that north-western Europe had pulled ahead earlier than Pomeranz thought, while his emphasis on energy and land has lasted. Folio uses The Great Divergence as the statement of the late, contingent, ecological account of why industrialisation began where it did. The companion books on the card, Allen's and Mokyr's, are intelligible as arguments with this one. Coal, colonies, wages, and useful knowledge are the candidate causes, and Pomeranz is the reason they are candidates in a comparison rather than in a European monologue.
