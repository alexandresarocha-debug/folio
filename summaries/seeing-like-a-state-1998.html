On 21 November 1849 the Spanish governor of the Philippines, Narciso Clavería y Zaldúa, ordered his subjects to take permanent surnames. Filipinos, his decree said, generally had no family name that would distinguish them by families. They drew baptismal names from a small stock of saints, and the result, for a tax list, was confusion. The remedy was a catalog, the Catálogo alfabético de apellidos, mixing personal names with words taken from plants, animals, minerals, and places. Each locality was given a block of pages, distributed by letter. Whole towns came out with surnames beginning with the same initial. In the Bikol region the alphabet is still laid across the map. The provincial capital starts at A, the coast runs on through the next letters, and the island of Catanduanes finishes the set. Clavería wanted an eight-column register: tribute, labor service, given name, surname, age, marital status, occupation, exemptions. A ninth column would record changes, to be inspected every month. The preamble is frank. The list would secure taxes, personal services, and payment for exemptions, and it would make unauthorized movement harder.

James C. Scott's Seeing Like a State (1998) treats that catalog as a small, complete case of a much larger habit. A state cannot act on a population it cannot see. Seeing, for officials, means a grid: one name per family, one measure for the field, one number for the trees. Anything that will not fit the grid is, from the office, illegible, and therefore as good as absent. The surname was not a record of what families already called themselves. It was a device that made families into rows.

His modern case is a city built to be seen from above. Brasília was inaugurated on 21 April 1960, planned by Lúcio Costa and Oscar Niemeyer on an empty site on the central plateau, about 1,000 kilometers from Rio de Janeiro, on land the state already owned. Housing, work, and traffic were separated. Apartment blocks were grouped into superquadras, each meant to hold roughly 1,500 to 2,500 people with its own school. The plan had no place for the workers who built it, the candangos, except as a labor force that would leave. They stayed, and they organized. By 1980, Scott reports, 75 percent of the population of Brasília lived in settlements the plan had never intended, and the planned city held less than half of its projected 557,000. The order at the center depended on an unplanned city at the edge. The two were not a success and a mistake. They were one arrangement.

The lesson, when an official figure looks beautifully clear, is to ask what had to be made uniform before anyone could write it down, and who lived in the part that would not fit. A name list can stop a clerk from losing you. It can also be the instrument that finds you for a tax or a labor draft. Scott's disasters share a further step. The plan is treated as the whole of the reality, and force is then used to make the place match the paper.
