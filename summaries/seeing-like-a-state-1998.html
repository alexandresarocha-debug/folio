Legibility as the state’s first problem

James C. Scott’s Seeing Like a State, published in 1998, is a book about a particular way of looking. States, he argues, are chronically half-blind. A population of local practices, local measures, local names, and local ecologies is a population a state cannot tax, conscript, vaccinate, or reform, because the state cannot see it in the form its offices require. The great projects of modern statecraft are projects of legibility. They translate a thick, local world into a thin, standard grid: permanent surnames so that people can be found again, cadastral maps so that land can be assessed, standardised weights so that a market can be policed, a simplified language of administration, a census, a street address. Scott does not think this impulse is always malign. A state that cannot see famine cannot relieve it. His subject is the pathology that follows when the grid is mistaken for the world, and when officials acquire the power to make the world resemble the grid.

He calls the ideology that licenses this mistake high modernism. It is a strong, even muscle-bound, confidence in scientific and technical progress, in the mastery of nature, and in the rational design of social order, held especially by planners, engineers, and revolutionary parties in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. High modernism is not the same thing as science, which is full of doubt, and not the same thing as bureaucracy, which is often cautious. It is the belief that an intelligent centre, equipped with a map, can redesign a forest, a city, or a countryside from first principles, and that the people who already live there are raw material. The book’s thesis is that the great disasters of social engineering combine four elements: an administrative drive to make society legible, a high-modernist ideology, an authoritarian state willing to use coercion, and a civil society too weak to resist. Remove the last two and the first two produce follies. Combine all four and they produce catastrophe.

Forests, maps, and names

The opening case is German scientific forestry, and it is his clearest parable because the trees could not talk back and the results could be counted. Early modern states wanted timber and revenue, and a diverse forest is hard to value. Forestry science replaced the mixture with even-aged stands of a single commercially useful species, planted in rows, stripped of the undergrowth that made measurement difficult. The first generation often paid handsomely. The grid was more productive, in the one dimension the state measured, than the old wood. The following generations paid the ecological bill: poorer soils, pests, windthrow, a forest that had lost the many small exchanges, of fungi, insects, leaf litter, and irregular shelter, that the simplified model had defined as noise. The later invention of a more cautious, restoration-minded forestry is, for Scott, the state quietly admitting that the legible forest was missing most of the forest. He then generalises. A cadastral map does to customary tenure what the planting plan did to the wood. It creates a single owner, a single boundary, and a single taxable parcel, and it abolishes the overlapping use-rights, the gleaning, the seasonal access, on which people poorer than the new owner had depended. The map is accurate about what it records and imperial about what it erases.

The same logic, applied to people, gives him the history of permanent surnames, of standardised patronyms, and of the state’s impatience with populations that name themselves in ways a clerk cannot file. Applied to cities, it gives him the grid plan as an instrument of policing and of taxation, useful precisely because a stranger with a map can find a door. Scott’s point in these early chapters is not that naming and mapping are sins. It is that each of them substitutes a state-centred optic for a local one, and that the substitution has costs which the optic is structurally unable to see. The official looks at the grid and sees order. The resident looks at the grid and sees the disappearance of a practice that had no column in the ledger.

High-modernist cities and the countryside

The middle of the book turns from the tools of legibility to the schemes in which high modernism tried to rebuild life to match a drawing. Brasília is his urban case. A capital raised in the interior to a design of monumental clarity, with functions separated, traffic sorted, and the view from the air treated as the relevant view, Brasília is legible in the extreme. It is also, in the experience of the people who staff it, a city that had to grow an unplanned twin, the settlements where life that the plan had not provided for could actually be lived. Scott reads this through Jane Jacobs, whose defence of dense, mixed, slightly chaotic streets is the antithesis of the high-modernist plan. Jacobs’s street works because strangers negotiate an order that no planner could specify in advance. The planned city works on paper because it has abolished the conditions under which that negotiation happens. Le Corbusier’s drawings of towers and highways are the ideology’s purest aesthetic. They are a view from an office in the sky.

The rural cases are where the four conditions of catastrophe are met. Soviet collectivisation imposed a legible, centrally commanded agriculture on a countryside of peasant practices the centre regarded as backward. The combination of a simplified model, a state willing to starve and shoot, and a peasantry whose own institutions had been broken produced famine and a long agricultural wound. Tanzanian villagisation, ujamaa in its coercive phase, gathered scattered cultivators into planned villages so that services could be delivered and production supervised. The services were the humane justification. The effect, when people were moved away from the fields, the soils, and the arrangements they knew, was a failure of production that the plan’s elegance could not detect until it was obvious. Scott’s reading of these episodes is not that planning is always cruel. It is that a plan which replaces local knowledge, rather than consulting it, will be wrong in detail across a landscape too various to be captured by the plan’s categories, and that an authoritarian state will impose the wrongness rather than learn.

Metis, and the knowledge that does not fit the file

The conceptual name he gives to what the plans omit is metis, a Greek word for cunning, knack, and practical judgement acquired by doing. Metis is the knowledge of when to plant in this field rather than in the field the manual describes, of how a particular neighbourhood actually polices itself, of how a machine in a real workshop differs from the machine in the diagram. It is contextual, and it resists being written down completely, because the next situation will differ in ways a rule cannot anticipate. Scott contrasts it with the formal, deductive knowledge that high modernism respects. His claim is not the sentimental one that locals are always right. Local knowledge includes prejudice, domination, and error. His claim is that any scheme which cannot incorporate metis will be brittle, and that the schemes of authoritarian high modernism were built precisely on the decision to ignore it. The implausibility of a fully specified social order is, for him, a fact about the world, not a taste for the picturesque.

This is also the basis of his politics in the book, which is anti-authoritarian rather than anti-state. He says, more than once, that a democratic state with a strong civil society can use the tools of legibility, maps, statistics, public health, without being captured by the fantasy of total design, because people can refuse, sue, demonstrate, and make the plan expensive to enforce. The disasters he studies were not produced by mapping. They were produced by mapping plus unchecked power plus contempt for the practical knowledge of the people being mapped. A reader who takes the book as an attack on all coordination has misread the conditions in the thesis. A reader who takes it as a licence for nostalgia has also misread it. Scott does not want the return of the illegible old regime. He wants planners who have been frightened, appropriately, by the body count of planners who were not.

What the later book adds, and what critics have said

Against the Grain, nearly twenty years later, is the deep-historical companion. The legibility Scott finds in forestry and in the census he finds, at the origin of the state, in the preference for grain over roots and in the obsession with fixing people to taxable land. Seeing Like a State is the modern history of that preference once it acquired scientific rhetoric and industrial means. Critics of the 1998 book have said that the case studies are too neatly chosen, that some large plans, vaccination, urban sanitation, the coordination of railways, are successes of exactly the centralising optic he distrusts, and that local knowledge is itself often the knowledge of local bosses. These points limit the sermon. They do not remove the diagnostic tool. When a project is failing because its categories do not match the ground, Scott supplies the vocabulary for saying so, and the historical warnings against pushing on anyway.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on Against the Grain points to Seeing Like a State as the classic companion: modern states making societies legible through surnames, maps, measures, and planned cities, and high-modernist schemes, Soviet collectivisation and Brasília among them, failing when they ignore local knowledge. That is the book’s own list, and it belongs on the card because the early-state argument is much easier to see once the modern examples have made legibility concrete. Grain is to the first tax collectors what the cadastral map is to the nineteenth-century treasury and what the planned village is to the twentieth-century development ministry: a simplification that makes extraction and improvement imaginable, and that breaks down when the world will not sit still inside the simplification.
