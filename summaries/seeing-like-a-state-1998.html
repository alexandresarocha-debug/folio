From about 1765 to 1800, foresters in Prussia and Saxony rebuilt the woods as a single crop. Norway spruce, valued because it was hardy and fast, was planted in rows of one age, and the underbrush, the deadfalls, and the standing dead trees were cleared so that the timber could be counted. James C. Scott, in Seeing Like a State (1998), takes this as the model of how a state makes a living world legible. The first rotation grew exceptionally well. It was living off the soil the old mixed forest had left behind. The loss showed up in the second and third generations. The forestry literature he cites puts it at one or two site classes, a production loss of 20 to 30 percent. The fungi, the birds, the insects, and the litter that had kept the soil going had been outside the account. Same-age spruce was also a standing invitation to the pests that specialize in spruce. German forestry had invented a discipline precise enough to teach. The precision depended on ignoring everything except the yield of one species.

The same habit, applied to people, is the forced villagization in Tanzania from 1973 to 1976. Julius Nyerere's government set out to settle most of the rural population in villages whose layout, housing, and local economy were drawn, in whole or in part, by officials at the center. Scott calls it the largest forced resettlement in independent Africa up to that time: at least five million Tanzanians were moved. At independence, an estimated eleven out of twelve million rural people had lived scattered, much of their farming and their local trade outside the easy reach of a tax collector. The new villages were meant to make that population countable, serviceable, and productive. Cultivators who knew their own soils resisted the grids. Projects designed from general figures for tractor hours and fertilizer, sometimes after a few weeks of reconnaissance from the air, failed on clay that compacted, on irregular rain, and on crop diseases the plan had not named.

Scott's name for the knowledge the plans leave out is metis, the practical sense of a particular place, learned by doing and hard to write into a manual. A map that an office can read is a map that can tax, plant, and resettle. It is also a map that can miss the reason the old arrangement worked. The disasters he stacks up share four conditions: a simplified administrative order, a high-modernist faith that a rational plan can replace the past, an authoritarian state willing to impose it, and a civil society too weak to answer back. Where the people on the ground can refuse the grid, the same urge to simplify does less harm. He is explicit that the German foresters themselves went on to invent remedies once the second rotation failed. The remedy was available. The politics of who may impose the first plan is the variable.
