German state foresters of the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries needed timber they could count. A mixed wood defeated the count. Scientific forestry cleared the underbrush, cut the number of species, and planted Norway spruce or Scotch pine in straight rows of one age. James C. Scott's Seeing Like a State (1998) uses that plantation as his plain case of what he calls legibility. About three-quarters of the older, diverse forest, he writes, had been broadleaf. The new stands were conifer, often a single species. The first crop, which might take eighty years to mature, paid in uniform timber. Foresters he cites then watched the second and third generations of pure spruce fall off. They put the loss at one or two grades of site quality, about 20 to 30 percent of production. Soil, insects, and windthrow had been living in the part of the wood the grid had classed as noise. The worst cases entered German as Waldsterben, forest death.

Brasília is the same way of seeing, applied to a capital. The city was inaugurated in 1960 on a plan by Lúcio Costa, with the monumental buildings of Oscar Niemeyer. Housing, work, and traffic were separated so the order would be obvious from the air. Scott follows James Holston's study of the built city. The drawing had no place for much of the life that actually arrived. The workers who raised the capital, and many of the people who then staffed it, lived in settlements the pilot plan had not drawn. The daily city grew in the gap between the picture and the residents.

A grid is a tool. A surname, a land survey, or a planting row lets an office find people, assess fields, and harvest timber. A state that cannot see a famine cannot send relief. The harm begins when officials take the simplified picture for the whole forest or the whole city, and have the power to force the ground into line. The spruce that is easy to measure can exhaust the soil the measurement ignored. A capital that looks finished from an airplane can still have nowhere for its builders to sleep. Scott's name for the confidence behind these schemes is high modernism: a center with a map, sure it can redesign a landscape from first principles. The cases where that confidence was joined to an authoritarian state are the ones that ran up a body count. A plan has to survive contact with one particular hillside, and one particular street.
