In 2005 Shane Frederick published a three-question test built so that a tempting wrong answer arrives before the right one. A bat and a ball cost $1.10, and the bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. The ball costs 5 cents, not 10. If five machines take five minutes to make five widgets, 100 machines take five minutes to make 100 widgets, not 100 minutes. A lily pad that doubles every day and covers a lake on day 48 covers half the lake on day 47, not day 24.

He gave the three items to 3,428 people in 35 studies, most of them paid $8 for a 45-minute questionnaire, starting in January 2003. The average score was 1.24 out of 3. A third of the people, 33 percent, got nothing right, and 17 percent got all three. At MIT, 61 students averaged 2.18, and 48 percent were perfect. At the University of Toledo, 138 students averaged 0.57, and 64 percent scored zero. One sample was not a classroom at all: 195 people picnicking by the Charles River before the July 4 fireworks, ages 15 to 63, averaged 1.53.

The score tracked choices that had nothing to do with bats or lily pads. Asked to take $3,400 this month or $3,800 next month, 35 percent of the low scorers waited, against 60 percent of the people who had solved all three. Asked to take $100 for sure or a 75 percent chance of $200, a bet whose average payoff is $150, 19 percent of 843 low scorers took the bet, against 38 percent of 475 high scorers. On a mirror-image loss, a sure loss of $100 or a 75 percent chance of losing $200, the low scorers were the ones who gambled: 54 percent of 339, against 31 percent of 141 high scorers.

In a store or a benefits form, the first number that feels right is often the one to refuse. "Pay nothing until next year," "the payment is only $100," and "the chance of a problem is tiny" all invite a fast yes. Frederick's result is that the people who catch the trick on a toy puzzle are also more willing to wait a month for a better sum, and more willing to take a risk when the arithmetic is actually on their side. The people who miss the puzzle grab the sure gain and throw the dice on the sure loss.
