A test for the moment of override

Shane Frederick’s paper “Cognitive Reflection and Decision Making”, published in the Journal of Economic Perspectives in 2005, introduced a three-item test that has since become one of the most widely used measures in the study of judgement. He called it the Cognitive Reflection Test. Its purpose is narrow and well chosen. Frederick wanted a way to capture, quickly, the difference between people who report the first answer that comes to mind and people who stop, notice that the first answer is wrong, and compute. The ability or disposition to do that stopping is what he means by cognitive reflection. It is related to intelligence and not identical with it. A person can have the raw ability to carry out the arithmetic and still not think to question the fluent answer. The test is aimed at that gap, the gap Kahneman would later describe as System 2’s failure to audit System 1.

The paper is also an argument about decision making. Frederick’s claim is that this small difference in cognitive habit shows up in the choices economists care about: whether people prefer a smaller reward now or a larger one later, and how they treat risky gambles. If reflection is more than a party trick with riddles, high scorers and low scorers should part company on patience and on risk in predictable directions. The paper reports that they do, in a large set of convenience samples, mostly students, collected across many separate studies. It is an exploratory paper that happened to introduce a tool the field could not stop using. Both the tool and the caution about how it was validated belong in any serious account.

The three items

Each item is built so that an intuitive wrong answer arrives before the right one, and so that the right one is obvious once it is pointed out. The first is the bat and ball. A bat and a ball cost $1.10 in total. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost? The fluent answer is ten cents. It is wrong, because a ten-cent ball and a bat costing a dollar more would total $1.20. The ball costs five cents, and the bat costs $1.05. Frederick notes that people who do not say ten cents almost all say five cents. Catching the error and solving the problem are, for this item, nearly the same act. The second item concerns machines. If it takes five machines five minutes to make five widgets, how long would it take a hundred machines to make a hundred widgets? The fluent answer scales every number and says a hundred minutes. The correct answer is five minutes. Each machine makes a widget in five minutes, and a hundred machines working at once still take five minutes to make a hundred widgets. The third item is the lily pad. A patch doubles in size every day and covers the lake in forty-eight days. How long does it take to cover half the lake? The fluent answer halves the time and says twenty-four days. The correct answer is forty-seven days, because the day before the lake is covered it must have been half covered, given that the patch doubles.

Frederick gives several reasons to believe that the wrong answers are produced by a first impulse rather than by ignorance of arithmetic. The same wrong answers dominate: ten, a hundred, and twenty-four, rather than a scatter of guesses. People who get the items right often show, in crossings-out and in what they say, that they considered the wrong answer first. People who miss an item think the item was easier, for other people, than the people who solved it do, which fits a picture in which the missers never saw the trap. And when the same structure is posed in numbers that do not invite the shortcut, respondents do much better. The trap is the point of the test. A mathematics test that avoided fluent errors would measure something Frederick was not after.

Who answered what, and how he used the scores

The test was given to 3,428 respondents in thirty-five separate studies over a little more than two years, most of them undergraduates paid to complete a longer questionnaire. Frederick publishes the distribution of scores by location, and the differences across institutions are large, which is one of the paper’s striking descriptive results: the same three items that many students at the most selective universities answer correctly are missed, in the same fluent way, by large majorities elsewhere. He is not running a horse race between colleges for its own sake. He is showing that the test spreads people out. In the analyses that follow he typically compares those who scored zero with those who scored three, and reports that the people who scored one or two fall in between. The extremes make the pattern readable. They are not a claim that only the extremes matter.

On intertemporal choice, higher scores went with greater patience. People who reflected were more willing to wait for a larger later reward, across a set of hypothetical and, in some studies, real choices. Frederick’s interpretation is the one the test was built for. Impatience and the fluent wrong answer have a family resemblance. Both are failures to override an immediate pull, the pull of the present reward, the pull of the salient number. He does not claim that reflection is the whole of patience. He claims it is a measurable part of why people in the same room, facing the same question, answer differently. On risk, the pattern is more interesting than a simple story in which thinking makes people cautious. Higher scorers were more willing to take a gamble when the gamble had the better expected value, and they were less drawn to the risk-seeking response when the choice was among losses. In the language of prospect theory, they departed less from the choice that the numbers, rather than the framing, would recommend. Reflection did not turn them into expected-value machines. It shifted them towards the numbers.

What else the scores track

Frederick compares the test with other cognitive measures, including a short intelligence test taken by a subset of the respondents, and argues that reflection is kin to measured ability without being the same variable. The test correlates with those measures and still predicts choice once one has acknowledged the kinship. This is the paper’s most debated empirical claim, and later work has spent a decade asking how much of the test is numeracy, how much is general intelligence, and how much is the specific disposition to think twice. The honest reading of the 2005 paper is that Frederick showed a distinctive and convenient measure, and that he did not finish the job of separating it from intelligence. He also reports sex differences: men scored higher on the three items than women, on average, and he discusses how that difference sits alongside sex differences in patience and in risk. Later commentators have asked how much of the gap is the mathematical content of these particular items rather than reflection as a general trait. The question is a reason to be careful with the test, not a reason to invent a different history of what the paper found.

One further result is worth keeping because it says something about the psychology of error. People who gave the fluent wrong answer were more confident that other people would find the problem easy. The trap is invisible from inside. That invisibility is exactly why a short test of this kind can predict behaviour outside the test. The people who do not notice the trap in a riddle are, Frederick suggests, less likely to notice it in a contract, a gamble, or a tempting present reward. The suggestion is correlational. He did not train people to reflect and then watch their choices change. He showed that a stable difference in the habit of checking lines up with stable differences in choice.

Limits, including success

The limits are practical and conceptual. Three items is a very short test, and all three are numerical, so the measure confounds reflection with a particular kind of numeracy. The samples are mostly students, and the choices are mostly hypothetical, though not all of them. The largest limit arrived later, as a consequence of the paper’s success. The items became famous. They appear in newspapers, in interviews, and in other experiments. A test of the first answer that comes to mind is no longer the same test once a large fraction of respondents have already been told that the first answer is wrong. Subsequent researchers have written new items, and have asked respondents whether they have seen the old ones, precisely because the 2005 instrument has been contaminated by its own celebrity. There is also a conceptual limit that Evans and Stanovich make precise. A dual-process theory needs an account of when the override happens, and a three-item performance test is not that account. It is a useful individual-difference measure sitting on top of the theory. It does not show that there are two systems. It shows that people differ in whether they reject a fluent error, which any architecture, one process or two, will have to accommodate.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on two systems uses Frederick’s bat and ball as the illustration of a fast wrong answer surviving into a stated choice, and it lists this paper as the source of the Cognitive Reflection Test, a short measure of the slow, deliberative habit. The card is right to put the paper next to Kahneman’s book and next to Evans and Stanovich’s review. Kahneman supplied the characters and the claim that the slow character is lazy. Frederick supplied a way to count, in three questions, whether a particular person bothered to override the fast answer, and he showed that the count lines up with patience and with the shape of risky choice.
