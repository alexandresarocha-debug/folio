In 1977 the behaviour geneticists Robert Plomin, John DeFries and John Loehlin listed three ways genes and environments can be linked. Six years later Sandra Scarr and Kathleen McCartney, then at Yale, turned that list into a theory of how children grow up. Their claim was bold: genes do not just act alongside experience, they help steer which experiences a person gets.

The first kind is passive. Parents give a child both genes and a home. Their example is reading. Parents who read well and enjoy it tend to fill the house with books, so their children are more likely to become keen readers, for genetic and for home reasons at once. It can also run the other way. Skilled-reader parents with a child who struggles may give that child extra help with reading.

The second kind is evocative. A child's genetically influenced traits shape how other people respond. They suggest that smiley, active babies are likely to get more social contact than sober, passive ones, and that cooperative, attentive preschoolers get more pleasant teaching from adults.

The third kind is active: niche-picking. People seek out settings they find compatible and stimulating, in sports, study and relationships. Scarr and McCartney call this the most powerful link between people and their environments.

Their key prediction is about age. The passive kind matters most in infancy and fades as children spend more time outside the family. The active kind grows as children choose their own friends and pursuits. They used this to explain puzzling data. In adoption studies, unrelated children raised together had IQ correlations of 0.25 to 0.39 in early to middle childhood, but about zero by late adolescence. Non-identical twins correlated 0.60 to 0.75 for intelligence in infancy and early childhood, higher than genes alone predict, but around 0.55 at school age. Living together mattered less once each child could build their own world.

What this means in real life: a child's surroundings are not just handed to them. Over time, children increasingly pick and provoke their own.

Caution: this is a theory paper. It rests on studies from the 1970s and early 1980s, and its examples, like the smiley babies, are illustrations, not measurements. The authors also warn that passive effects make research on parenting in biological families uninterpretable, because parents pass on both genes and home. That makes the theory hard to test directly.

Source. Scarr, S., and McCartney, K. (1983). How people make their own environments: a theory of genotype → environment effects. Child Development, 54(2), 424-435. https://doi.org/10.2307/1129703
