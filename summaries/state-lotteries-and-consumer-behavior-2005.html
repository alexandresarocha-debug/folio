Lotteries are often defended as a painless way to raise money for states. Melissa Kearney, then at Wellesley College, asked two questions about what they do to the people who buy tickets. Do lotteries mainly replace other forms of gambling, or do they reduce spending on everything else? And do players respond to the expected return on a ticket, as careful buyers would, or do they seem misinformed about the risks and returns?

She used several sources of household-level data on gambling and spending, together with information on jackpots and games gathered from state lottery agencies and the Multi-State Lottery Association.

Her first finding was that lottery spending does not substitute for other gambling. Instead, household lottery gambling crowds out about $38 a month, or 2%, of other household consumption, with larger proportional reductions among low-income households.

Her second finding complicates the picture of players as simply dazzled by jackpots. Demand for lottery products responds positively to the expected value of the gamble, controlling for other features of the game. She concluded that lottery consumers are not simply uninformed, and may be making fully informed purchases, for example because they enjoy the game or the hope it offers.

What this means in real life: ticket money comes out of the household budget, not out of some separate fun fund, and it weighs most on households with the least.

Caution: the crowd-out estimate is an average from survey data and depends on the statistical design. Responding to expected value does not mean players know the odds well, only that sales rise when tickets become a better deal, as they do with big rollovers.
