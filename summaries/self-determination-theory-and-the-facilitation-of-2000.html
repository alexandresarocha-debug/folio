Richard Ryan and Edward Deci's 2000 paper is the short statement of self-determination theory, and the evidence it stands on is what people do after a reward ends. In a meta-analysis they published the year before, rewards that people had been told about in advance cut the time they later spent on an interesting activity once the reward was gone. The drop was d = -0.40 when the reward was for merely taking part, d = -0.36 when it was for finishing, and d = -0.28 when it was for doing well. Positive feedback moved the other way. It increased free-choice time, d = 0.33, and it increased reports of interest, d = 0.31.

The theory explains that split with three needs. Autonomy is the sense that you are the one doing the thing. Competence is the sense that you can do it and are getting better. Relatedness is the sense that you matter to the people around you. A promised payment or a gold star can crowd autonomy, because the activity becomes something done in order to get the prize. Praise that says the work was good can feed competence, which is why the same literature finds a gain from positive feedback. Relatedness is why a person will take on a dull task for someone they feel connected to, and why a bare order from a stranger is a weak way to get a value inside a person.

In a workplace, a classroom, or a clinic, the design question is which need the arrangement feeds. A bonus for hitting a number can get the number and still make the work feel like someone else's. A grade that only ranks students can do the same to a subject a child had liked. Room to choose a method, clear information about progress, and a person who explains the reason and listens are the conditions the theory says keep motivation inside the person. Ryan and Deci are explicit that this is a case for structure of a certain kind. Pressure alone, on their evidence, is the arrangement that shrinks later interest.

Source. Ryan, R. M., and Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. American Psychologist, 55(1), 68–78. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68
