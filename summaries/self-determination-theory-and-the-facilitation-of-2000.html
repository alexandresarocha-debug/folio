Three needs, and a claim about what motivation is for

Richard Ryan and Edward Deci published Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being in American Psychologist in 2000. It is the short statement of a theory they had been building since the 1970s, and Folio's card calls it the clearest brief introduction. The core claim is that people have three basic psychological needs. Autonomy is the sense that one's actions are self-endorsed, that one stands behind them. Competence is the sense of being effective, of having an effect on the task. Relatedness is the sense of connection with other people. When the three are supported, people tend to be more intrinsically motivated, more persistent, and higher in well-being. When they are thwarted, motivation shifts towards pressure, guilt, and the pursuit of rewards, and well-being suffers.

The paper is a review with a point of view. It organises a large literature, from classrooms to clinics to workplaces, around the needs, and it argues that social environments can be judged by whether they support or frustrate them. That is a different project from a catalogue of tricks for raising output. Ryan and Deci care about the quality of motivation, not only its amount. A person can be highly motivated in the sense of being driven, anxious, and dependent on approval, and the theory treats that state as a cost. A person can be motivated by interest in the activity itself, and the theory treats that state as the one that contexts should try to protect. The 2000 article is where this distinction is set out for a general scientific audience, with the evidence and the vocabulary in one place.

Intrinsic motivation and the forms of doing something for a reason

Intrinsic motivation, in their usage, is doing an activity for its inherent satisfaction. The contrast is extrinsic motivation, doing it for a separable consequence. The paper's important move is to refuse a single extrinsic bin. A student who studies from fear of shame, a student who studies because the subject has come to matter personally, and a student who studies for a prize are all, in a crude classification, externally moved. Self-determination theory ranks these by how fully the reason has been taken in. Pressure and guilt sit far from the self. A reason one endorses as one's own sits closer, even when the activity is not fun. The practical upshot is that teachers, clinicians, and managers have more options than a choice between play and bribery. They can support the taking-in of a reason, by offering a rationale, by acknowledging reluctance, and by leaving room for choice, and they can expect persistence and well-being to differ according to how far that taking-in has gone.

Autonomy, on this account, is self-endorsement. Dependence on other people can sit inside it, and so can a structure the person has accepted. A patient who understands a treatment and agrees to it can be acting autonomously inside a strong recommendation. A child who chooses a book inside a lesson the class has accepted can be acting autonomously inside a structure. The theory's critics sometimes hear autonomy as individualism of a particularly American kind. Ryan and Deci spend part of the article on the claim that the need is broad, and that relatedness is a need of the same rank, so that connection sits beside self-endorsement rather than against it. The three needs are joint. A context that buys competence by humiliation, or autonomy by isolation, has not met the theory's standard.

Rewards, and the finding that made the theory famous

The early surprise in this tradition is the undermining effect. Tangible rewards for an activity people already enjoy can reduce their later intrinsic interest. The classic demonstration is Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's 1973 study: preschoolers who expected a certificate for drawing later drew less in free time than children who had not been promised a reward. The 2000 paper treats that effect as established enough to theorise, and it points to the 1999 meta-analysis by Deci, Richard Koestner and Ryan, covering 128 experiments, which found that expected, tangible, task-contingent rewards reliably undermined intrinsic motivation, while verbal praise tended to enhance it. The interpretation is need-based. An expected prize can shift the perceived cause of the drawing from interest to payment, which frustrates autonomy. Praise, given in a way that informs the person they are effective, can support competence. Rewards differ in the theory. Their meaning depends on what they tell the person about why they are acting and about whether they are any good.

The article is careful enough to live with a controversy that was already running. Some researchers, notably in the line of argument associated with Eisenberger and Cameron, held that the negative effects of rewards were limited and avoidable, and that the literature had overstated the threat to intrinsic interest. Ryan and Deci's stance in the 2000 paper is that the meta-analytic pattern is real for the kinds of rewards and the kinds of measures the reviews isolated, and that a blanket defence of incentive schemes does not survive that pattern. A reader of Folio's card should hold both the pattern and the existence of the dispute. Expected tangible rewards, contingent on doing the task, have a reliable undermining effect in the experiments the 1999 review assembled. That is a different claim from the claim that every bonus, wage, or word of thanks is poison.

Where the theory has been asked to work

The card lists the fields the theory has influenced: education, healthcare, sport, workplace management, and the design of video games. The 2000 paper is already making that spread part of its argument. A classroom that offers choice, a meaningful rationale, and feedback that informs rather than controls is a classroom supporting the needs, and the prediction is more interest and more persistence. A clinic that hears a patient's perspective and avoids a controlling style is making the same bet about adherence and well-being. A game that provides clear effects of action, a sense of choice, and a way of mattering to other players is, sometimes without having read the theory, building the three supports into play. Ryan and Deci treat these as one hypothesis seen in many institutions. The hypothesis can be fruitful and still be unevenly confirmed, which is the ordinary situation of a framework this wide.

Daniel Pink's popular book Drive later carried a simplified version of the theory to managers, and the card notes the simplification. Simplification is the fate of a 2000 review once it leaves the building. What tends to be kept is the warning about contingent pay and the praise of autonomy. What tends to be thinned is relatedness, the difference between kinds of extrinsic reason, and the condition that undermining shows up for interesting tasks under expected tangible rewards rather than for every form of compensation in every job. Reading the American Psychologist article is the remedy. It is short by the standard of the theory's later handbooks, and it is long enough to keep the three needs, the spectrum of reasons, and the reward evidence in a single frame. A manager who has only the slogan will design a different workplace from a manager who has read the frame.

Well-being is the outcome that stops the theory from being only a performance theory. Ryan and Deci argue that need support predicts not just persistence but a better quality of experience, less pressure, more vitality, a sense that the day is one's own. A workplace that raises output by threat can win on a quarterly metric and lose on the outcome the theory says is the point. That stance is normative as well as empirical, and the article does not hide the norm. Psychology, they imply, should be able to say that some motivational climates are better for people, and should be able to say it with evidence. The evidence they assemble is broad. It is also, like any review of its era, a review of studies of uneven size and flexibility. The replication crisis has made that kind of breadth a reason for caution as well as a reason for confidence. The caution belongs in the reading. It does not, by itself, pick a different trio of needs.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness, uses this article as its source and as the clearest short introduction. The three needs, the claim about intrinsic motivation and well-being, and the undermining effect of expected tangible rewards are the card's content. The 1999 meta-analysis is the quantitative backing for the reward claim, across 128 experiments, and the 1973 drawing study is the classic illustration. Read the 2000 paper to see how those pieces were meant to form one theory, including the point that extrinsic reasons themselves differ in how fully a person can endorse them. Read the meta-analysis and the preschool study when you want the reward effect at closer range, and remember that the effect was contested. The article is the map. The card is an invitation to use the map without collapsing it into a slogan about paying people less.
