Self-control as a life that rarely needs a fight

Brian Galla and Angela Duckworth published More than resisting temptation in 2015. The subtitle carries the result Folio's card relies on: beneficial habits mediate the relationship between self-control and positive life outcomes. People who score high on trait self-control tend to do better at school, at health, and in other domains the studies look at. The usual picture of that advantage is heroic resistance. The self-controlled person meets temptation and wins, again and again, by effort in the moment. Galla and Duckworth's picture is quieter. The advantage shows up as fewer temptations, and as habits that carry useful behaviour without a daily struggle. The mediation is the statistical form of that picture. The link from self-control to good outcomes runs, in substantial part, through the habits people have already built.

The paper sits downstream of Wendy Wood and David Neal's account of habits as context-response associations. If a repeated act in a stable setting comes to fire with little reference to one's current effort, then the person who looks disciplined may be the person whose kitchen, timetable, and route already contain the right responses. Willpower, on that view, is what remains when the environment and the habit have not been arranged. Folio's card states the finding in those terms. People high in trait self-control report fewer temptations, and their edge lies in habits and environments. This paper is the evidence the card is pointing at.

What has to be shown for the claim to hold

A mediation claim has a particular shape. Self-control should be associated with beneficial habits. Beneficial habits should be associated with the outcomes. And the direct path from self-control to the outcomes should shrink once the habits are taken into account, because the habits were carrying part of the association. Galla and Duckworth look for that shape across more than one study and more than one domain, including the kinds of repeated behaviour that structure a student's day and a person's health. The point of using several studies is that a single correlation between a questionnaire and a grade is too easy to over-read. A pattern that appears when the outcome is homework, and again when the outcome is sleep or exercise, is a pattern about a general feature of self-control rather than about one school subject.

The contrast with resistance is the contrast that makes the paper a revision rather than a footnote. If high self-control meant a superior ability to suffer through temptation, the self-controlled person should report as many temptations as anyone else, and a higher rate of winning against them. The reports go another way. The people who score as self-controlled describe days with fewer occasions on which a desire and a goal are in open conflict. They are doing the desired thing as a matter of course, in the place and at the time where it belongs, so the undesired thing is less often in the room. Resistance still happens. It is a poorer description of the average difference between high and low scorers than the description that starts with habit.

Diary methods are well suited to that distinction, because a questionnaire about one's general character cannot see a Tuesday. Asking people through the day what they want, what they do, and whether they are fighting an impulse produces a record of conflict rather than a self-portrait. Wood's earlier diary work had found that a large share of everyday behaviour is repeated in the same context while attention is elsewhere, the figure Folio's card gives as roughly 43 per cent. Galla and Duckworth use the same spirit of measurement to ask who has the beneficial versions of those repetitions. The self-controlled, on their evidence, are people for whom the automatic acts are the useful ones. Trait self-control starts to look like a history of arrangement.

Habits as the path, not the slogan

Calling a habit beneficial is a way of keeping the claim from praising automaticity in general. An automatic route to the cupboard is a habit, and it is not the habit that mediates good outcomes. The mediators in the paper are the repeated acts that line up with the goals the outcomes require: studying, sleeping, exercising, the behaviours a person would endorse if asked in a calm moment. Self-control predicts those habits. Those habits predict the outcomes. The theoretical gain is that self-control has been given a mechanism that does its work before the moment of temptation, which is why the moment of temptation is rarer. Wood and Neal had said that goals matter most while a habit is being formed, and that context matters most once it is performing. Galla and Duckworth can be read as the individual-difference version of that sequence. People who end up scoring as self-controlled are people who have completed the sequence for the behaviours that matter.

The mediation also disciplines a popular moral. If the benefits of self-control arrive through habits, then lectures about grit that leave the day unchanged are lectures about the wrong phase. The phase that needs help is the installation: a stable cue, a repeated response, friction removed from the useful act and added to the rival. Lally and colleagues' study of formation, with its median of sixty-six days and its very wide range, is a reminder that installation is slow and uneven. Galla and Duckworth do not time that curve. They show why the curve is worth climbing. A habit that eventually fires without a decision is an asset that keeps paying, and the people who hold more of those assets are the people the questionnaires call self-controlled.

There is a risk of circularity, and a fair reading names it. People who already do well may find it easier to build the habits, so the habits are a side effect of success rather than a path to it. Mediation models on observational data cannot fully close that loop. A student with a quiet place to work and a family that expects homework will find the study habit easier to install, and will also tend to score well for reasons that include the quiet place. The paper's claim is about the statistical path, not about an experiment that assigns habits and watches grades move. The claim is still informative. It says the path through habit is large enough to take seriously, and that the path through constant resistance is a weaker account of the same data.

What the paper leaves to the rest of the card

Wood and Neal's 2007 review is the theory this evidence needs. Without the distinction between direct cuing and goal-directed action, a habit is just a word for doing something often. With the distinction, the mediation has a mechanism. Context starts the response. The goal that formed the habit need not be consulted every time. Fewer temptations follow, because the rival response is not the one the context has learned. Galla and Duckworth are testing a prediction that falls out of that mechanism once it is applied to individual differences. Their paper is thinner on the animal evidence and on the philosophy of the interface, and it is thicker on the question a reader of the card actually asks: why do the people we call self-controlled seem to have easier lives?

Wood's later book, Good Habits, Bad Habits, is the place the answer is said for a general audience, with the diary proportion, the formation time, and the friction advice gathered in one argument. The 2015 paper is the tighter empirical step inside that argument, the step that says beneficial habits carry the relationship between a trait score and a life outcome. A reader who wants the advice can go to the book. A reader who wants the test of the advice's premise, that habit rather than struggle is the carrier, stays here. The premise can be true on average and still fail for a person whose environment will not hold a cue still, or whose goal is new enough that no association has formed. The paper describes a path. It does not guarantee that every life contains the materials to walk it.

Ego depletion, the idea that self-control is a tank that runs dry, is a useful contrast and a contrast to handle lightly. Baumeister's studies had people resist cookies and eat radishes, then persist less on an unsolvable puzzle. A large preregistered multilab test led by Hagger in 2016 did not recover that effect. Galla and Duckworth are not running that debate. Their result does, however, sit more comfortably with a world in which the main benefit of self-control is avoiding the fight than with a world in which the main benefit is having a larger tank for fights one cannot avoid. If the fights are rarer, a theory about the fuel for fights explains less of the trait's value. That is an implication to notice, and it is an implication the 2015 paper was free to leave untested.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Habits live in context, not in willpower, uses this paper to answer the obvious objection to a habit theory. If context does the work, what is left of self-control as a trait that predicts good lives? Galla and Duckworth's answer is that the trait predicts those lives largely because it marks people who have installed beneficial habits and who therefore meet fewer temptations. Resistance in the moment is real, and it is the smaller part of the advantage. Read the paper for that mediation. Read Wood and Neal for the interface that makes mediation intelligible, Lally and colleagues for how slowly and variably a new habit approaches automaticity, and Wood's book for the argument in everyday language. The card's advice about friction and cues is what you do if you believe this paper. You arrange the fight out of the day, rather than training harder for a fight the day keeps offering.
