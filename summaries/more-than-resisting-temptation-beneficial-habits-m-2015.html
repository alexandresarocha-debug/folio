Brian Galla and Angela Duckworth ran six studies, 2,274 people in all, on why a high score on self-control goes with better grades, steadier health habits, and more follow-through. The usual answer is that these people fight temptation harder in the moment. The studies point at a routine that has already started.

The longest test was Study 6. Nine hundred eighteen high school seniors in three public schools, about two-thirds of them eligible for free or reduced-price lunch, reported their self-control, their homework habits, and whether they finished homework. School records supplied the senior-year grade point average. The National Student Clearinghouse supplied college enrollment about a year later. Students with better self-control reported stronger homework habits, r = .29, and more homework completed, r = .28. Homework habits carried part of the path from self-control to senior-year grades and to staying enrolled in college, after the researchers accounted for the school, gender, lunch status, race and ethnicity, and a test of intelligence.

Study 1 asked the same question about health, with 500 adults recruited online in the United States. Habits for exercise, a healthy snack, and a regular bedtime and wake time carried the link between self-control and two reports: the behavior felt more automatic, and it took less effortful inhibition to do it. The other studies filled in studying while distracted and a meditation practice three months after a five-day retreat for teenagers. Across the set, the correlations among self-control, habit, and the outcomes mostly sat around .20 to .40. Habits were related to the outcomes. They were a separate measure from the outcomes.

The practical consequence is where to spend the effort. The moment of temptation is a bad moment to negotiate with yourself about homework, exercise, sleep, or a medication. A stable time and place, repeated until the sequence starts on its own, does the work that a fresh decision keeps dropping. Self-control still mattered in these studies. It mattered largely because it was tied to having the routine already in place.

Source. Galla, B. M., and Duckworth, A. L. (2015). More than resisting temptation: Beneficial habits mediate the relationship between self-control and positive life outcomes. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 109(3), 508–525. https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000026
