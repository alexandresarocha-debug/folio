In 717 an Arab army and fleet under Maslama ibn Abd al-Malik camped before Constantinople. Leo III held the city. The weapon that mattered at sea was Greek fire, a petroleum mixture pumped through siphons, which burned on the water. Judith Herrin gives it a chapter in Byzantium: The Surprising Life of a Medieval Empire (2007) because it is a specimen of her subject. A state that had already lost Syria, Egypt, and North Africa in the seventh-century Arab conquests still had a capital, a fleet, and a guarded formula. In 718 the siege failed. What remained was smaller and poorer. It was a working state, with another seven centuries in front of it.

Her method is one short chapter on one concrete thing, and the things are often domestic. A Byzantine bride in Venice ate with a small gold fork. The churchman Peter Damian treated the habit as vain and decadent. The story is easy to enjoy and easy to misread. Herrin's point is that the court which owned the fork also trained administrators, kept Roman law alive in Justinian's compilation of the 530s, and ran a money economy through centuries when many western kingdoms were doing without those tools. The fork is a trace of artisans, trade, and manners. So is the military fact at the other end of the book. The same city, besieged again and again, fell to the Ottomans on 29 May 1453, when Mehmed II's cannon broke through and the last emperor, Constantine XI, was killed in the fighting.

The length of that survival is the fact any theory of Rome's fall has to live with. Whatever ended Roman government in the west in the fifth century did not end the Roman state that continued at Constantinople. Walls, a tax system, a sea empire, and a weapon that could burn a fleet are part of the explanation, and so is the outcome of particular sieges, including the one in 717. Herrin writes against the habit of treating that state as a decadent footnote to classical Rome. The chapters are the correction: a medieval government, strange to a modern reader in its eunuchs and its icons, and present in Europe's law and map for as long as the western Middle Ages lasted.
