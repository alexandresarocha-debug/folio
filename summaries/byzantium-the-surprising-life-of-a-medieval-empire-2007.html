One afternoon in 2002, two workmen repairing the old buildings at King's College London knocked on Judith Herrin's office door. The notice read "Professor of Byzantine History." They asked her what Byzantine history was. They thought it had something to do with Turkey. Years of teaching had not prepared her for a ten-minute answer. They thanked her and asked her to write that answer for them. Byzantium: The Surprising Life of a Medieval Empire, copyright 2007, is the book that came out of the visit. In the introduction she sets aside the usual chronicle of ninety emperors and about 125 patriarchs of Constantinople, stretched across eleven hundred years. She writes by topic: the city, the law, the faith, the coin, the neighbors.

The city is the clearest reason the state outlasted the western emperors. Constantinople was dedicated in 330, on a triangle of land with the sea on two sides. The land walls completed in the fifth century, under Theodosius II, turned a siege into an enormous job. A government could lose a campaign and still keep the capital, the mint, and the tax office. In the 530s Justinian's lawyers gathered centuries of Roman rulings into the Corpus Juris Civilis, a law book later Europe inherited. The gold coin, the nomisma, remained a piece of money neighbors would take. Herrin's practical claim is that walls, a written law, and a stable gold coin are how a medieval Roman state stays in business.

That state was hit hard and still lasted. In the seventh century, after a ruinous war with Persia, Arab armies took Syria, Palestine, and Egypt, the rich provinces that had fed the eastern empire. A smaller state, now Greek in its ordinary language, went on crowning emperors from Constantinople. In 1204 a crusading army sacked the city. On 29 May 1453 the Ottomans took it, and the empire ended. A final defeat does not turn the previous thousand years into a footnote. Herrin wrote the book for readers who had been given the adjective "Byzantine," meaning devious and decayed, and almost none of the institutions.
