A state hiding in its own name

Judith Herrin's Byzantium: The Surprising Life of a Medieval Empire, published in 2007, begins from a simple embarrassment. The medieval Roman state centred on Constantinople lasted from the foundation of that city in the fourth century until the Ottoman conquest in 1453, and most general readers have been taught to file it as a footnote, or as a byword for decadence. Herrin has told the story of being asked, by workmen repairing her office, what Byzantine civilisation was, and of realising that she had no short answer fit for people who had never been offered one. The book is that answer. Its opening claim is that this empire was not an oriental curiosity attached to the edge of medieval Europe. It was a durable and inventive Roman polity, Greek in language for most of its life, Christian in an eastern form, and important to anyone who wants to know why the Roman name did not end in 476.

She writes thematically as much as chronologically. A reader meets the city, the court, the law, the icons, the economy, and the neighbours in essays that move back and forth across the centuries. The method suits a civilisation that is usually crushed by the length of its own timeline. Herrin would rather you understood what an icon was, or why the gold coin mattered, than that you memorised every dynasty in order. The argument is cumulative. Each institution she explains is a reason the state could survive shocks that ended other powers.

Constantinople, law, and the classical inheritance

The city is the first explanation. Constantinople sits on a triangle of land that can be walled across the base, with the sea as the other two sides. The Theodosian walls, completed in the fifth century, made a siege of the land side a vast undertaking. Herrin uses the walls as a fact about politics, not as scenery. A capital that rarely falls lets a government lose campaigns and keep the state. Emperors could be overthrown in the palace more easily than the city could be taken from outside. That pattern produced the court intrigue for which Byzantium is mocked, and it also produced continuity. The chancery, the tax, and the mint survived changes of ruler that would have been fatal in a less anchored kingdom.

Roman law is her second monument. Under Justinian in the sixth century the scattered rulings and juristic writings of centuries were compiled into the Corpus Juris Civilis. Herrin treats the codification as a Byzantine achievement with a life far beyond Byzantium. The corpus transmitted Roman legal reasoning to medieval and modern Europe. Inside the empire it gave officials a language of rule that was older than any dynasty. Greek learning is the parallel case. Classical texts were copied, taught, and commented on in a Greek Christian society that never decided Homer was someone else's ancestor. When Italian humanists later wanted Greek books, Byzantine scholars and Byzantine libraries were where many of them had to come from. Herrin is making a claim about transmission. A great deal of what Europe calls its classical inheritance passed through the hands of people who called themselves Romans and whom later Europeans often refused to call Romans.

Faith, icons, and the shape of orthodoxy

Religion, in this book, is not a haze over an otherwise administrative story. It is one of the ways the society recognised itself. Herrin explains the controversy over icons, the long quarrel about whether holy images were a legitimate form of Christian worship or a relapse into idolatry. Iconoclasm was an imperial policy in more than one period, and the restoration of images was a decision about doctrine and about the independence of religious tradition from the emperor's latest theology. The cult of icons, the monasteries, and the liturgy in Greek became the ordinary piety of the empire and then of the Slavic world that took its Christianity from Constantinople. Orthodoxy here means a form of Christian life, not a synonym for rigidity. Herrin is interested in how that life was practised by women as well as by monks and emperors, and she gives space to empresses who ruled, Irene among them, rather than treating power as a male sequence with occasional scandals.

The missionary consequence is part of her case for importance. The conversion of the Slavs, the alphabet associated with Cyril and Methodius, and the religious allegiance of the Balkans and later of Rus tie a large part of eastern Europe to the Byzantine pattern. A history of the Middle Ages that starts and ends in Latin Christendom cannot see this expansion. Herrin's point is that the surprising life of the empire includes lives lived far from the Bosporus, in churches and law codes shaped by its example. The empire was a centre, and then it was a model that outlasted the centre's strength.

Economy, diplomacy, and the seventh-century test

On the economy she emphasises a fact that moralising accounts of decline forget. The Byzantine gold coin, the nomisma, remained a standard of weight and fineness for centuries, a piece of money that neighbours trusted. A stable high-value coin is evidence of a fiscal authority and of workshops that could be controlled. It does not mean every peasant prospered. It means the state could still do one of the things the western empire ceased to do. Constantinople's position on the routes between the Black Sea, the Aegean, and the Mediterranean gave it trade as well as walls. Herrin uses commerce and craft as part of the surprise. A society remembered for ceremony was also a society of silk, of harbour dues, and of a monetary system serious enough that its coin was imitated.

The hardest test of the whole structure came in the seventh century. After a ruinous war with Sasanian Persia, the Arab conquests took Syria, Palestine, Egypt, and North Africa, the wealthy provinces that had fed the late Roman east. A lesser state would have ended. Herrin describes a reorganisation around a smaller Anatolian and Aegean core, a Greek rather than a bilingual society, and a military and administrative order built to survive constant raiding. Specialists still argue about how early and how tidy the system of themes, the military provinces, actually was. She does not need the tidiest version. She needs the outcome, which is not seriously disputed. An empire that had lost the rich south was still there a century later, still minting, still crowning emperors, still defining itself as Roman. That survival is her answer to any theory of Roman collapse which forgets that half of Rome did not collapse on the familiar western schedule.

1204, 1453, and the uses of the word decadence

The later narrative does not pretend that survival was comfort. The Fourth Crusade, diverted to Constantinople, sacked the city in 1204 and broke the empire into successor states. Herrin treats 1204 as a catastrophe inflicted by fellow Christians, and as a wound the restored Palaiologan empire never fully healed. The final Ottoman siege in 1453 ended the state. She refuses the habit of reading the last two centuries as proof that the whole millennium had been a decline. A polity can be vigorous for a very long time and still be conquered at last. The Enlightenment portrait of Byzantium, which she associates with writers such as Montesquieu and with the tone of Gibbon's later volumes, judged the whole by the court intrigue and the theological quarrel, and concluded that the east had been a long decadence. Herrin's essays are a point-by-point substitution of institutions for that adjective. Walls, law, icons, coinage, diplomacy, and a capital are not the furniture of a decadent footnote. They are the reasons a medieval empire lasted.

The book is not a research monograph with a new archival find in every chapter. It is a defence, written by a historian who has spent a career in the sources, against a popular ignorance. The limitation follows from the genre. Contested technical questions, the precise origin of the themes, the size of the seventh-century economy, the degree of Greek national feeling, are handled lightly. Readers who want those arguments have to go to the specialist literature, including the work of historians who think the Roman identity of Byzantium should be stated even more starkly than Herrin states it. Within its chosen task, explaining a whole civilisation to people who have been given only the adjective, the book is a coherent case that the adjective was an insult rather than a description.

Why it matters here

The card byzantium-survival is the claim that Rome did not end in 476, and that the medieval empire centred on Constantinople is the test any theory of the fall has to pass. Herrin's book is the card's main text. It supplies the content of the survival: Justinian's law, the keeping of Greek literature, the Orthodox and Slavic inheritance, the buffer the empire formed on Europe's eastern side, and the reorganisation after the Arab conquests. It also supplies the historiographical point. Calling this society a decadent oriental appendix is an Enlightenment habit, not a result. The card's further observation, that whatever killed the west had to be something the east could survive, is the question Herrin leaves the reader trained to ask. Geography, a fiscal base, and the walls of Constantinople are her practical answers, set out through the life of the society rather than as a formula.
