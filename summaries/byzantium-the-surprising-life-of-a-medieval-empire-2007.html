On 11 May 330 Constantine dedicated a new capital on the site of the old Greek city of Byzantion and called it Constantinople. On 29 May 1453 Ottoman troops under Mehmed II broke into that city, and the last Roman emperor, Constantine XI, died in the fighting. Judith Herrin's Byzantium: The Surprising Life of a Medieval Empire (Allen Lane, 2007) is a series of short chapters on what that state did in the eleven centuries between those two days. She begins from the fact that it was a working government, with a tax system, a professional army, and a capital that was for long periods the largest Christian city in the world.

Two chapters make the machinery concrete. In January 532 the Nika riot burned the great church of the capital. Justinian rebuilt it. The new Hagia Sophia was dedicated on 27 December 537, with a dome on a scale no earlier Roman church had carried. Herrin treats the building as evidence of a state that could still assemble architects, timber, brick, and taxes, not as a souvenir of a dead empire. A later chapter takes up Greek fire, the burning liquid the Byzantine navy shot from siphons, which could float and burn on the sea. The formula was kept as a state secret. During the Arab siege of 717–718 the city held, and the weapon was one of the tools used against the fleet. Herrin uses that survival, and others like it, to argue that the empire's position on the straits, its walls, its diplomacy, and its navy repeatedly bought it another century.

The name people use decides the story they tell. "Byzantium" sounds like a separate, slightly unreal kingdom. The people who lived there kept the Roman law, the Roman title of emperor, and, in their own usage, the Roman name. The practical record is a sequence of recoveries: the city lost to the Fourth Crusade in 1204, recovered in 1261, and held again until 1453. Decline is a bad description of a government that could rebuild Hagia Sophia in five years and still defend its harbor two centuries later. The end, when it came, was a siege with a date, against an Ottoman army with cannon, not a mood that had lasted for a thousand years.
