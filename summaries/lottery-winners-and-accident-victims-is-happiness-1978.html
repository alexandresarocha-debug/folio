Philip Brickman and Dan Coates of Northwestern University and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman of the University of Massachusetts started from adaptation level theory. They predicted that a fortune would raise happiness less than expected, for two reasons: the thrill of winning would make ordinary pleasures seem duller by contrast, and people would get used to the new pleasures money could buy.

Study 1 had three groups. The 22 lottery winners came from a list of 197 major winners of the Illinois State Lottery; seven had won $1 million, and the smallest prizes were $50,000. The comparison group of 22 was drawn from the phone book in roughly the same areas of the city. The 29 accident victims, 11 paraplegic and 18 quadriplegic, had been interviewed for an earlier study at a rehabilitation institute, between one month and one year after their accidents. Many interviews were done by phone, with response rates of 52% for winners and 41% for the comparison group.

On 0 to 5 scales, present happiness averaged 4.00 for winners, 3.82 for the comparison group and 2.96 for the accident victims. Winners and comparison group did not differ significantly. Winners rated everyday pleasures, such as talking with a friend, hearing a joke or getting a compliment, lower than the comparison group, 3.33 against 3.82. The victims rated their past as happier than the others did, which the authors called a nostalgia effect, but their present happiness was still above the middle of the scale. The accident victims, they wrote, did not appear nearly as unhappy as might have been expected.

Study 2 compared 86 people who did or did not buy lottery tickets, and found that the results were not explained by pre-existing differences between buyers and non-buyers.

What this means in real life: we tend to overestimate how long and how much a single event, good or bad, will change our everyday happiness.

Caution: the groups were small and measured only once, the winners and comparison group differed in background, and the study made no statistical adjustments. Larger, longer studies have reached more mixed conclusions.
