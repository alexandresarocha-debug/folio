The rationalist picture Haidt is arguing with

Jonathan Haidt's paper The emotional dog and its rational tail, published in Psychological Review in 2001, is a theory of moral judgement aimed at a particular opponent. The opponent is rationalism about morality, the view, strong in the tradition of Lawrence Kohlberg and influential far beyond it, that a moral judgement is the product of reasoning. On that view a person takes in the facts, applies principles about harm, rights or justice, and arrives at a conclusion. Feeling may accompany the conclusion. Feeling is not what produces it. Development, on the same picture, is the improvement of the reasoning. Haidt's title states his alternative in a metaphor he then spends the paper cashing out. The dog wags the tail. Intuition comes first and does the work. Reasoning is the tail, real and often in motion, usually not the cause of the movement.

He calls the alternative the social intuitionist model. Both words are doing work. Intuitionist, because the typical cause of a moral judgement is a fast, affectively charged evaluation that arrives before reasons do. Social, because the reasoning that does occur is aimed largely at other people, and because other people's judgements can change our intuitions directly, without our having followed an argument step by step. Haidt is not claiming that nobody ever reasons their way to a moral change of mind. He is claiming that this is the rare case, and that a psychology of moral judgement built to describe the rare case will misunderstand the ordinary one.

The links in the model

The model is a small set of causal links, and the argument is about which links are busy. The first link runs from a situation to an intuition to a judgement. Someone sees or hears of an action, a moral intuition fires, and the person knows that the action is wrong, or right, before they have constructed a case. The second link runs from the judgement to reasoning after the fact. Reasons are produced to justify the judgement already reached, especially when a listener asks for them. Haidt treats this post hoc reasoning as skilled, not as a stammer. People are good at finding reasons. The goodness is the goodness of a lawyer who has already been retained, not the goodness of a judge who has not yet heard the case.

The third link runs from one person's reasons to another person's intuitions. Argument matters, on this account, but it matters socially. I offer you reasons in the hope of shifting your intuition, and if I succeed, your judgement changes because your intuition changed, not because you executed a private deduction and then felt accordingly. The fourth link is social persuasion in a stricter sense. The bare fact that people whose judgement you share have come down one way can move your intuition, even when you never inspect their reasons. Alongside these, Haidt allows links that the rationalist picture treats as central: private reasoning that really does produce a judgement, and private reflection that modifies an intuition. He places them in the model so that the theory is not a slogan, and he predicts that they are used less often than the social and post hoc links. The empirical programme is then to show situations in which judgement and reason come apart in the way the busy links predict.

Moral dumbfounding

The exhibit is moral dumbfounding. Haidt and his colleagues gave people stories constructed to be offensive and also to be harmless. The best known is a story of consensual incest between adult siblings who use contraception, keep the matter secret, suffer no psychological harm, and decide not to do it again. Other stories carry a similar structure, an action that triggers disgust or disrespect without a victim the listener can point to once the facts are granted. The listener judges the action to be wrong. The experimenter then answers the reasons the listener offers. If the listener says someone might be hurt, the story stipulates that no one was. If the listener appeals to consequences the story has closed, the experimenter points at the closure. Many people remain sure the action is wrong and, at the same time, run out of reasons. They are dumbfounded. They will say, in effect, that they know it is wrong even though they cannot say why.

Dumbfounding is useful because of what it does to the rationalist order. If reasoning produced the judgement, then removing the reasons should remove the judgement, or at least leave the person uncertain. If the intuition produced the judgement, and the reasons were a report written afterwards, then removing the reasons should leave the judgement standing and the person empty-handed. The stories are designed to make the usual harm-based reasons inapplicable, so that the report has nothing true to say, while the intuition still has something to feel. Haidt takes the resulting pattern as evidence that the causal arrow runs from intuition to judgement to reason. He does not take it as evidence that such acts are in fact wrong, or that disgust is a reliable moral guide. The paper is about the psychology of the judgement, not about the normative question of what to do with a judgement that survives its reasons.

What the social half adds

A theory that stopped at dumbfounding would be an intuitionist theory, and Haidt thinks intuitionism by itself is incomplete. Moral reasoning exists, people spend a great deal of time on it, and moral views do change. The social intuitionist account relocates reasoning rather than deleting it. Reasons are for other people. We reason in order to justify ourselves and in order to recruit other people's intuitions to our side. That relocation explains why reasoning so often has the form of advocacy, why people search for evidence that supports the judgement they already hold, and why a conversation can change a mind even when, looking at the transcript afterwards, the decisive step was not a logical one. The other person acquired a new intuition, perhaps from a vivid example, perhaps from the pressure of being out of step, and the new intuition produced a new judgement, which then acquired its own post hoc reasons.

This is why the paper's argument is connected, in spirit if not yet in the letter, to later work on reasoning as a social tool. If the function of moral reasoning is persuasion and justification, then its failures as a private method of inquiry are not surprising. A process designed to defend a conclusion will be good at defending conclusions. Haidt's own emphasis stays on the moral emotions and on the cultural shaping of what triggers them. Different societies educate the intuitions differently. The model is not the claim that everyone has the same disgusts. It is the claim that, whatever the local intuitions are, judgement typically begins with them, and reasoning typically follows in their service.

Where the exhibit has been challenged

The dumbfounding findings have been re-examined, and the re-examination belongs in the argument rather than in a footnote, because the paper leans on them. Some attempts at replication have found that fully dumbfounded responses are less common than the original reports suggested. Many participants, pressed on a harmless-but-offensive story, do offer reasons, and the reasons are coherent inside a moral view that counts more than physical harm. A person who says that incest violates a norm that protects families, or that using a flag as a rag degrades a symbol, is not empty of reasons merely because the experimenter has ruled out injury. Whether those reasons are post hoc is a harder question than whether they are offered. The phenomenon of being unable to say anything at all turns out to be a less stable foundation than the 2001 paper made it seem.

A second challenge goes at the construction of the stories. Kurt Gray and his colleagues argue for a theory of dyadic morality in which perceived harm is the basis of moral judgement. On that view, the incest story is not processed as harmless, whatever the stipulations say. Listeners perceive a victim even when the text denies one, and their judgement is a judgement about harm after all. If that is right, dumbfounding does not show a non-harm intuition surviving the removal of reasons. It shows a harm intuition that the experimenter failed to talk the listener out of. Haidt's model can survive a revision of its exhibit. It cannot treat the exhibit as decisive if the exhibit itself is unstable. The durable part of the paper is the claim about order and function: intuition is the usual cause, reasoning is usually justification, and the interesting causal work of reasoning is social.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The elephant and the rider: intuition first, reasoning second, begins with this paper. The card's account of moral dumbfounding, including the story of consensual incest with contraception and no consequences, and the spectacle of people who still know an act is wrong after their harm-based objections have been answered, is a reading of the 2001 evidence. The card then follows Haidt into the later books, where the dog and the tail become the elephant and the rider, and into moral foundations theory, where the intuitions are sorted into kinds. It also records the criticisms that a careful reader of the paper now has to carry: dumbfounded silence is less common in some replications than the original work implied, and a rival theory holds that perceived harm is doing the work the stories tried to exclude. The paper remains the place where the model is stated as a model, with links, rather than as a metaphor. The metaphor is what The Righteous Mind gives the general reader. The links are what this paper gives anyone who wants the argument in a form that can be wrong.
