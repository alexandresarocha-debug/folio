Jonathan Haidt's 2001 paper opens with a story he and his colleagues had been putting to people in interviews. Julie and Mark, brother and sister, are on vacation from college. One night they decide to make love. She is already on the pill, he uses a condom, they enjoy it, they decide not to do it again, and the secret makes them feel closer. Was that all right? In the interview study this paper draws on, with 30 participants, 20% said at the start that it was okay. After an interviewer had answered the usual objections, about pregnancy, harm, and the relationship, 32% said it was okay. Many of the rest arrived at a sentence Haidt quotes: "I don't know, I can't explain it, I just know it's wrong."

Haidt's claim is that the judgment came first and the reasons were assembled afterward. He calls the state moral dumbfounding: keeping the judgment, feeling stuck, and having no reason you can still defend. The same interviews included Heinz's dilemma, the man who must decide whether to steal a drug to save his dying wife. On Heinz, the reasons people gave tracked the judgment they reached. On the incest story, people laughed, abandoned arguments, and still condemned the act. The paper's model is social as well as intuitive. Other people's reactions, and the reasons a culture keeps ready, shape both the judgment and the story a person tells about it.

That is why a cited fact so often bounces off in an argument about a punishment, a deal, or a family decision. A person who already feels the act is wrong will look for a reason that fits, and the feeling does not dissolve when the first reason fails. Reaching the intuition is slower than winning the sentence. Reasons still do work. They are how people persuade one another and how a group settles a rule. They are a thin description of the moment the judgment was made.

Edward Royzman, Kwanwoo Kim, and Robert Leeman later took the same story apart in Judgment and Decision Making. Across three studies, people often refused to believe the night had truly been harmless. Once that disbelief was counted, the authors' estimate of genuine dumbfounding was about zero.

Source. Haidt, J. (2001). The emotional dog and its rational tail: A social intuitionist approach to moral judgment. Psychological Review, 108(4), 814–834. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.108.4.814
