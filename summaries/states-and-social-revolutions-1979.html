Theda Skocpol's definition, on the first pages of States and Social Revolutions (1979), is narrow on purpose. A social revolution is a rapid, basic transformation of a society's state and of its class structure, and it is accompanied and partly carried through by class-based revolt from below. By that test she keeps three cases: France from 1789, Russia in 1917, and China from the fall of the empire through 1949. She sets aside political revolutions that changed who governed while the class structure stayed. England in the seventeenth century is that kind of case: the monarchy broke, and the landed order returned. She also sets aside modernizations imposed from above. Meiji Japan rebuilt the state without a peasant revolt carrying the change.

France is the first of the three, and it does not begin with a Jacobin plan. After the bills of the American war, the Bourbon monarchy could not get a new tax through the privileged orders. In 1787 an Assembly of Notables refused the controller general's plan. In the summer of 1789, with the royal administration already splitting, peasant revolts destroyed seigneurial claims across large parts of the countryside. The men who later called themselves revolutionaries did not schedule that rising. They inherited a state that had lost its ordinary grip, and the state they and Napoleon built was more centralized than the one that had collapsed. Russia follows the same order of events. In February 1917 the autocracy gave way under defeat, shortage, and a garrison that would not keep firing, before the Bolsheviks were in any position to capture a working government. Through that year peasants took the land. The regime that came out of the civil war taxed, conscripted, and planned on a scale the tsars had not managed. Skocpol's sentence on the outcome is that social revolutions build stronger states, not weaker ones.

The practical point, when someone is deciding why one country broke and a neighbor did not, is to start from the state and the countryside rather than from the program of the party that inherited the wreck. Ideas and leaders matter after there is a wreck to inherit. A reforming monarchy that can still pay its army, and a peasantry that cannot act together once the bailiff disappears, can modernize without a social revolution. Prussia and Meiji Japan are her examples of that path. France, Russia, and China are the cases in which international military pressure, a dominant class that blocked the taxes the state needed, and peasant communities with enough solidarity to revolt all arrived together.

William H. Sewell Jr., in the Journal of Modern History in 1985, argued that this leaves out the language in which the French break became the Revolution it became. Enlightenment political ideas were not decoration on top of a fiscal crash. They shaped what the new state was rebuilt to be. Iran in 1979 was the case that fit the model badly: a state enriched by oil, and a revolt of cities and clerics, rather than a peasant war after military bankruptcy. Skocpol took that revolution up herself in World Politics in 1982, and treated the oil income and the Shi'a networks as reasons her 1979 pattern had to be widened.
