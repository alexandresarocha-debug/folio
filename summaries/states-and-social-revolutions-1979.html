In the last week of July 1789 and the first days of August, peasant communities across France attacked châteaux and burned the papers that recorded seigneurial dues. On the night of 4 August the National Assembly voted to abolish the feudal privileges. Theda Skocpol's point is that the Assembly was catching up with the countryside. The royal government had seized up over taxes and debt, after decades of wars with Britain, and the soldiers who had protected lords were no longer reliably there. Villages that already had their own assemblies could act together in that gap. Where landlords sat on top of the village and organized the work themselves, the same national crisis did not produce the same rural attack. The Great Fear was a peasant revolt made possible by a state breakdown, and the new order had to live with what the peasants had already destroyed.

She sets Russia beside it. The imperial state went into the First World War still tied to a landed class and an unreformed countryside, and in 1917 the administration and the army came apart. Peasant communes seized landlord land and divided it by the village's own rules of repartition. The Bolsheviks won the capital and then the civil war. They did not have to persuade the villages to invent that seizure. Skocpol's third case, China from the collapse of the empire in 1911 through the Communist victory in 1949, is the same pairing stretched over decades of invasion and broken authority: a state that could not hold, and rural power that could be rebuilt against landlords. She uses the contrasts on purpose. Japan's Meiji renovation after 1868, and Prussia's reforms, rebuilt states under foreign military pressure without a peasant war that destroyed the landed class. England's crisis in the 1640s rearranged who was sovereign and left the agrarian order much more intact.

When someone explains a revolution by saying the people were miserable or hopeful, her question is the prior one. Did the state's army and tax machine actually stop, and could villagers act as a group when it did? Anger was present in all three countries. It was also present in countries that reformed from above and stayed recognizable. The rare event, on her evidence, is the pair: collapse at the center, and a countryside organized enough to take the land.

Sewell Jr. answered in the Journal of Modern History in 1985 that this leaves out the political language of the French Revolution, which shaped what the actors could do and what they thought they were doing. Skocpol replied in the same issue. The 1979 book is the argument that the structure can be traced without treating that language as the cause.
