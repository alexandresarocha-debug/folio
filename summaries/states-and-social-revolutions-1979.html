What she means by a social revolution

Theda Skocpol’s States and Social Revolutions, published in 1979, starts by narrowing the word revolution until it can be used as a category rather than a cheer. Social revolutions, in her definition, are rapid, basic transformations of a society’s state and class structures, and they are accompanied and in part carried through by class-based revolts from below. The definition is doing several exclusions at once. A change of rulers that leaves the state and the property system standing is not a social revolution. A slow social transformation without a political break, however deep, is not one either. A revolt that fails to remake the state is a rebellion, historically important and analytically different. By this standard the great cases are few. Skocpol builds the book on three: France in 1789, Russia in 1917, and China from the breakdown of the imperial system through the victory of the Communists in 1949. She sets beside them countries that experienced political crisis, reform from above, or rebellion without the full combination, so that the argument is comparative rather than a set of three celebrations.

The definition already announces her quarrel with the theories then dominant. Explanations that began with the grievances of the masses, with relative deprivation, with a revolutionary ideology, or with the will of a vanguard, seemed to her to start in the wrong place. Misery and anger are widespread. Social revolutions are rare. The interesting question is which structures make a revolutionary transformation possible, not which emotions make it feel justified. She calls her approach structural and non-voluntarist. People act, but they act inside administrative, agrarian, and geopolitical circumstances that select, far more strictly than their ideas do, which actions can consolidate into a new order. The book is the most influential statement of that method in the study of revolutions, and much of the later literature is a debate with it.

States in a world of competitors

Skocpol’s first structural condition is the state itself, treated as an organisation with its own interests, not as the instrument of a dominant class. Old-regime states in her cases were agrarian bureaucracies trying to survive in a system of competing states. Survival meant extracting resources for war. Extraction meant pressing on landed elites and on peasants who were already the source of the surplus. The crisis comes when international pressure, military competition, defeat, or the threat of it, forces the state to demand more, and the structures through which it would have to demand more are controlled or obstructed by the very elites it needs. In France, the Bourbon monarchy’s repeated defeats in great-power competition left it fiscally exhausted, and its attempts to tax the privileged were blocked by the corporate bodies, parlements and estates, that the monarchy had never abolished. In Russia, the strain of the First World War on a state that had survived 1905 without solving its relationship to the nobility, the army, or the peasantry produced a collapse of administration at the front and in the capital. In China, a weaker imperial state broke earlier, and decades of warlordism and of Japanese invasion kept any successor from establishing the kind of stable conservative rule that might have made a peasant revolution unnecessary.

The comparison with countries that did not have social revolutions is the method’s discipline. England’s seventeenth-century crisis, in her reading, produced a political revolution, a rearrangement of sovereignty, without the transformation of class relations that France underwent. Japan’s Meiji transformation was a revolution from above, a state-led reconstruction in response to international threat, without a peasant war remaking the countryside on the French or Chinese pattern. Prussia modernised its state and its army while keeping a landed class inside the new order. Skocpol’s claim is not that these societies were calm. It is that the full pattern, state breakdown plus class transformation driven partly from below, requires a specific alignment. Military-geopolitical pressure is common. The inability of a particular kind of agrarian state to meet that pressure without coming apart is not.

Peasants, and why the countryside matters

State breakdown is not yet a social revolution. Elites can muddle through a fiscal crisis if the countryside is quiet. Skocpol’s second condition is the capacity of peasants to revolt in ways that destroy the old agrarian class relations, and that capacity, she argues, is structural too. It depends on the solidarity and the autonomy of peasant communities: on whether villages have institutions of their own, and on whether landlords are present in the village or are absentees represented by agents. In France, peasant communities attacked the seigneurial system once the political crisis had opened the opportunity, and the destruction of feudal dues was not a gift from the National Assembly but a fact the Assembly had to recognise. In Russia, the peasant commune, the mir, provided a collective actor capable of seizing land when the army and the state ceased to guarantee the landlords. In China, the Communists were able to link themselves to peasant grievance in regions where the old elite’s protection had failed and where the party offered a local order against landlords, warlords, and the Japanese. Where peasant communities were fragmented, closely supervised, or dependent on landlords for the organisation of production itself, the same national crisis did not produce the same rural revolution.

This is her answer to theories of rising expectations. Davies’s J-curve and Gurr’s relative deprivation describe moods that she regards as far too widely available to pick out her three cases. French peasants were not revolutionary because they had read the philosophes, and Chinese peasants were not revolutionary because a curve of satisfaction had hooked downward in a textbook shape. They were revolutionary when the state’s coercive presence withdrew and when their own institutions let them act together against landlords. Ideas and leaders are not absent from the book, but they are treated as resources that become effective inside these openings. A Jacobin, a Bolshevik, or a Communist cadre cannot create the opening by doctrine. Skocpol was later criticised for this demotion of ideas, and she took parts of the criticism seriously in subsequent work. In this book the demotion is the point. She is showing how far one can get without treating ideology as the cause.

What revolutions build

The third movement of the argument is about outcomes, and it is the movement that connects her most closely to Tocqueville. Social revolutions do not, in her cases, produce decentralised liberty. They produce stronger, more centralised, more bureaucratic states than the ones they destroyed, states with a new capacity to extract, to conscript, and to transform agrarian society from above. The French Revolution cleared the corporate obstacles to a uniform national state. The Russian Revolution built a party-state that could industrialise by command. The Chinese Revolution built a state capable of reaching villages that the empire had never reliably administered. The continuity with the old regime is not the continuity of personnel. It is the continuity, and the intensification, of the project of state-building under international pressure. Revolutionaries who had attacked a state for being oppressive ended by constructing a state better organised for the same competitive world.

This is why she refuses to tell the story as a morality play with liberty as the natural ending. The class transformation is real: seigneurs, landlords, and old ruling classes lose their property and their political veto. The political form that consolidates the transformation is hierarchical. Skocpol’s structural method is well suited to seeing this, because a method that starts from state competition is not surprised when the winners of a revolution behave like state-builders. A method that starts from the revolutionaries’ slogans is surprised, and then has to speak of betrayal. She prefers not to. The new state is not a betrayal of the revolution’s social content. It is the instrument through which that content was made to stick, and it has interests of its own that outrun the peasants who made the opening.

Limits she left open

The criticisms that have lasted are as structural as the book. One is selection. Three positive cases, however carefully contrasted with negative ones, are a small base, and the contrasts were not chosen by a rule that a sceptic could rerun. Later comparative work has added cases, Iran among them, that fit her agrarian and state-breakdown template awkwardly, and revolutions in more urban and more ideological registers have asked for a framework she set aside. A second criticism, made early by William Sewell and others, is that the exclusion of ideology and of political culture is not a neutral simplification. The French Revolution’s actors argued in a language that shaped what they could do, and a structural account that treats that language as foam will misread the sequence. Skocpol’s later writing grants culture and agency a larger place than States and Social Revolutions did. The 1979 book should be read as a deliberate wager, not as her last word.

A third limit is historical and has become clearer with distance. The book explains a kind of revolution that belonged to a world of agrarian empires and great-power war. It is less articulate about coups, about anti-colonial movements in which the international pressure was imperial rather than a war between neighbours, and about the collapse of regimes that do not sit on peasant property relations of the old kind. Jack Goldstone’s demographic-structural work offers a different long-run mechanism, population pressure on state resources, that competes with hers inside some of the same cases. None of these rivals has made her two conditions look like the wrong place to have started.

Why it matters here

On Folio’s card about the Tocqueville paradox, Skocpol is the structural corrective. The card says that relative-deprivation theories, the tradition running from Tocqueville through Davies and Gurr, have mixed empirical support, and that political scientists now put more weight on state capacity and on the organisation of opposition.
