In February 1787 Louis XVI called an Assembly of Notables and asked the privileged to accept a tax that would reach them. They refused. The crown, already carrying the debt of the American war, could not make its own ruling class pay, and the quarrel ended in the Estates-General, which opened on 5 May 1789. Theda Skocpol's States and Social Revolutions (1979) starts the French Revolution there, in the failure of the state, not in a pamphlet or a bread riot. By the summer the administration and the army were no longer holding the countryside in the old way. Peasant revolt then destroyed seigneurial dues and, with them, the local power of the landed class. The political crisis came first. The social revolution used the opening.

She sets Russia beside France, and the order is the same. Peasant grievances and socialist circles were old by 1914. What broke the autocracy was the world war. In February 1917, by the Russian calendar, crowds in Petrograd were joined by the garrison. Nicholas II abdicated within days. Through the rest of that year peasants seized land. The state had already stopped being able to coerce, and the countryside moved into the gap. Skocpol's phrase for the pattern, borrowed from an older argument she makes her own, is that weakness, not oppression, breeds this kind of revolution. Oppression had been available for decades. A collapsed tax machine and a collapsed army had not.

She is strict about what counts. A social revolution, in her definition, is a rapid overturning of both the state and the class order, done through conflict, not a change of cabinet and not a revolt that fails. France, Russia, and China are her three cases. In China the old dynasty gave way in 1911, but the peasant war that finished the landed order succeeded only after Japan's invasion, beginning in 1937, broke the Nationalist government's hold on the countryside. The comparison that makes the claim is with countries that felt the same outside military pressure and did not come apart this way. Meiji Japan after 1868, and Prussia, rebuilt the state from above. Their landed classes were not expropriated by a peasant insurrection. Misery and foreign threat were not enough. The state had to split, and country people had to be in a position to act together when it did.

The use of the book, when someone explains 1789 or 1917 by how angry people had become, is the reminder that anger was common and this outcome was rare. Look for the month the army and the tax office stopped working, and for whether villagers could reach the land when that happened. A theory that starts from the mood will find moods everywhere. Hers starts from the institution that failed.

The Iranian revolution of 1979 did not match the pattern. There was no defeat in a great war and no peasant seizure of estates. It was an urban revolt against a monarchy financed by oil, with mosque networks doing much of the organizing. Skocpol's own essay of 1982, "Rentier State and Shi'a Islam in the Iranian Revolution," was an attempt to account for a case her 1979 comparison had not covered.
