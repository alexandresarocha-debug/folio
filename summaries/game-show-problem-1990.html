Marilyn vos Savant wrote the Ask Marilyn column in Parade magazine. In 1990 a reader, Craig F. Whitaker, sent her the question that made her famous: you pick door 1, the host, who knows what is behind the doors, opens door 3 to show a goat and offers you door 2. Is it to your advantage to switch?

Her answer was yes. The first door has a one-in-three chance of winning, and the second door has a two-in-three chance. To make it obvious, she suggested imagining a million doors: you pick door 1, the host opens every other door except number 777,777, and you would switch to it quickly.

The response was enormous. Her collected columns quote letters from readers with doctorates at George Mason University, the University of Florida, Georgetown and elsewhere, telling her she had made a basic error. One began, "You blew it!" Another, "You are the goat!" She reported that of the letters from the general public, 92% were against her answer, and of the letters from universities, 65%. Overall, nine out of ten readers completely disagreed.

She then turned the dispute into an experiment. She asked maths classes to play it with three paper cups and a penny: the host hides the penny using a die, the contestant picks a cup, the host lifts a losing cup, and the contestant either stays or switches. Each strategy was to be played two hundred times. Classes across the country wrote back that the results supported her, and many teachers said their students were astonished. A scientist at MIT wrote that most of his colleagues, and he himself at first, had thought she was wrong.

What this means in real life: when someone with knowledge removes options for you, the information they give is not neutral. What they could not remove tells you something.

Caution: the archived page gathers the columns and letters as she presented them, and the letters are a selection. The answer depends on the host always opening a goat door and always offering the switch.
