In September 1953, on Koshima, a small island off Miyazaki in Kyushu, a female Japanese macaque named Imo began washing sweet potatoes. She was one and a half years old. Researchers had started leaving potatoes out for the troop in 1952, so the animals would stop raiding farms and would stay where they could be watched. The potatoes came up covered in sand. Imo carried them to the edge of a brook that runs into the sea and rinsed them in fresh water. Masao Kawai, who watched the same troop, published the count in Primates in 1965.

The habit moved one animal at a time. By 1956, eleven monkeys washed potatoes. Kawai's table for 1953 through March 1958 is the figure people still quote: 2 of the 11 adults had the habit, and 15 of the 19 monkeys aged two to seven. By August 1962, 36 of 49 monkeys older than two were washing potatoes. Of the 11 animals born before 1950, only two still did it, and both were females, Eba and Nami. Males older than about four almost never learned it. At that age a male shifts to the edge of the troop and stops sitting with the juveniles who already wash, so he rarely gets a close look at feeding time.

Kawai follows Syunzo Kawamura on the routes. One runs through playmates. The other runs through kin, and inside a family it usually runs from the younger animal to the older: child to mother, younger sibling to older sibling. The water changed too. In 1953 and 1954 the washing was done at the brook, in fresh water, and not in the sea. At the surveys of 1957 and 1958 many of the washers were using salt water. The brook dries up in a drought. The animals who washed came to prefer the sea.

The same shape shows up wherever a small new trick has to be seen to be copied. It moves along the people who actually sit together while the work is done, and it can move from the newest person to the older one. Someone who has stepped out of that daily contact can miss it for years. A head count of converts does not make the missing people suddenly know the trick.

The popular retelling says otherwise. In Lifetide (1979) Lyall Watson wrote that once about a hundred monkeys were washing potatoes, the rest of the troop took it up in an evening and the habit leapt to other islands. Ken Keyes repeated a version of that claim in The Hundredth Monkey (1982). Ron Amundson, writing in the Skeptical Inquirer in 1985, went back to Kawai's tables. The Koshima troop numbered 20 in 1952 and 59 in 1962. There was never a hundredth monkey there. In 1958, the year Watson treats as the miracle, only two animals learned the habit, the young females Zabon and Nogi.
