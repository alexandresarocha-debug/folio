Cass Sunstein asked 83 University of Chicago law students how much they would pay to get arsenic out of their drinking water. The risks were the sort the Environmental Protection Agency was actually weighing. Students were split into four groups. Two groups got a plain description of a cancer risk, either 1 in 1,000,000 or 1 in 100,000. Two groups got the same odds, but the cancer was described as very gruesome and intensely painful, eating away at the internal organs.

They picked a price from a short list: $0, $25, $50, $100, $200, $400, or $800 or more. In the plain version, raising the risk tenfold raised the mean willingness to pay from $71.25 to $194.44. The medians moved from $25 to $100. In the gruesome version the means went from $132.95 to $241.30, a smaller percentage jump, and the median stayed at $100 at both odds. A tenfold risk did not produce anything like a tenfold price. Overall, mean willingness to pay went from $103.57 at the lower risk to $220.73 at the higher one, a bit more than a doubling. Sunstein is frank that the gap between the plain and the gruesome percentage increases was not statistically significant. Making the description more gruesome raised the overall mean from $129.61 to $188.33, and that rise was not significant either. The pattern points the way he predicted. It is not a clean proof on its own.

The cleaner numbers in the essay come from a study he is reporting, not running. Yuval Rottenstreich and Christopher Hsee asked people what they would pay to avoid a short, painful, but not dangerous electric shock, or to avoid a $20 cash penalty. For the cash, the median was $1 to avoid a 1 percent chance and $18 to avoid a 99 percent chance. For the shock, the median was $7 at 1 percent and $10 at 99 percent. Once the harm was the kind you can feel, the odds almost dropped out.

That is the pattern Sunstein wants law to notice. A vivid worst case, a poisoned well, a plane, a child's illness, pulls attention onto how bad the outcome would be. The chance of it happening gets little weight. A rule written in that mood can spend a great deal to wipe out a tiny risk and ignore a larger, duller one. His practical suggestion is the unglamorous one: when the probability really is small, say the number, and do not build the rule around the picture. He also says fear is itself a harm. If telling people the odds will not calm them, a government may still have a reason to reduce the risk, because the fear is real even when the odds are not.

This is not the availability heuristic. Availability is judging how common something is by how easily an example comes to mind. Probability neglect starts once the example is already vivid. The question is no longer "how often does this happen?" It is "how bad would it be?" and the odds stop moving the answer. The arsenic study is small, the prices were picked from a menu, and Sunstein's own contrast did not clear a significance test. The shock prices, which did separate, are the stronger exhibit, and they are hypothetical. Nobody in that room was actually wired up.
