Does it help to write about the worst thing that ever happened to you? In the mid-1980s James Pennebaker and Sandra Beall tested it. Their participants were 46 students in an introductory psychology course. On four days in a row, each wrote for about 15 minutes. Some wrote about a personally traumatic life event. Others, the comparison group, wrote about trivial topics.

Among those writing about trauma, the researchers also varied what the students were told to include. As later summaries describe the design, one group wrote only the facts of the event, one wrote only their feelings about it, and one wrote about both. The study asked which of these, thinking, feeling or both, was linked to the effects.

The researchers took a wide set of measures. They recorded blood pressure and other physiological readings, and asked students about mood and physical symptoms during the experiment. They also obtained health center records for the following six months.

The results were mixed in an interesting way. Writing about both the emotions and the facts of a traumatic event went with higher blood pressure and more negative mood right after the essays. That is, it was hard to do. But the same writers made fewer health center visits in the six months after the experiment. According to later summaries, writing about the facts alone looked more like the trivial-topic control.

The idea behind the study was that holding back thoughts and feelings about an upsetting event is a kind of work for the body, and that facing it, by writing or talking, should reduce the toll. Pennebaker had earlier studied people who had lost a spouse by suicide or accident and found that those who did not confide in others fared worse. Writing offered a way to test confiding without a listener, and without the risk of being judged.

What this means in real life: the paper gave a simple tool that anyone can try, which is to write for a short time about something stressful, with both what happened and how it felt. It also suggests that the first effect may be unpleasant, and that this is not a sign that the writing failed.

Criticism: this was a small first study, with students as participants and a clinic's records as the measure of health. The authors said so themselves, calling the findings preliminary. The next work in this section asks whether the effect holds up across many studies.
