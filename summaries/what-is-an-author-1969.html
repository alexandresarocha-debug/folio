On 22 February 1969 Michel Foucault opened a lecture to the Société française de philosophie with a line from Beckett: "What does it matter who is speaking," someone said, "what does it matter who is speaking." He does not take the shrug as an answer. He takes it as the ethic of a certain modern writing, and then asks what the author's name still does after you have agreed that the writer is not the meaning of the book. His concrete picture of the older arrangement is Scheherazade. In The Thousand and One Nights, narrative is a way of holding death off. She tells stories through the night so that the morning, and the execution, do not arrive. The Greek epic, he says, made a different bargain: the hero dies young so that the story can carry him into immortality. Our culture, Foucault argues, has turned the relation around. Writing is now linked to the sacrifice of the writer. The work that once had the duty of keeping a life alive "possesses the right to kill, to be its author's murderer," and he names Flaubert, Proust, and Kafka.

Saying "the author has disappeared" is not, for him, the end of the problem. You still have to say what a work is. If you edit Nietzsche, do the rough drafts count, the deleted lines, the appointment noted in a notebook, the laundry list? There is no theory that tells you where the work stops. He also refuses the idea that the name on a title page is simply a pointer to a person outside the text, the way an ordinary proper name points. A private letter has a signer and not an author. A contract has a guarantor and not an author. An anonymous text on a wall may have someone who posted it and still not have an author. The name "author" marks a mode of circulation. It says this discourse is to be received as something that lasts, that can be attributed, punished, collected, and taught. Which discourses get that treatment has flipped. In the Middle Ages, he says, texts we would now call scientific were accepted as true only when marked with a name. "Hippocrates said" and "Pliny recounts" were the stamps. Stories, epics, and plays could circulate with nobody asking who wrote them. From the seventeenth or eighteenth century, scientific texts are taken up into methods and results, and the author's name fades, while literary texts are accepted only when an author is attached.

"Who wrote this?" can mean who is liable, who gets paid, which books belong in one pile, or whose life you hope will explain a sentence. Foucault's answer is that those are jobs done by a function, not one trip back to a mind. You can need the name for the legal and historical jobs and still not treat it as the last word on the sentence. The laundry list and the name "Hippocrates" are there so you do not mistake that function for an empty space.
