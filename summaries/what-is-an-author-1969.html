After the announcement that the author has vanished

Michel Foucault's What Is an Author?, given as a lecture in 1969 and then published as an essay, begins where a certain contemporary rhetoric had decided to stop. It had become possible, even fashionable, to say that the author was dead, that writing was a tissue of signs without a sovereign subject, that one should not look behind a text for the person who guaranteed it. Foucault does not spend the essay defending the old sovereign. He argues that the announcement of disappearance is the beginning of a problem, not the solution of one. If the author has vanished as a theological origin, something remains, because we still use authors' names, we still group texts under them, we still ask whether a newly found page is really by the person whose name it would make valuable, and we still hold persons responsible for discourses. The something that remains is what he calls the author-function. The essay is an analysis of that function. It is less a celebration of the reader than Barthes's essay of two years earlier, and it is more historical. Foucault wants to know what social and institutional work the author's name does, once we have stopped believing that the name is the name of a meaning's source.

The difference in temperament between the two essays is part of the intellectual event. Barthes ends on a birth, the reader coming into the place the Author has vacated, and the prose is a permission. Foucault ends on a question he borrows from Beckett, the question of whether it matters who is speaking, and he treats the question as a horizon rather than as a liberty already achieved. He is suspicious of any figure, author or reader, that criticism installs as the new principle of unity. His own method, here and elsewhere, is to look at the rules that make certain statements possible, and at the ways persons are attached to statements when a culture needs someone to reward, punish, or classify. The author is one of those attachments. Understanding it is a matter of describing the attachment, not of choosing a side in a dispute about whether writers have souls.

The name that does more than refer

Foucault's first analytical point is that an author's name is not an ordinary proper name. A proper name points at a person. An author's name does that and something else. It functions as a description and as a means of classification. It gathers certain texts and excludes others. It marks a manner of being of discourse, so that saying the name is not only saying who wrote a sentence but saying what kind of sentence it is and how it should be taken. Disputes about attribution make the extra function visible. When scholars argue about whether a text is really by a given author, they are not only arguing about a historical fact. They are arguing about whether the text belongs in a class, whether it can be used as evidence for what that class of discourse is like, whether its contradictions with other texts in the class are problems to be solved. The name is a principle of organisation. Remove it, and a set of texts falls apart into a pile. Keep it, and the pile becomes an oeuvre, with a development, a unity, and a border.

The border is not given by nature. Foucault is interested in how much labour goes into making an oeuvre coherent, into deciding which notes, drafts, laundry lists and private letters count as part of the work, and which are merely the traces of a life. The decision is critical and institutional, not a fact the texts announce. A culture that organises discourse by authors will do this labour and then forget that it was labour, experiencing the oeuvre as the obvious output of a person. The essay asks the reader to remember the labour. Once it is remembered, the author looks less like a cause and more like a result, a way of handling texts that a particular organisation of knowledge requires. This is the sense in which Foucault accepts the death of the Author as a theology and declines the conclusion that authors have therefore ceased to matter. They matter as functions. Functions are real.

Different discourses, different attachments

The author-function is not the same in every kind of writing, and it has not been the same in every century. Foucault sketches a history in which scientific texts once needed an author's name as a mark that the statement was credible, a proposition attached to a person who could be believed, and later came to stand on their own as science, so that the name became a historical courtesy rather than a condition of truth. Literary texts moved, roughly, in the other direction. Anonymous stories, circulating without a proprietary voice, gave way to a regime in which the literary text is expected to have an author, a style, a deepening oeuvre, a life that criticism can relate to the pages. The sketch is broad, and a historian can quarrel with its stages. The conceptual point survives the quarrel. There is no single answer to what an author is, because authorship is a role assigned differently by law, by science, and by literature, and assigned differently at different times. A theory that defines the author once and for all, whether as the owner of meaning or as a dispensable illusion, has ignored the variability that is the phenomenon.

Foucault also distinguishes ordinary authors of books from a rarer kind of figure, the founder of a discursivity. Marx and Freud are his examples. A novelist produces texts. A founder of discursivity produces not only texts but the possibility and the rules of other texts, a field in which later statements are tests of fidelity, revisions, returns to the origin. Reading a later psychoanalyst is not like reading a later novelist who has been influenced by an earlier one. The later text stands in a relation to the founding text that includes the possibility of being told that it has misunderstood the founder. The author-function here is a principle of a whole discourse, and the arguments inside the discourse are partly arguments about what the founder really said. Foucault offers the distinction as a way of seeing why some names are not just labels on books. They are conditions of what can be said in a discipline. The death of the author, announced globally, is a blunt instrument for a situation this differentiated.

The features of the function

Across these differences, Foucault picks out recurring features of the author-function as it operates in the culture he is describing. It is tied to legal and institutional systems. Ownership, copyright, the possibility of punishment for what one has written, the responsibility of a person before a court or a church or a party, all require that a discourse be attached to someone. A culture that prosecutes statements needs authors in a way that a culture of drifting anonymous tales does not. The author-function is also not spontaneous. It is constructed by the operations of criticism, editing, biography and attribution, the work of deciding which texts count and what unity they are said to have. And it does not refer purely and simply to a real individual. The person in the bookshop and the author-function constructed from the books are related and not identical. Pseudonyms, collaborations, the gap between a life and a style, the several egos a single narrator can contain, all show the non-coincidence. Foucault is assembling a description of a role. The role can be filled by a real person and still be a role, with rules the person did not choose.

This description is his answer to the empty celebration of writing as an anonymous flow. Writing is not, in the institutions we actually have, an anonymous flow. It is a flow that those institutions keep pinning to persons, for reasons of property and punishment and classification. A criticism that talks as if the pinning had already come undone is, in Foucault's view, mistaking a theoretical desire for a historical situation. The desire may be honourable. The situation is that the author's name continues to organise what we can find, what we are allowed to say, and who will answer for saying it. Analysis should turn towards that organisation. It should ask how the function was formed, under what conditions it arose, and what it excludes. Those are historical questions. They are also, for him, the way to avoid putting the reader on the throne the author has just been pushed off. A throne with a new occupant is still a throne, and Foucault's suspicion of unified subjects does not stop at the writer.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the death of the author pairs this essay with Barthes as the usual next reading, and the pairing is the card's real argument. Barthes announces the death and the reader's birth. Foucault asks what the author-function still organises, how a name groups texts and assigns responsibility, and how founders of discursivity differ from writers of books. The card also sets Hirsch against both, as the defence of intention, and mentions the older American argument of Wimsatt and Beardsley, the intentional fallacy, as a different route to a suspicion of authors' plans. What Is an Author? is the piece that prevents the topic from collapsing into a yes or no. Read it after Barthes, for the refusal to let the reader simply inherit the vacant office, and before Hirsch, for a reminder that the institutional author Foucault describes is not the same thing as the intending mind Hirsch defends. The essay's question is its title, and the answer it offers is not a person and not a nothing. The answer is a function, historically variable, legally useful, and too busy to be declared dead by a change in critical manners.
