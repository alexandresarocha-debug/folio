Yuval Rottenstreich and Christopher Hsee asked University of Chicago students what they would pay to stay out of a psychology experiment. For some, the experiment carried a chance of a short, painful, but not dangerous electric shock. For others, it carried a chance of a $20 cash penalty. The chance was 1 percent, 99 percent, or certain. The shock and the penalty were imaginary. Nobody was shocked.

When the shock was certain, the median price to avoid it was $19.86. Twenty-four of the 30 people in that cell would pay $20 or less, so most of them would rather take the shock than hand over more than $20. At a 1 percent chance, the median price to avoid the shock was $7, against $1 to avoid a 1 percent chance of the $20 penalty. At 99 percent, the shock median was $10 and the cash-penalty median was $18. The cash price moved almost in step with the odds, from $1 to $18. The shock price barely moved, from $7 to $10. A 1 percent chance of a shock was already priced like a near certainty.

A happier version of the same twist used a kiss. Twenty Rice undergraduates were offered either $50 in cash or a kiss from their favorite movie star, for sure. Seventy percent took the cash. Another twenty were offered a 1 percent chance of the kiss or a 1 percent chance of the $50. Sixty-five percent took the kiss lottery. Under certainty the money won. At a slim chance, the vivid prize won.

A third study held the money constant and changed only the feeling. A $500 coupon toward a first trip to Paris, Venice, and Rome, and a $500 coupon toward tuition, were worth about the same if you already had them: median selling prices of $499 and $500. A 1 percent chance of the Europe coupon had a median price of $20. A 1 percent chance of the tuition coupon had a median price of $5. At 99 percent the order flipped. The Europe coupon was priced at $450 and the tuition coupon at $478. The exciting prize was overpriced when it was unlikely and underpriced when it was almost sure.

In ordinary choices this is why a tiny chance of a gruesome side effect can dominate a medical decision, while a 1 percent chance of losing $20 does not. The picture arrives first. The percentage is still on the page, and it stops doing much work. The same tilt sells lottery tickets and horror-story insurance. A small chance of something you can see is treated as a real chance. A small chance of a dull loss is treated as almost nothing.

The limit is that these were questionnaires, not bills and not shocks. People said what they would pay. They did not hand over the money or sit in the chair. Prospect theory already said people overweight small probabilities and underweight large ones. What this paper adds is that the bend is sharper when the outcome is the kind that stirs you. Later writers have treated that as a real pattern and also as a pattern that depends on how the chance is described. The $7 and the $10 are still the numbers to remember. They are medians from one set of Chicago students, not a law of every frightened person.
