In the spring of 1716, John Flamsteed, the Astronomer Royal, burned printed sheets of his own star catalogue on Greenwich Hill. The book was the Historia Coelestis of 1712. Isaac Newton and Edmond Halley had seen it through the press against his wishes: observations he had not approved, a star catalogue he considered corrupted, a preface by Halley. About 400 copies were printed. After Queen Anne died in 1714, Flamsteed got hold of 300 of them, kept the pages printed under his own eye by the end of 1707, and burned the rest. He called the fire a sacrifice to truth. The edition he was willing to own, the Historia Coelestis Britannica, came out in 1725, six years after his death. Adrian Johns tells this story because it is a printed scientific book, expensive and handsome, that the man named on it judged to be a false edition.

The Royal Society had already spent half a century trying to build a kind of print a stranger could trust. It was founded in 1660. From 1665 Henry Oldenburg put out the Philosophical Transactions, and the Society kept a register in which a result could be entered under a name and a date. Fellows were asked to "peruse" a manuscript and report back, so the Society could stand behind a printed claim. Johns's point is that those habits were doing the work that a simple story assigns to type. A reader in 1670 did not believe a sheet because it was printed. He asked who had printed it, whether the Society had passed it, and whether this copy matched the one a colleague had.

The same question is still the practical one. A page can be copied perfectly and still be the wrong edition, the abridgement, or the text the author tried to withdraw. Credit sits in the institution that will say which copy counts: a company of stationers, a society with a register, a journal that dates what it receives. Johns's evidence is London, from the late sixteenth century into the early eighteenth, a market famous for unauthorized editions. The Flamsteed fire is what that market could do to a book of record.

Elizabeth Eisenstein answered in the American Historical Review in 2002. She argued that a study of English piracy misses the continental fact: print, from the fifteenth century, put the same texts into many cities at once, which manuscript copying had not done. Johns replied in the same issue that the reliability of those texts was built locally, by readers and by the trades, and was not a property of the machine.
