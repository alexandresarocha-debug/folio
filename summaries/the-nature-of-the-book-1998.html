In 1712 a star catalogue came off a London press with no bookseller's name on the title page, only "Londini: Typis J. Matthews." It was John Flamsteed's life's work as the first Astronomer Royal, and he did not accept it. Printing had begun in 1706 under a committee of referees that included Isaac Newton. By the end of 1707 the equivalent of 97 or 98 sheets had been printed, and then the press stopped. After Flamsteed's royal patron, Prince George of Denmark, died in 1708, Queen Anne paid for the rest, and Edmond Halley edited the volume that appeared in 1712. Observations only up to 1689 had gone through with Flamsteed's approval. Four hundred copies were printed. After the queen died in 1714 and the Earl of Halifax died in 1715, Flamsteed got hold of 300 of them. He pulled out the sheets printed by the end of 1707, for reuse in an edition he would control, and burned the rest. He called the fire a sacrifice to truth. The authorized Historia Coelestis Britannica appeared in 1725, after his death. Adrian Johns makes this quarrel chapter 8 of The Nature of the Book (1998). A large, expensive, well-printed book was, to the man who had made the observations, a theft. Copies of the 1712 edition even differ in small type on a few pages.

Johns's wider claim is that early modern England did not receive fixity as a gift of the machine. Piracy was a normal part of the trade. A reader who wanted to trust a book asked who had printed it, under what privilege, and whether the sheets had been altered. The Stationers' Company, chartered by Mary I in 1557, tried to make the trade legible by registering the right to print a title and by policing printers and booksellers. The Royal Society, founded in 1660, built a register of another kind. A fact, a technique, or a paper could be entered under the name of the person who claimed it and the date of the claim. Philosophical Transactions, begun by Henry Oldenburg in 1665, was one public face of that effort. Credit, in Johns's account, was a social repair, made because the unregulated sheet was easy to copy and easy to doubt.

The practical point is that two copies with the same title can still be different books, and that a printed sentence does not authenticate itself. Someone decided which sheets counted, who could sell them, and whose name sat on the entry. When a scientific or a political claim arrives as "the book," the useful questions are who controlled the edition and what was left out of it. Flamsteed's fire is the extreme form of an ordinary problem. He could not trust the object that carried his own numbers.

Elizabeth Eisenstein replied in the American Historical Review in February 2002. She had compared the handpress with the scribe. Johns, she argued, was comparing the handpress with later steam-powered machines, and then finding the earlier one unreliable. She had written that careless printers multiply error. She had not written that the press, by itself, makes a text true. The exchange leaves both claims standing at different scales. A technology can be chaotic in a given London shop and still, over generations and against the manuscript, make a shared page more possible. Johns's subject is the work required before readers would believe that.
