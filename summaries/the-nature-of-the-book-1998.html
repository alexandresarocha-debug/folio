In 1712 a star catalogue the Astronomer Royal had not agreed to release was printed anyway. John Flamsteed had been observing at Greenwich for decades. A committee that included Isaac Newton had charge of publication, the money came first from Prince George and then from Queen Anne, and Edmond Halley edited the volume that appeared, Historia Coelestis. Flamsteed thought it unfinished and unauthorized. After Anne died in 1714 and Newton's patron the Earl of Halifax died in 1715, Flamsteed got hold of 300 of the 400 copies. He pulled out the pages printed under his own eye, for a later edition he would control, and burned the rest. He called the fire a sacrifice to truth. The authorized Historia Coelestis Britannica came out in 1725, after he was dead. Adrian Johns builds a chapter of The Nature of the Book (1998) on this quarrel. A printed scientific book, expensive and well made, was still something a powerful colleague could issue against the author's will, and something the author could then destroy.

Johns's argument is that nothing in the press itself made a text fixed or believable. Early modern London had piracy, variant editions, and printers who would set a serious book on the same premises as a scandal. Galileo's drawings of the moon were copied badly by other hands and traveled in that damaged form. A reader who wanted the real observations had to know who had supervised the printing. Credit was a social achievement. The Stationers' Company, chartered in 1557, was a large part of it. The right to print a title, called a copy, belonged to the stationer who entered it, not to the person who wrote it. The company hall, the register, and the habits of the trade decided which books were proper and which were stolen. The Royal Society later built a similar discipline around experimental reports: a result counted when the right people had witnessed the procedure and the right printer had been watched. Fixity, in Johns's phrase, was made. It was not delivered by type.

The Flamsteed fire is what that looks like in practice. The same observations existed as Halley's book and as the book Flamsteed wanted, and the difference was not a philosophical one. It was who held the sheets. When a printed page is treated as automatically the author's, and automatically the same in every copy, this episode is the correction. Trust had to be arranged, and it could be withdrawn with a match.

Elizabeth Eisenstein replied in the American Historical Review in 2002. She had compared the handpress with the scribe, she wrote, not with the steam-powered press of the nineteenth century. Against handwriting, printed copies were still far more alike, and a study centered on London does not cancel a change that ran across Europe. She had already said that bad printers multiplied error. The dispute that remains is how much of the stability later readers took for granted was sitting in the machine, and how much of it had to be policed into existence by guilds, editors, and scientific clubs.
