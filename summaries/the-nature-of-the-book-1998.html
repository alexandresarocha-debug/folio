A book about credit, written against a book about machines

Adrian Johns’s The Nature of the Book, published in 1998, is a history of printing and of scientific reading in early modern England, and it is organised as a challenge to Elizabeth Eisenstein. Eisenstein had argued that the press itself brought fixity, standardisation, and reliable dissemination, and that those properties underwrote the Reformation, the Renaissance recovery of texts, and the cumulative character of science. Johns argues that fixity was not a gift of the machine. Early printed books were variable, pirated, incomplete, and widely distrusted. What eventually made some printed objects reliable enough for a natural philosopher to cite was a set of social institutions: the Stationers’ Company, the practices of licensing and registration, the reputations of particular printers and booksellers, and the procedures of bodies such as the Royal Society. Print culture, in his title’s sense, is not a culture that print automatically produced. It is a culture that people built, slowly and unevenly, because the raw output of the press was not yet something a careful reader could take on trust.

The empirical theatre is London, from the late sixteenth century through the early eighteenth, which is a deliberate choice of ground. London was the largest press in the English-speaking world and a centre of experimental philosophy, and it was also a market notorious for unauthorised editions. If fixity were a technical property, it ought to have been visible here as soon as the presses were busy. Johns finds instead a running battle over who had the right to print a given work, whose text was the true one, and how a reader at a distance could tell. The book is long because the battle was conducted in trials, petitions, prefaces, and laboratory records, and he wants the reader to watch credit being assembled rather than to take his word for the sociology.

Stationers, pirates, and the uncertainty of the printed object

The early chapters are a history of the book trade as a problem of order. The Stationers’ Company held, by royal privilege, a set of powers over who might print and over the registration of copies, in a sense of “copy” that is an ancestor of copyright but not yet that modern right. Those powers were justified as the protection of good order and of literary property, and they were exercised as the protection of a craft. They never eliminated unauthorised printing. Johns follows printers and booksellers who lived in the gaps: editions issued against the owner of a copy, texts altered to suit a market, political pamphlets produced when licensing collapsed, and the ordinary commercial habit of treating a successful book as an opportunity rather than as a sacred original. His celebrated cases of conflict in the trade, including the mid-century struggles in which printers used both law and politics to unseat one another’s privileges, are there to make a conceptual point. If the people who produced books spent this much energy fighting over which book was the authorised one, a historian cannot start from the assumption that “the printed book” was a stable object.

Readers, on this evidence, knew as much. A sophisticated reader did not pick up a printed text and accept it because type looks authoritative. He looked at who had printed it, which edition it claimed to be, whether the bookseller was known to be careful, and whether the work came recommended by someone whose judgement he had tested before. Johns treats that suspicion as rational. Compositors erred. Correctors were uneven. A sheet might be altered during a run, so that two copies of the “same” edition were not the same. Pirated editions abridged and corrupted. The appearance of print, far from ending the instability of script, created new forms of instability at larger scale. Eisenstein’s standardisation is, in this light, something the trade sometimes achieved and often claimed, not a feature one can read off the invention of movable type.

The Royal Society and the trustworthiness of experiment

The argument then moves from the trade to natural philosophy, which is where Johns’s quarrel with Eisenstein becomes a contribution to the history of science and not only to the history of books. Experimental knowledge in the later seventeenth century had a famous problem of distance. A result produced in a London room, before a handful of witnesses, had to travel to readers who were not there. Steven Shapin and others had shown how much of that travel depended on the social identity of gentlemen and on the conventions of virtual witnessing. Johns asks how print, specifically, could serve as the vehicle, given everything he has established about the unreliability of printed objects. The Royal Society’s registers, its licensing, its journal, and its relationships with particular printers were, on his account, attempts to build a channel in which a printed report could be believed because of who stood behind it, not because print as such was believable. The Society was manufacturing the very fixity that a simpler story would have attributed to the press.

This reframing changes what a scientific book is. It is not a transparent container for an idea that already existed in the author’s head. It is the product of a negotiation among author, printer, bookseller, and, in the philosophical cases, a society with its own credibility to protect. Errors and priority disputes look different once that negotiation is visible. So does the reader’s skill. Understanding a scientific text in this period required understanding the conditions of its production well enough to judge which claims the conditions could support. Johns spends much of the book on that skill, because it is the practical refutation of technological determinism. If readers had to be taught to trust print, trust was not in the type.

What he leaves standing of Eisenstein’s picture

A fair reading does not turn Johns into a claim that print changed nothing. The fights he documents were fights about a technology that had made texts abundant, public, and worth stealing. No one conducts a century of litigation over the right to copy manuscripts that take a year to reproduce by hand. The scale of the press is what made piracy a business and made a company of stationers a plausible policeman. Johns’s point is about the location of the causal work. The revolutionary consequences Eisenstein associated with the machine, reliable reference, cumulative correction, a public that could argue about the same page, arrived only where institutions had done the additional labour of stabilising the page. In places and genres where that labour was not done, print multiplied variants and quarrels. The history of early modern knowledge is therefore a history of those institutions as much as of ideas, and a history that stays inside one country’s book trade can show the labour in detail that a European synthesis has to skip.

The limitation is the mirror image of Eisenstein’s. She floated above the print shop. He is so deep inside London’s shops and its philosophical community that the European pattern, the sheer fact of dissemination across borders, the vernacular pamphlet in the Reformation, the reprint as the ordinary life of a text, can fall out of view. A critic of Johns can say that fixity was often good enough for the uses that mattered, even when a bibliographer can find variation, and that Eisenstein was describing that good-enough fixity at the level of scholarly practice. The debate between them, which continued in essays after both books, has settled into a productive division rather than a knockout. Macroscopic claims about the press as a condition of public knowledge remain Eisenstein’s. Microscopic claims about credit, piracy, and the construction of textual authority are Johns’s, and any macroscopic claim that forgets them will sound more automatic than the past deserves.

Why the argument travels

The reason the book is still read, including by people who will never specialise in the Stationers’ Company, is that its question keeps recurring for new media. A technology of reproduction arrives with a reputation for making information free, fixed, and self-authenticating. The users then discover variants, forgeries, decontextualised copies, and a crisis of credit, and they build institutions, slow, partial, and self-interested, to decide which copies count. Johns offers a historical case in which that sequence is fully visible, and in which the winning institutions were not neutral servants of truth. The Stationers policed texts in the crown’s interest and in their own. The Royal Society’s procedures favoured certain kinds of witness and certain kinds of claim. Fixity had a politics. That is a harder lesson than the lesson that print mattered, and it is the lesson his title is pointing at. The nature of the book is not given by the press. It is what a society has decided a book is, and it can be decided otherwise.

Why it matters here

On Folio’s card on the printing press, Johns is the classic critique. The card says that early print was less fixed and less reliable than Eisenstein claimed, that piracy and error were ordinary, and that trust in print had to be constructed. This book is that argument at full length, with London as the evidence. Dittmar’s finding that early print cities grew faster does not refute it, because growth does not require the bibliographical perfection Johns denies. Anderson’s imagined communities, on the other card where Eisenstein appears, assume a reading public that can treat a newspaper or a novel as the same object in many hands. Johns would ask that assumption to name the institutions which made that sameness credible, and to allow for publics that did not yet have them. Read him when the press is being asked to do too much by itself. The machine multiplied texts. People, companies, and societies decided which of the texts would count as knowledge.
