The 2010 meta-analysis found an average effect near zero, which left a puzzle: why did some studies find choice overload clearly while others found none? In 2015 Alexander Chernev, Ulf Böckenholt and Joseph Goodman published a different meta-analysis in the Journal of Consumer Psychology. They did not only average. They asked what differed between the studies that found overload and the ones that did not.

They pooled 99 observations from earlier research, with 7,202 people. They identified four factors that change how strongly a bigger assortment hurts. The first is choice set complexity: how complicated the set of options is. The second is decision task difficulty: how hard the task of deciding is. The third is preference uncertainty: not knowing what you want. The fourth is the decision goal, and in particular whether the person mainly wants to get it over with.

Each of the four factors had a reliable effect. Higher decision difficulty, a more complex set, more uncertainty about preferences and a stronger wish to minimise effort all made choice overload more likely. By the same logic, someone who knows exactly what they want, and is choosing among simple options, should show less overload.

They also checked how overload had been measured. Four measures used in earlier research, satisfaction or confidence, regret, deferring the choice, and likelihood of switching, turned out to be about equally powerful, so they can be used interchangeably. And when the four factors are taken into account, the overall effect of assortment size was significant. The authors say this runs against the earlier meta-analysis.

What this means in real life: how many options is too many depends on the person and the task. If you are shopping for something you know well, a big range is fine. If you face a complex choice and are unsure what you want, such as a pension plan or a first laptop, trimming the list or sorting it into categories will probably help.

Criticism: this is one meta-analysis built on studies of varying quality, and moderator analyses like this can only show patterns across studies, not prove cause. It is best read together with the 2010 paper, which found no reliable overall effect.
