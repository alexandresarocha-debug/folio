Background

Many Labs was a response to a suspicion that single laboratories were too free and too small. The same protocol would be run in many places, the analysis would be shared, and the field would learn which famous effects were properties of the protocol rather than properties of one successful room. Many Labs 3 included, among its tasks, an ego-depletion experiment. Miguel Vadillo, Natalie Gold and Magda Osman took that part of the project as their subject. Their 2018 paper in Royal Society Open Science, Searching for the Bottom of the Ego Well, reports a failure to uncover the effect. Folio's card places the paper with the other careful searches, beside Hagger's replication and the later paradigmatic test, rather than with the original claim.

The title is a small piece of wit and a methodological point. If willpower is a well, a serious search ought to be able to find the bottom, the place where a first draught makes the second harder. A search that comes up without the phenomenon is news about the well. It is also news about how a null, in a large collaborative dataset, ought to be read.

The question

Does the ego-depletion protocol embedded in Many Labs 3 show the impairment the resource theory predicts, once the data are examined as a test of that prediction rather than as one battery among many? Vadillo, Gold and Osman are asking a question the original Many Labs report could only ask in passing. A large multi-purpose project measures many effects and publishes a panorama. A panorama can mention a small or absent depletion effect without forcing the field to sit with what the absence means. This paper sits with it. The question is not whether some other task, on some other day, might still tire someone. It is whether this widely administered version of the classic sequence provides evidence that a limited resource was spent.

How they approached the data

The material is a shared dataset, which changes the ethics of the analysis. The participants, the depletion task and the comparison were fixed by the Many Labs 3 protocol before these authors wrote their paper. What Vadillo, Gold and Osman add is a focused examination of that contrast, with the descriptive and inferential detail a specialist critique requires. They look at whether people who received the depleting version of the first task performed worse on the second than people who received the control version. They look at whether any difference is large enough to take seriously, or so small that it is noise around no difference. And they look at the features of the task that would have to be in place before a null could be called a failed manipulation rather than a failed prediction.

That last look is the paper's distinctive care. A depletion study is interpretable only if the first task actually demanded more self-control in the depleting condition than in the control. If both versions were equally effortful, or if participants did not experience the difference the protocol assumed, then a flat result on the second task is ambiguous. The authors treat that ambiguity as something to inspect in the data one has, rather than as a blanket excuse available whenever a predicted impairment fails to arrive. A search for the bottom of the well includes checking whether the bucket was lowered.

What they found

They did not find a meaningful ego-depletion effect. Performance on the second task did not show the reliable cost of having first exercised self-control that the resource account predicts. The collaborative sample, spread across the sites Many Labs 3 had recruited, was the sort of sample in which a robust sequential impairment should have been visible. It was not visible. The title's failure is a failure of the phenomenon to appear, not a failure of the authors to look. Searching and not uncovering is the result they are willing to sign.

The paper's further point is about how easy it would be to keep looking until a bottom appears. A dataset of this size contains subgroups, alternative scorings and optional exclusions. Any of them might, by chance, draw a picture a believer would recognise. Vadillo, Gold and Osman are attentive to that temptation, because the history of depletion is a history of pictures drawn in small studies and then loved. Their conclusion stays with the planned contrast. The well, in this administration of the paradigm, did not yield a depletion effect one could responsibly teach as a finding.

How they read a null

A null result is not a proof that an effect is impossible. It is a statement about the sensitivity of a particular search. These authors read their null as informative because the search was large, the protocol was shared, and the prediction was the standard one. Under those conditions, absence of evidence is evidence about the protocol. It says that researchers who run this version of ego depletion should not expect the textbook impairment to appear, and that textbooks which present the impairment as a reliable laboratory fact are ahead of this dataset. The reading is modest in form and stern in consequence. Modest, because another protocol might still work. Stern, because the protocol in question was not an obscure one. It was the version a major replication project had thought worth running.

They also resist the consolation that a null is what a noisy measure always gives. Noise widens the uncertainty around an estimate. A large sample narrows it. The point of looking in Many Labs data, rather than in one more small classroom, is that the uncertainty has been narrowed enough for a large predicted effect to be in trouble. A theory can survive a noisy failure. It cannot, without amendment, survive a precise failure to see the thing it is named for. The bottom of the well, in their image, is where one would have felt the bucket strike. The bucket did not strike.

Limits

Many Labs 3 was not designed solely as a trial of ego depletion, and a task that rides along inside a long session is not identical to a task that is the whole afternoon. Fatigue, boredom and the company of unrelated measures could in principle mask a real effect or create a false one. Vadillo, Gold and Osman are limited to the administration they inherited. They can say what that administration shows. They cannot rebuild it into the paradigmatic test Vohs and colleagues would later run, with depletion as the point of the day and proponents in the room when the tasks were chosen. The 2018 paper is a thorough look at one major dataset. It is an item in the case against a general resource, not the whole case.

There is a limit, too, in what a reanalysis can do with beliefs. Job, Dweck and Walton had argued that depletion appears chiefly in people who think willpower is limited. Many Labs 3 was not built to sort its participants by that belief. A null average is compatible with a hidden subgroup effect the protocol never measured, and it is also compatible with there being nothing to hide. This paper does not pretend to have run the missing moderator analysis. It refuses to let an unmeasured moderator function as a reason to ignore the average. If the belief account is to rescue depletion, it has to be tested, not invoked.

Where the search sits in the card

The card's chronology has a shape. A striking claim in 1998. A belief-based alternative in 2010. A registered multi-lab null in 2016. This 2018 examination of another multi-lab dataset, reaching the same qualitative conclusion. A paradigmatic registered test in 2021 that finds the effect very small at best. Vadillo, Gold and Osman occupy the step in which a dataset collected for a broader purpose is asked, specifically, whether it contains the famous effect, and answers that it does not. That step matters because it is independent. It is not the Hagger protocol described a second time. A second search with a second design, failing in the same direction, is how a literature earns the right to change its mind.

The practical sentence does not grow between these papers, and it should not. Rest and motivation remain worth taking seriously. A fuel tank of the self, drained by a brief laboratory override and visible at the bottom of a well if only one looks hard enough, is what these searches did not find. The metaphor in the title is a good guide to the tone. The authors went looking. The honest report is that the looking failed to uncover the thing.

Why it matters here

Folio's card includes this paper because the replication story is not a single famous null. Hagger and colleagues showed that one preregistered protocol, in 23 laboratories, yielded an effect very close to zero. Vadillo, Gold and Osman showed that the depletion task inside Many Labs 3 yields the same kind of disappointment when it is examined on purpose. Vohs and colleagues later closed the objection that the tests were not the tests proponents would have chosen. Read this paper for the middle search, and for a model of how to talk about a null without theatrics. The bottom of the ego well was not found in the data they had. On the card's account, that failure is part of why ego depletion is now a lesson in the replication crisis rather than a lesson in the hydraulics of willpower.
