Miguel Vadillo, Natalie Gold, and Magda Osman went back to data that had been collected for a different question. Many Labs 3 had run 3,433 people through a half-hour session of mixed tasks, in an order that was scrambled. Two of those tasks are staples of ego-depletion research: a Stroop color-word test, and a set of anagrams, some of them impossible, on which people could quit when they liked, up to a cap of 240 seconds. The reanalysis kept the 2,062 people who faced the anagrams in the middle of the session. Of those, 1,003 did the anagrams before the Stroop, and 1,059 did the Stroop first. If the Stroop spends a limited resource, the second group should have given up on the anagrams sooner.

They did not. Which task came first made no detectable difference to persistence, p = 0.87. Restricting the comparison to people who started the anagrams immediately after the Stroop, with nothing in between, still showed nothing, p = 0.85. The Stroop itself was hard. In the original Many Labs 3 report its effect was large, d = 0.91, and it appeared in every subsample, so people really were inhibiting a habitual response. What did shift was patience across the session: anagrams that came up later were abandoned a little sooner. That correlation was about −0.05. It reached significance only because the sample was huge. Averaged across the 20 research teams, the depletion gap was indistinguishable from zero, p = 0.81, and the labs did not disagree with one another more than chance would allow.

The sample was large enough that an effect of the size earlier reviews had claimed for this pair of tasks should have appeared. The authors put their power to detect an effect as small as g = 0.18 above 0.98. A hard inhibition task can be measurably hard and still leave persistence on the next task untouched. When a small drop in sticking with the anagrams showed up at all, it tracked how far into a long session the person had gotten. For anyone explaining a quit as "I used up my willpower on the last thing," this dataset is a reason to look first at ordinary fatigue, and then to notice how small even that was.
