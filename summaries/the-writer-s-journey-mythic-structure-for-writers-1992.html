In 1985 Christopher Vogler was a story analyst at Disney. He wrote a seven-page memo, a practical guide to Joseph Campbell's The Hero with a Thousand Faces, and passed it around the studio. The memo already walks through films the way a development executive would. In Star Wars, Luke Skywalker is bored as a farm boy before anyone offers him the universe. Princess Leia's message to Obi-Wan is the call. Luke refuses, goes back to his aunt and uncle's farm, and finds that the stormtroopers have killed them. The refusal is over because the ordinary world is gone. Dorothy's first steps on the yellow brick road are Vogler's picture of the crossing: the hero is in the special world now, and the story has taken off. The cantina, where Luke meets Han Solo and makes an enemy of Jabba, is the phase of tests, allies, and enemies. The trash compactor in the Death Star, where Luke is pulled under and the audience is allowed to wonder if he is dead, is the ordeal.

The Writer's Journey (1992) is that memo opened into a book. Vogler sets the trip out as twelve stages: the ordinary world, the call, the refusal, the meeting with the mentor, the crossing of the threshold, tests and allies and enemies, the approach to the inmost cave, the ordeal, the reward, the road back, the resurrection, and the return with the elixir. Campbell's seventeen steps are shortened and renamed for people who have a second act to fix by Friday. The mentor hands over advice or a weapon, then can go only so far. Obi-Wan gives Luke his father's lightsaber. The ordeal is the black moment, played so that the people outside the cave, which is to say the audience, fear the hero may not come out.

Used as a set of questions, the twelve stages are a good diagnostic. Does this person have a life before the problem? Is there a point of no return? Is the worst moment actually worst, or only noisy? Does the ending bring anything back that the opening world can use? Used as a checklist that every scene must satisfy in order, the same list flattens films that were never built as quests. A story can be a debate, a heist, a marriage, or a collapse, and still be finished, without a mentor and an elixir. Vogler's own examples work because the films already have a departure and a test. The memo shows you where to look. It does not prove that every script is Star Wars with the names changed.
