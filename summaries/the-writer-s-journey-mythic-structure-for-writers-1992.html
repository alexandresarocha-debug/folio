A studio memo that became a template

Christopher Vogler's The Writer's Journey, which grew out of a memo he wrote as a story analyst in the 1980s and was published as a book in 1992, is the form in which Joseph Campbell's monomyth reached working screenwriters. Campbell's The Hero with a Thousand Faces had described a hero who leaves the ordinary world, is tested in a region of wonder, and returns with a boon. The book was long, metaphysical, and full of myths retold as psychology. A development office cannot hand a novelist-mythographer to a writer on a deadline. Vogler condensed the circuit into a sequence a film could be checked against, and he translated the vocabulary of initiation into the vocabulary of acts, protagonists, and scenes. The condensation is the argument. He claims that the monomyth is not only a description of old stories but a practical design for new ones, and that a writer who can see the stages can see what a stalled script is missing.

The debt to Campbell is open, and so is the change of purpose. Campbell was trying to say what myths mean, using Jung and the comparative method, and he was willing to let a stage be a symbol rather than a scene. Vogler is trying to say what a mainstream story needs in order to feel complete to an audience trained on adventure. Meaning, in his version, is mostly structural. A stage earns its place if the audience feels the story turn when the stage arrives. The metaphysical return, the hero as master of two worlds in a spiritual sense, thins out into a visible change that the last act can show. That thinning is why the book could travel through studios, and it is why readers who love Campbell sometimes find Vogler blunt. Bluntness is the craft decision. A tool has to be usable on a Wednesday draft.

The twelve stages, and what each one is for

Vogler's sequence has twelve stages. The hero is introduced in the ordinary world, so that departure will be a change rather than a beginning from nowhere. A call to adventure disturbs that world. The hero refuses, or hesitates, which lets the audience feel the cost of leaving. A meeting with a mentor supplies advice, a gift, or a push. The hero then crosses the first threshold into the special world, and the story commits itself. In that world come tests, allies, and enemies, the material of the long middle. An approach to the inmost cave tightens the danger towards a central place. The ordeal is the crisis, a confrontation with death or the threat of it. The reward follows, something seized or granted. The road back is the decision to return, often under pressure. A resurrection, near the end, tests whether the hero has really changed. The return with the elixir brings the benefit home, to the ordinary world or to whoever was left there.

Read as a list, the stages look like a checklist, and the book is often taught as one. Read as an argument, they are a theory of what an audience has to be shown. The ordinary world is there so the special world can be felt as special. The refusal is there so the crossing is a choice. The mentor is there because Vogler thinks audiences accept a leap more readily when someone wiser has named it. The ordeal sits in the centre because a story that only accumulates incidents never makes the audience fear a real loss. The resurrection is his most characteristic addition to the popular memory of Campbell. Campbell already had a difficult return. Vogler wants a second crisis late in the story, so that the final act is not a commute back from a victory already won. Films that feel as if they end twice are often following this advice, whether or not the writer has read it.

What the condensation keeps and what it drops

The useful part of the condensation is the set of questions it forces. Who is the hero before the trouble, in a life the audience can recognise? What would persuade that person to stay home? What is the threshold, the point after which the old life is operationally gone? Who helps, and is the help a person, an object, or a piece of knowledge? What is the ordeal a test of, rather than merely a large fight? What would count as coming home, and what would be different there because the hero left? A script that cannot answer these is often a script whose incidents are exciting and whose shape is blank. Vogler's book is a machine for finding the blank. In that limited office it has earned its place in the industry, and the place is visible in how many adventure films share a rhythm even when their surfaces differ.

The cost of the condensation is the part of Campbell that could not be scheduled. Campbell's stages included meetings, with a goddess, with a temptress, with a father, that were interpretations of mythic images, not slots a writer must fill. He also allowed the hero to refuse the return, and he spent half the book on a cosmogonic cycle, the world itself emerging and dissolving, of which the hero's trip was a local case. Vogler has little use for a hero who stays in the other world, because a film that refuses to come home often feels, to the audience he is writing for, unfinished. He has still less use for cosmology. The twelve stages are a hero's employment, not a theory of how worlds are born. Writers who take the template as the whole of the monomyth have inherited the employment and missed the speculation. That may be a relief. It is also a narrowing, and it concentrates the pattern's biases. The default hero remains a lone quester. Allies and enemies are functions of the quester's progress. The elixir is something one person brings back to a community that waited.

A pattern that began to confirm itself

Once the template was in circulation, stories built to it started to look like evidence for it. George Lucas had already pointed at Campbell as an influence on Star Wars. Vogler made the influence into a memo that other films could follow without anyone reading the myths. A viewer who then notices the pattern everywhere is often noticing a local tradition, the tradition of late-twentieth-century commercial storytelling, rather than a universal law. Vogler is franker about this than his imitators. He presents the stages as a form, comparable to other forms, and he spends time on exceptions, on stories that scramble the order or that distribute the hero's functions across several people. The frankness is easy to skip when the twelve names are printed on a poster. The poster is not the book. The book is an argument that a form with a beginning in the ordinary world, a death-and-return in the middle and at the end, and a gift brought home will feel satisfying for reasons that are older than the studio, and that a writer may use the feeling without pretending to have discovered the key to all narratives.

The argument is weakest where it borrows Campbell's authority and strongest where it stays inside craft. It cannot show, and does not really try to show, that the world's myths share these twelve stages. Campbell's own evidence was selective, and Vogler's evidence is the films that already move like adventures. What he can show is that writers who cannot say what their hero is refusing, what the ordeal costs, or what the return is for, are often writers whose third act collapses. The stages are a diagnostic. Used as a diagnostic, the book is a descendant of Aristotle's demand that incidents follow, and of Freytag's demand that a play know where its turn is, translated into a Hollywood dialect. Used as a law, it produces the very sameness its users then congratulate themselves for finding in human nature.

Why it matters here

The idea you are reading is the hero's journey together with its critics. Campbell states the monomyth as a claim about world myth and about the psyche. This book is how that claim became a twelve-stage template: ordinary world, call, refusal, mentor, threshold, tests, approach, ordeal, reward, road back, resurrection, return with the elixir. The template is what most films mean by the hero's journey, and it is more orderly, more cinematic, and less metaphysical than the book it adapts. The critics of the monomyth apply here with extra force, because a memo that tells writers to hit the stages will flatten stories by design, and will then be confirmed by the stories that obeyed. The idea's advice is the right use of Vogler. Treat the stages as one pattern among many, a set of questions about departure and return, not as a universal law. Propp's functions, counted on a defined body of tales, are the empirical contrast. Aristotle's causal plot is the older demand that a shape has to be necessary, not merely familiar.
