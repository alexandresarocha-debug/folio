Jesse Graham, Jonathan Haidt, and Brian Nosek tested, in four ways, whether liberals and conservatives treat different concerns as moral. The first way was a direct question. Adults who had registered at the Project Implicit website, 1,613 of them, with a median age of 29, rated how relevant various concerns were when they made a moral judgment. Nine hundred two described themselves as liberal, 366 as moderate, and 264 as conservative. Eighty-one skipped the politics question. Liberals rated harm and fairness as more morally relevant than loyalty, authority, and purity. Conservatives rated harm and fairness highly too, and they rated loyalty, authority, and purity higher than liberals did. The same pattern appeared among the American respondents, the British respondents, and the respondents from other countries.

The fourth study left the website and counted words. Graham, Haidt, and Nosek collected 69 Unitarian sermons and 34 Southern Baptist sermons given between 1994 and 2006, most of them from 2003 onward, and ran them through a dictionary of foundation words. The Unitarian sermons totaled 177,629 words. The Baptist sermons totaled 136,706. Harm words were 0.44% of the Unitarian text and 0.26% of the Baptist text. Fairness words were 0.22% and 0.10%. Authority words were 0.46% and 0.98%. Purity words were 0.25% and 0.64%. Raw mentions of the in-group were more common in the liberal sermons, 0.52% against 0.22%. When raters read the distinctive words in context and scored whether the speaker was supporting the foundation, that loyalty gap reversed: Baptist uses endorsed it more than Unitarian uses did.

The pattern is useful in an argument that will not move. A dispute about punishment, sex, a flag, or a family duty is often a dispute about which of the five concerns is allowed to count as a moral one. One side hears the other adding taboos, or ignoring harm. These samples are volunteers on a research website, and the sermons are two denominations chosen because they sit at the political edges of American churches. Inside those groups, the same split showed up when people rated their own morals and when ministers spoke to their own congregations.
