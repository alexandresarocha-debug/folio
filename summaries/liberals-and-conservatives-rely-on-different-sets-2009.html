A theory that makes a measurable prediction

Liberals and conservatives rely on different sets of moral foundations, published in 2009 by Jesse Graham, Jonathan Haidt and Brian Nosek, is the paper that turns moral foundations theory into a claim with numbers attached. The theory, developed in Haidt's work and later popularised in The Righteous Mind, says that moral judgement rests on several intuitive foundations, among them care, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity. It also says that political ideologies differ in which of these they are willing to treat as moral. Liberals, in the American sense of the word, are predicted to rest most of their morality on care and fairness. Conservatives are predicted to endorse those two as well and, in addition, to give real moral weight to loyalty, authority and sanctity. The paper's job is to test that pattern, and to test it in more than one way, so that a single questionnaire cannot be the whole of the evidence.

The prediction is distinctive because it is not the prediction that the two sides value morality differently in amount. A familiar caricature says that conservatives care less about harm, or that liberals care less about order. Graham, Haidt and Nosek predict something more specific. On care and fairness, the two groups should differ modestly if at all, and liberals may even endorse them more strongly. On loyalty, authority and sanctity, conservatives should endorse them more strongly, so that the conservative profile is broader rather than simply shifted. A result in which conservatives scored lower on everything would embarrass the theory. A result in which the groups differed only by a general tendency to agree with moral-sounding sentences would embarrass it too. The pattern has a shape, and the shape is the claim.

How the endorsement was measured

The studies ask people about moral relevance and about moral judgement. Relevance questions ask how much a consideration, such as whether someone suffered, whether someone was disloyal, or whether someone did something degrading, has to do with deciding that an action is right or wrong. Judgement questions ask people to evaluate particular actions that embody one foundation or another. The distinction matters. A person might admit that loyalty is psychologically noticeable and still deny that it is a moral reason. A person might condemn a concrete act of disrespect while describing their principles in the language of harm alone. Using both kinds of question is a way of checking that the liberal-conservative difference is not an artefact of one wording.

The paper also tries to step outside self-report of a familiar kind, because a theory of implicit moral difference has to worry that people are describing the ideology they know they are supposed to have. If liberals have learned to talk about compassion and conservatives have learned to talk about tradition, a questionnaire will find the lessons. Graham, Haidt and Nosek therefore look at the pattern across methods that are not all the same direct self-description, and they look at it across large numbers of respondents who place themselves on the political spectrum. The argument does not require that every method be immune to self-presentation. It requires that the same shape keep appearing, so that the shape is harder to dismiss as the vocabulary of one item set. The card's summary is the shape they report. Liberals emphasise care and fairness. Conservatives draw more evenly on all of the foundations.

What the pattern does and does not explain

Taken at face value, the pattern changes the diagnosis of political disagreement. If the two sides shared a foundation and differed only in the policies they thought served it, argument could proceed by evidence about consequences. If they build on different sets of foundations, then a policy argument is often a disagreement about whether a consideration is a moral consideration at all. A liberal speaker who translates every issue into harm and fairness is, on this evidence, speaking the whole of their morality. A conservative listener who also hears a question of loyalty or sanctity is not being evasive in refusing the translation. They are responding to a foundation the speaker has classified as out of bounds. The paper supplies the descriptive premise for an argument Haidt then writes at book length: some of what looks like bad faith is a difference in the catalogue.

The paper is also, more quietly, a restriction on that argument. The differences it measures are differences in endorsement and in judgement among people who are willing to say where they stand politically, mostly in the United States, on items written to express the five foundations. That is a real and replicable-looking pattern within those limits. It is not a demonstration that the foundations are innate, that they are modular, that they exhaust morality, or that political identity reduces to them. A person can endorse sanctity on a questionnaire and still vote on prices and safety. A tradition can talk in the language of loyalty and still be driven by interests the questionnaire does not ask about. The authors' claim is about the moral considerations people say they rely on. The further story about evolution and about the blindness of groups is additional.

The measurement argument against the theory

Critics have pressed hardest on whether the five foundations are measured as five. Items designed for separate foundations can correlate, and the statistical separation between them is a matter of dispute. If loyalty, authority and sanctity move together as a single traditionalist cluster, then the tidy picture of five foundations, two of them shared and three of them distinctively conservative, overstates the structure of the moral world even if it fits the structure of the scale. Psychometric problems of this kind do not erase the political difference. Liberals and conservatives do answer these questions differently, and the difference is not confined to a single slogan. They do threaten the interpretation in which each foundation is a distinct psychological system with its own evolutionary history. A cluster of related conservative concerns is a finding. A set of innate moral organs is a theory laid on top of the finding.

There is a further, conceptual objection that the paper's design cannot answer internally. The items have to specify, in advance, what counts as a care consideration or a sanctity consideration. If a respondent sees harm inside an item the experimenters filed under sanctity, they may condemn it for a reason the scoring will mislabel. That is the opening through which a harm-based rival theory, of the kind Kurt Gray and his colleagues have defended, criticises the whole programme. The objection does not say the data are noisy. It says the data are coded in the theory's own language, so agreement with the theory is partly built into the coding. A fair reading of Graham, Haidt and Nosek holds both points. The pattern in political endorsement is informative, and the inference from that pattern to five distinct foundations is underdetermined by the questionnaires that produce it.

What the paper contributes anyway

Even with those limits, the paper does a job that the books cannot do. It specifies a prediction sharp enough to be disappointing, and it reports a pattern that matches the prediction's shape: not a simple story in which one side is more moral, but a story in which one side moralises a wider set of considerations. Later work can argue about factor structure, about whether the same shape appears in other countries, and about whether liberty, added later, belongs in the set. Those arguments inherit a target. Without this paper and its kin, moral foundations theory would be a suggestive vocabulary. With it, the vocabulary is committed to a distribution of answers that other researchers can, and do, reanalyse.

The paper also clarifies the sense in which the theory is social rather than only individual. The measurements are of people sorted by political identity, and political identity is a social fact. The foundations are being used as a way to describe the moral culture of two broad coalitions. That is a more modest use than the claim that each person's mind contains five modules, and it is the use the data most directly support. Liberals and conservatives, as groups who answer these instruments, rely on different sets. The title is the result. The theory's heavier claims about innateness and about the futility of reason are not results of this design, and they should not be smuggled back in as if the surveys had established them.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the elephant and the rider cites this paper for the empirical pattern that moral foundations theory adds to the older claim about intuition and reasoning. The card reports that survey studies find liberals emphasising care and fairness, and conservatives drawing more evenly on all the foundations. Graham, Haidt and Nosek are the study behind that sentence. The card then notes that critics doubt the foundations are as distinct or as innate as claimed, and that the measurement has psychometric problems. Those doubts are part of reading this paper well, not a reason to skip it. The 2001 paper argues that intuitions come first. This paper argues that the intuitions people moralise differ by politics, and shows the shape of the difference. The Righteous Mind weaves the two arguments into a single public account. A reader who wants the measurement without the weave should stay with Graham, Haidt and Nosek, and should keep the measurement at the size the questionnaires earn.
