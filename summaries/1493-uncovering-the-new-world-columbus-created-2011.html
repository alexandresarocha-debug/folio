On September 13, 1845, potato blight was reported in Ireland. Cormac Ó Gráda has estimated that Irish farmers had planted about 2.1 million acres of potatoes that year. Within two months, Phytophthora infestans destroyed the equivalent of half a million to three-quarters of a million of those acres. The next year was worse, and the year after that. The attack did not ease until 1852. A million or more people died. Within a decade, two million more had left Ireland, almost three-quarters of them for the United States. As late as the 1960s, Ireland's population was about half of what it had been in 1840. Charles C. Mann sets that famine against the fact that made it possible. By the late eighteenth century, in much of northern Europe, the Andean potato had become the food that ended routine famine. Roughly 40 percent of the Irish ate no solid food other than potatoes. The figure was between 10 and 30 percent in the Netherlands, Belgium, Prussia, and perhaps Poland.

The fertilizer arrived by the same ocean. The Chincha Islands, off Peru, were covered in seabird guano to a depth of as much as 150 feet. After Justus von Liebig described plants' need for nitrogen in 1840, farmers bought the guano and saw yields double or triple. In forty years Peru exported about 13 million tons of it, most of it dug by Chinese workers held in conditions Mann describes as slavery. In 1856 the United States Congress passed the Guano Islands Act, letting Americans claim guano deposits they found. Over the next half century, U.S. merchants claimed 94 islands, cays, and atolls. Mann's sequence is the potato, then concentrated fertilizer, then arsenic compounds once the Colorado potato beetle hit the new monoculture. He is careful about one link in the chain. The blight is widely thought to have reached Europe on the guano ships from Peru, first breaking out at Kortrijk in the summer of 1845. He says that will never be proved.

The practical lesson is that a crop which removes an old limit creates a new one. Europe's food supply became large enough to support more people, more cities, and more industry. It also became a clone, planted in huge blocks of genetically identical potatoes, fed with a mineral dug on the other side of the world, and open to any pest that could cross the water. A farmer who plants one variety, buys fertility by the ton, and sprays whatever the latest beetle survives is living inside the system Mann traces to these years. The gain was real. So was the famine that followed when the plant failed everywhere at once.
