A name for the world after the oceans opened

Charles Mann’s 1493 is the narrative sequel to 1491, and it is also a sequel, in a different register, to Alfred Crosby. Where 1491 asked how many people lived in the Americas before Columbus, and how completely epidemic and conquest then shattered that world, 1493 asks what kind of planet was created once the shatterings and the transfers had begun. Mann borrows a geological mood for the answer. He calls the new age the Homogenocene: an era in which creatures that evolution had kept apart for millions of years were thrown into the same fields, the same harbours, and the same bodies, until the biotas of the continents started to resemble one another. Globalisation, on this telling, is not a late-twentieth-century invention. It is the ecological fact that follows from 1492, and Columbus is less a discoverer than the accidental author of a permanent mixture.

The book is organised as a set of journeys around that mixture rather than as a single chronology. An introduction and an opening chapter on two monuments set the terms. Then four parts carry the reader across the Atlantic, across the Pacific, through Europe’s new relationship with the rest of the world, and through Africa’s. A coda returns to a small place and to the currents, literal and historical, that now run through it. Mann is a science journalist writing history. He builds each part from places, parasites, commodities, and arguments with specialists. The method has a risk, which is that a vivid case will stand in for a continent, and a benefit, which is that the reader can see a mechanism, a mosquito, a worm, a vein of silver, doing work that a generalisation would only name.

The Atlantic: tobacco, worms, and fever

The Atlantic part begins on the tobacco coast, the Chesapeake world that English promoters expected to be a simpler, richer version of home. Mann’s theme is that the colonists did not walk into an empty ecological theatre. They walked into a set of relationships they did not know how to see. Tobacco made the colony economically real and, at the same time, exhausted soils and locked it into a labour system that English servants could not sustain. He is also interested in less famous passengers. Earthworms, largely absent from the glaciated north of the continent since the ice ages, arrived in ballast and in root balls and began to rewrite forest floors, accelerating the decomposition of leaf litter and changing what could grow. The point of such a detail is methodological. The Columbian Exchange is not only the famous list of potatoes, horses, and smallpox. It is a crowd of organisms whose effects are local, physical, and easy to miss if one is looking only at treaties.

The chapter he calls Evil Air turns the same logic onto disease and geopolitics. Falciparum malaria and yellow fever, both tied to African environments and to the mosquito, became powers in the tropical Americas. They killed Europeans in dreadful proportions, killed indigenous peoples, and killed African newcomers too, though populations from regions where falciparum had long been present carried inherited and acquired forms of resistance that gave them a better chance of surviving the plantation zones. Mann’s argument is that this differential mortality shaped the map of empire. It helped determine where northern Europeans could settle and persist, where sugar colonies could be staffed only by the continuous importation of enslaved Africans, and why European armies in the Caribbean and the Gulf were so often defeated by fever before they were defeated by enemies. The American Revolution and the Haitian Revolution both sit, in this account, inside a disease regime. Political history that ignores the mosquito misreads who could occupy what. Crosby supplied the category of the exchange. Mann supplies a set of cases in which the category decides wars.

The Pacific: silver, silk, and Chinese calories

The Pacific part is Mann’s most important widening of the usual Atlantic story. Silver from American mines, above all the great mountain at Potosí, did not stop in Seville. A large share of it crossed the Pacific on the Manila galleons and entered China, where demand for silver, including demand created by the conversion of imperial taxes onto a silver basis, made the metal the product China would open its doors to. In return, silk and other Chinese goods flowed back towards Mexico and beyond. Mann’s phrase for the pairing is silk for silver, and he splits it across two chapters. The first is about the mines, the galleons, and the strange global currency system in which a Bolivian mountain underwrote commerce in Fujian. The second is about what travelled with that commerce besides cloth and coin: sweet potato and maize into China, where they became staples on hillsides that rice could not hold, and tobacco, the lovesick grass of his chapter title, into a new social life. American crops in Chinese soils are, for Mann, as consequential as American silver in Chinese treasuries. One remade the budget of the state. The other remade the carrying capacity of the countryside.

This is where 1493 does something Crosby’s Atlantic frame only pointed towards. The Columbian Exchange is usually taught as a two-sided Atlantic affair. Mann insists on a Pacific loop that ties the Andes, Mexico, Manila, and coastal China into one circuit by the late sixteenth century. A peasant in the Chinese interior eating sweet potatoes and a miner in the high Andes dying to produce the silver that paid for Chinese goods are in the same history. So are the ecological warnings he will not drop. Hillsides planted to New World crops could feed a larger population and could also fail, erode, and flood. The exchange raises ceilings and invents new ways to fall through them.

Europe and the agro-industrial complex

The European part gathers two commodities that look, at first, unrelated: the potato and guano. Mann’s potato chapter runs from Andean domestication and the ingenuity of freeze-drying in the highlands, through European resistance and promotion, to the Irish famine. He treats the famine as a modern catastrophe rather than a medieval leftover. A single clonal crop, adopted because it was the best calorie engine peasant Europe had ever seen, met a pathogen, Phytophthora infestans, that the same global mixing had made mobile. The potato had helped to build the population. The blight showed what monoculture at that scale costs. He sets beside this the nineteenth-century trade in Peruvian guano and nitrates, the fertiliser that let European and other farmers push yields until industrial chemistry, in the following century, learned to fix nitrogen on its own. The agro-industrial complex of his chapter title is this stacking: New World crops, New World nutrients, Old World cities growing on the result.

Black gold, the following chapter, is rubber. Mann follows it from the Amazonian forests, where it was a wild product tapped under brutal labour regimes, to the seeds and seedlings by which it left the Americas, and on to the plantations of Southeast Asia that broke the Amazonian monopoly. He is interested in the biological irony. South American leaf blight kept plantation rubber from thriving in its own native region, while the tree, freed from its pests, became an industrial crop elsewhere. Fordlandia, the American attempt to grow rubber in the Amazon anyway, appears as a parable of ignoring ecology. The chapter ties a raw material of the industrial age back to the homogenocene. The car is partly an Amazonian organism, redistributed by empire.

Africa, and the people who were exchanged

The African part refuses to leave the human beings of the exchange inside the word “labour”. Mann writes about the societies built under slavery and against it: about the mixing of peoples in American port cities, and about maroon communities, the forests of fugitives who established polities in the hinterlands of plantation colonies from Brazil to Suriname and the Caribbean. The emphasis falls on African initiative. Enslaved and self-freed people carried rice-growing and other agricultural knowledge that made certain colonies viable, maintained religious and kinship forms under extreme pressure, and, where geography allowed, built communities the colonial state could not easily reduce. Disease is present again. The same fevers that killed Europeans made some interior refuges more defensible. A history of the exchange that contains only crops and germs, without these political creations, has left out the agents who did much of the growing and much of the resisting.

The coda, set in a Philippine fishing community, is Mann’s way of saying that the process has not closed. Currents still move organisms and people. Local decisions about what to plant and what to eat are still decisions inside a world Columbus accidentally unified. He is not sentimental about that unification. Homogenocene is a descriptive term with a critical edge. Mixture brought potatoes to the hungry and malaria to the previously unexposed, silver to world trade and slavery to the plantations, guano to tired fields and blight to monocultures. The book’s achievement is to hold those outcomes in one narrative without averaging them into a bland story of connection.

Why it matters here

Folio’s Columbian Exchange card names 1493 as the lively modern retelling of Crosby, the one that carries the argument into silver flows to China, malaria, and rubber. That list is exactly the book’s own route: Pacific silver in the second part, evil air in the first, rubber among the commodities of the European and industrial chapters, with the African chapters making sure the forced migration of people is part of the same plot. Nunn and Qian measure one element of Mann’s European story, the potato’s contribution to population and urbanisation, and they are more cautious than any narrative can be about the size of the effect. Mann supplies what their identification strategy leaves out: the institutions, the famines, the Pacific, the maroons. Read him after Crosby if you want to see the exchange as a world system rather than as an Atlantic list, and read him remembering that a case, however well reported, is not yet an estimate.
