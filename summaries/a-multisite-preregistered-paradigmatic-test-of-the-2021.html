Background

The 2016 replication of ego depletion had a vulnerable spot, and the original theorists pointed at it. A null result, they argued, shows nothing about a limited resource if the first task was too weak to drain one. A fair test would use a procedure the theory's defenders recognise as the real phenomenon, registered in advance, and run at a scale that could detect a small effect as well as a large one. Kathleen Vohs, Brandon Schmeichel and a multi-site collaboration published that test in 2021, in Psychological Science, under the title A Multisite Preregistered Paradigmatic Test of the Ego-Depletion Effect. Folio's card records the outcome in a single careful phrase. The effect was very small at best.

Paradigmatic is the word that distinguishes this paper from a generic repeat. The protocol was chosen to be a central, agreed exemplar of the original claim, not a peripheral variant easy to disown. If depletion is a real sequential impairment of self-control, this is the sort of experiment in which it ought to appear. The paper is the field taking the 2016 objection seriously and paying the cost of a better experiment.

The question

When the manipulation is one that proponents accept as depleting, and when the analysis is frozen before anyone sees the outcome, does a first act of self-control still impair a second? The question is the 1998 question with the escape hatches closed. A positive result would revive the resource account under the conditions its defenders had asked for. A null, or a result too small to carry the theory's original weight, would be much harder to attribute to a miserly task. Vohs and colleagues were explicit that the study was built to be that kind of decisive procedure, the kind that survives the sentence we would have found it if the task had been right.

How the test was set up

Multisite and preregistered are the two constraints that define the design. Several laboratories ran one protocol, so that the result would not be the property of a single room. The protocol, the exclusions and the analysis were specified in advance, so that the result would not be the property of a comparison chosen afterwards. The word paradigmatic names a third constraint, which is social as much as statistical. The tasks were selected, with the involvement of researchers who had produced and defended depletion effects, to represent the paradigm rather than to ambush it. A first task requires self-control. A comparison task matches it in superficial demands without requiring the same override. A second task measures the performance or persistence the resource account says should suffer. The prediction is the classic one. People who have just controlled themselves should do worse.

The sample was large, because the debate had moved from whether a huge effect exists to whether any reliable effect exists. A small study can confirm a dramatic claim and cannot police a modest one. A large preregistered study can do the policing. It can also disappoint both sides in a precise way. It can find a difference that is statistically detectable and still far too small to justify the metaphor of a tank running dry after a brief exertion. The paper was equipped to make that distinction, which is why the card's phrase, very small at best, is a possible outcome the design can actually express.

What they found

The depletion effect in the combined data was very small at best. The difference between people who had just exercised self-control and people who had not was not the substantial impairment the early literature and the popular books had described. Depending on the exact summary one prefers, the result sits at the edge of a tiny positive effect or at the edge of no effect, and it does not sit anywhere near the effect a reader of the 1998 studies would have expected to sail through a sample of this size. The laboratories had run a procedure designed to be strong enough. Strength of procedure did not restore strength of effect.

That outcome closes the particular escape route the 2016 result had left open. It is no longer comfortable to say that the registered failures used a task too gentle to count. A paradigmatic task had been used, by arrangement with the idea's defenders, and the tank had not appreciably emptied. Whatever sequential changes in effort the laboratories did observe were too slight to carry the theory in its ambitious form. A limited resource that produces only a whisper of impairment, under conditions arranged to make it shout, is not the resource the programme advertised.

How the authors read the result

Vohs, Schmeichel and their collaborators present the finding as a constraint on theory, not as a joke at a literature's expense. The data are compatible with a very small effect. They are not compatible with the effect size that would make ego depletion a general, practically important fact about the next half-hour of a person's self-control. The reading they encourage is revision or retirement of the strong resource claim. Revision would mean a theory that predicts impairment only in narrow conditions, and that says in advance what those conditions are. Retirement would mean stopping the use of depletion as an explanation whenever two hard tasks occur in sequence. The paper does not force a choice between those two by logic alone. It removes the third option, which was to keep the strong claim and blame the tests.

They also model a kind of adjudication the crisis had called for. Proponents helped define the paradigm, sceptics helped hold the registration, and the number was everyone's number. That procedure does not guarantee truth. It guarantees that the argument afterwards is about a shared observation. The 2021 paper is the shared observation for this debate. Later claims that depletion is robust have to climb over it, with a new prediction, rather than around it, with a complaint about the 2016 task.

Limits

Even a paradigmatic test is one paradigm. A sequence of two laboratory tasks, however carefully chosen, does not exhaust the ways self-control might fatigue across a day of temptation, sleep loss and hard work. The paper speaks to the sequential laboratory effect that made ego depletion famous. It does not speak to every folk report of being spent, and it should not be quoted as a finding that effort is free. People get tired. Motivation shifts. Beliefs about willpower, as Job, Dweck and Walton proposed, may change how a second task feels. None of those truths is a limited resource of the sort the 1998 theory inferred from cookies and puzzles. Keeping them distinct is the limit and the lesson.

There is a statistical limit in the phrase at best. A very small effect is hard to tell from zero, and a large sample can make a trivial difference look like a detection. The card's caution, very small at best, refuses to promote a whisper into a vindication. Readers who need the strong theory are not entitled to round a whisper up. Readers who want a slogan that nothing sequential ever happens to effort are not entitled to round it down into a universal claim about human stamina. The paper's precision is the point. The effect the field had believed in was large. The effect the fair test delivered was not.

Where this leaves the idea

After 2021 the resource account is in a narrow place. The original studies remain on the record. The two large preregistered examinations, Hagger's and this one, do not reproduce the effect at a size that would explain them. Publication bias and flexible analysis are the standing explanation for that discrepancy, and they are an explanation the crisis made familiar. Alternative theories that never required a fuel tank, a shift in motivation and priority, a belief that willpower runs out, are the standing candidates for whatever real sequential phenomena remain. Vadillo, Gold and Osman's look at Many Labs 3 belongs with the failed searches rather than with the rescues.

The practical sentence the card permits is smaller than the sentence the 1998 paper hoped to earn. Rest matters. Motivation matters. There is no reliable evidence that willpower is a fuel tank drained by a short act of laboratory self-control. This study is a large part of why that sentence can be said without a loophole for a better task. The better task was the brief.

Why it matters here

The ego-depletion card would be unfinished if it stopped in 2016, because a fair objection was still standing. This paper is the answer to the objection. A multisite, preregistered, paradigmatic test, built so that a real depletion effect would have a clean chance to appear, found an effect that was very small at best. Together with Hagger's result it is why the card tells you to treat the tank as unproven. Read the 1998 paper for the claim in its original force. Read Job, Dweck and Walton if you want a rival that locates the phenomenon in belief. Read this paper for the test that was designed to give the claim every reasonable advantage, and for the modesty of what that advantage produced.
