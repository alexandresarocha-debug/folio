The 'ecocide' theory holds that the Rapanui overexploited their island and suffered a major population collapse in the 1600s. J. Víctor Moreno-Mayar and an international team tested this with ancient DNA, working with Rapanui community representatives.

They radiocarbon dated and sequenced the whole genomes of 15 Rapanui individuals who lived between about 1670 and 1950, from collections held at the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris. The individuals were Polynesian in origin and most closely related to present-day Rapanui, a finding the authors say will help repatriation efforts.

Genomes carry traces of past population size. Using reconstructions of effective population size and extensive simulations, the team rejected a scenario in which the population went through a severe bottleneck during the 1600s, as the ecocide theory proposes. Instead, the population appears to have grown steadily until the 1860s.

The study also addressed a second debate. Both ancient and present-day Rapanui carry about 10% Native American ancestry, and the team estimated that the mixing occurred around 1250 to 1430, centuries before Europeans reached the island. That points to contact with the Americas well before Columbus.

The real catastrophe was later. European visitors killed islanders and brought new diseases; in the 1860s Peruvian slave raiders kidnapped a third of the population, and after a smallpox outbreak the population fell to an estimated 110 people.

What this means in real life: ancient DNA can test historical narratives directly, and studies done with descendant communities can also help return ancestors' remains.

Caution: the sample is small, and all the individuals lived from the late 1600s onwards, so the genetic inferences about earlier centuries rely on models. Earlier ancient-DNA studies had not detected the Native American ancestry, and the timing of contact is still debated.
