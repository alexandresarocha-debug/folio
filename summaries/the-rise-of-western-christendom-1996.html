Augustine, a monk sent by Pope Gregory I, reached Kent in 597. In 601 Gregory sent further orders with the missionary Mellitus. Bede copies them. The temples of the idols were to stay standing. The idols were to be destroyed, the buildings sprinkled with holy water, altars set up, and relics placed inside. Where people had sacrificed oxen, they were to feast on the days of the saints. Gregory's reason, as Bede reports it, is practical: "it is certainly impossible to eradicate all errors from obstinate minds at one stroke." Peter Brown's The Rise of Western Christendom (1996) treats decisions of this kind as the way a church was actually built in the north. A religion formed in Roman cities had to be fitted to a rural society, and the bishop of Rome was telling his own mission to do the fitting.

The same local work appears as a fight over the calendar. At Whitby in 664, King Oswiu of Northumbria had to choose between two dates for Easter. Wilfrid of York, who lived from 634 to 709, argued for the Roman calculation and the Roman form of the tonsure. Monks trained in the Irish tradition defended the date they already kept. Oswiu chose the Roman date. Each side held that its own custom was the true practice of the universal church. Brown's name for that kind of Christianity is a micro-Christendom: a region with its own saints, books, and rules, convinced that what it had at home was a full copy of the apostolic faith. Ireland, Northumbria, Francia, and Visigothic Spain each built one.

A conversion date is the start of that work. A king's baptism leaves open what a village did with the new god in the next generation. Gregory's letter keeps the old buildings and changes the rite. Oswiu's decision at Whitby takes one local custom and declares it universal. From about 200 to about 1000, the west became Christian through constructions of that sort. The same years look different if the question is pottery and workshops. Brown's evidence is the religious assembly, and the assembly is what it can show.
