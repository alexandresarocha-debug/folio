Around 590 an Irish monk, Columbanus, left Ireland for the continent. He founded the monastery of Luxeuil in Burgundy and died in 615 at Bobbio, in Italy. Peter Brown uses him, in The Rise of Western Christendom (Blackwell, 1996; later revised), to show a change in what people thought happened at a death. Families had brought food and drink to the grave to care for the dead. The penitential teaching that spread with Columbanus's monasteries treated the soul as being in danger, and treated the Mass, rather than a family meal at the tomb, as the help that mattered. Brown also describes those monasteries as economic centers, gathering the work and the produce of a whole countryside, in places where the Roman villa had once been the large institution.

A generation later the same pattern appears as an argument over the calendar. At Whitby in 664, King Oswiu of Northumbria had to choose between two ways of fixing Easter. Wilfrid of York, who lived from 634 to 709, argued for the Roman calculation and the Roman form of the tonsure. The monks trained in the Irish tradition defended the date they already kept. Oswiu sided with the Roman date. Both sides believed they were holding the true, universal practice. Brown's name for this kind of local Christianity is a micro-Christendom: a kingdom or a region with its own saints, its own sacred places, and its own customs, convinced that its version matched the faith of the apostles. Ireland, Northumbria, Visigothic Spain, and Francia each built one. Rome was a symbol and a court of appeal. It did not run the religion of a northern valley from a switchboard.

The practical consequence is that "the conversion of Europe" is a series of local settlements, not one finished model carried outward from Rome. A royal baptism mattered. So did the monastery that held the land, the saint people asked for rain or justice, and the funeral the clergy came to control. By the year 1000 those pieces covered the west. They were Christian, and they were different from each other, and different from the imperial church of Constantine's time.
