In 596 Pope Gregory I sent a party of monks from Rome toward Britain, by then a patchwork of Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. They lost their nerve in Gaul, and Augustine, their leader, went back to Rome. Gregory sent him on. The party landed in Kent in 597. King Æthelberht already had a Christian wife, the Frankish princess Bertha, and a bishop who had come with her. Augustine preached, the king was baptized, and Canterbury became the seat of a church. Peter Brown's The Rise of Western Christendom (Blackwell, 1996) dwells on scenes like this because Christianity in the north spread by local bargains. The western empire was already gone. A pope, a Frankish queen, and a Kentish king made a church.

A second bargain, inside one kingdom, shows how little unity that produced. Christians in northern Britain kept two dates for Easter, one brought by Irish missionaries and one brought from Rome. In 664 King Oswiu of Northumbria called both parties to Whitby and chose the Roman date. Colmán, who had argued the Irish side, left Lindisfarne. The kingdom had a winner, and it still had two Christian pasts sitting in its recent memory. Brown builds the book from such scenes, from Gregory's Rome to Ireland to the Saxons of Britain, because he treats early medieval Christianity as a set of local churches, each with its own saints, learning, and habits.

The detail matters when the question is how a religion moves. A shaded map suggests a center and a passive frontier. Kent in 597 was a court with its own reasons, a queen from Francia, and monks who had already tried to turn around. Whitby was a king choosing a calendar so that Easter, fasting, and the authority of his bishops would line up. Local rulers and local clergy built different churches out of the same religion. In the revised edition of 2003 Brown called those churches micro-Christendoms. The 1996 book already has the evidence in the two Easters. The spread was not only negotiation. Charlemagne's wars against the Saxons, begun in 772, used forced baptism and mass killing of resisters. A church chosen at Whitby and a church imposed by a conquering king are both in the record Brown's centuries cover.
