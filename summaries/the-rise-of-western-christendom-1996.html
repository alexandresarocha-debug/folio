Not one conversion, but many Christianities

Peter Brown's The Rise of Western Christendom, first published in 1996, is the long history that grew out of the religious half of The World of Late Antiquity. It follows Christianity from about the year 200 to about the year 1000, and it refuses the picture of a single western church marching outward from Rome until Europe was converted. Brown's organising idea is that the early medieval west was a patchwork of micro-Christendoms. Ireland, Francia, Visigothic Spain, Italy, the Balkans, and the northern worlds that received missionaries each built a Christian life with its own saints, its own sacred places, its own relation to papal Rome, and its own way of using the Roman past. Unity was claimed often. Uniformity was rare. The rise in his title is real, in the sense that Christian institutions came to frame European society, but it is a rise of many local constructions, not the spread of one finished model.

He later revised and widened the book, pushing the map further and adjusting emphases. The core claim does not depend on those revisions. It is already present in the decision to treat Christianity as a social world that people assembled out of scripture, relics, kingship, and local custom. A royal baptism matters in this history. It does not settle the history. What a farmer, a monk, or a local lord did with the new religion in the next generation matters as much, and is much harder to make into a heroic tale.

The late Roman church as a starting point, not a template

The book opens in a Roman empire that is already becoming Christian, and it treats that imperial church as one form among others rather than as the definition of the real thing. Bishops in great cities, councils arguing over doctrine, the alliance with the emperor after Constantine, the growth of monastic communities, and the cult of the martyrs are described as a particular late Roman achievement. They depend on cities, on literacy, and on a state that can enforce, or try to enforce, a definition of orthodoxy. When that state loses the west, the religion does not revert to nothing. It is carried into societies with different landscapes: a rural north, a tribal military elite, a monastery that may be the largest institution for many miles.

Brown is attentive to the body and to wealth, themes he has pursued across his work. Late Roman Christianity spent extraordinary energy on sexual discipline, virginity, and the renunciation of goods. It also became very rich. Churches accumulated land and treasure. The tension between radical poverty and institutional wealth is not a hypocrisy he pauses to scold. It is a structural fact of a religion that both honoured the ascetic and served the powerful. By the time Christian kingdoms cover the former western provinces, that tension is already old. It will be lived differently in a Frankish villa and in an Irish monastery, which is another reason he will not tell one story.

How Christian life was actually built

The middle of the book is a geography. Brown moves among the Christianities of the post-Roman west and of the north, looking at how each one made itself local. Relics and saints gave a place a protector and a calendar. Monasteries became centres of literacy, landholding, and, sometimes, mission. Kings used Christianity to join a wider world of prestige and to reorganise their own peoples. None of these processes was automatic. A mission could fail. A shrine could be a political prize. A monastery could be a family firm. Brown is wary of the older habit of hanging a conversion on a single saint's biography. Those biographies are sources he uses, and sources he treats as arguments made by the winners.

He is especially interested in the gap between the Christianity of Roman cities and the Christianity of the northern countryside. The first inherited buildings, bishops, and a dense textual culture. The second had to create sacred space almost from scratch, often by renaming springs, groves, and burials, and by inserting the liturgy into the agricultural year. This is not presented as a simple corruption of a pure faith by pagan leftovers. It is presented as the only way a universal religion becomes a lived one. The micro-Christendom is what you get when a small society takes the Christian name and fills it with its own dead, its own landscapes, and its own rulers. Rome remained a symbol and, increasingly, a court of appeal. It did not become a switchboard that controlled daily religion.

Neighbours that made the west western

A further consequence of his map is that western Christendom is defined by its neighbours. The Greek Christian empire, centred on Constantinople, remained a model of imperial Christianity and a rival. The Islamic world, after the conquests of the seventh century, held the southern Mediterranean and made the sea a border in a way it had not been in late antiquity. Brown's west is not a self-sufficient Europe waiting to be born. It is what took shape between those powers, often poorer and more fragmented than either, and convinced, in its own texts, of a special relationship to the apostles. The conviction was productive. It organised pilgrimage, papal claims, and the self-description of kingdoms. It should not be mistaken for a description of the whole Christian world.

Inside that frame, the centuries before the year 1000 are not a blank waiting for the later medieval church. They are the period in which the basic repertory was assembled: parish and monastery, saint and relic, Christian kingship, a Latin liturgy, a clergy that held a near monopoly on formal writing. Brown's account of that assembly is cultural and religious. It is not an economic history of living standards, and it does not try to be. A reader who has just absorbed the archaeological evidence for material collapse in the west will notice the silence. Brown is writing about what was built in the same centuries in which many workshops went quiet. The two accounts share a timeline and not a subject.

What the rise does and does not explain

The conclusion of the book is not that Christianity caused the fall of Rome, and not that it saved Europe. Both of those sentences belong to other arguments. Brown's own conclusion is descriptive. By the end of the first millennium, a variety of Christian societies covered the west, each of them convinced it was the heir of the apostles and of Rome, each of them in practice local. The variety is the point. It explains why medieval Christianity could be both intensely universal in its claims and intensely particular in its saints. It also explains why a history written only from Rome, or only from the conversion of one famous king, misses the way the religion actually spread, by adaptation as much as by decree.

He does not deny coercion, political calculation, or the destruction of older cults. He sets them inside a longer process of translation. People adopted Christianity because kings demanded it, because a holy place had a reputation for healing, because a monastery offered a career, and because the Christian god had come to seem the one who held the field. Motives of that kind are ordinary. Brown's achievement is to make the ordinary construction of many Christianities as interesting as the drama of a single conversion, and to give the early medieval west a religious history that is neither a decline from Augustine nor a prologue to the twelfth century.

Why it matters here

The Folio card brown-late-antiquity presents Brown's 1971 book as the shift from fall to transformation, and it points onward to this one. The Rise of Western Christendom is that next step, made specific. The card says late antiquity produced monasticism, the cult of saints, and new forms of religious life, and that barbarian kingdoms kept pieces of Rome. This book shows those pieces being turned into something that is no longer late Roman. Micro-Christendoms are the mechanism. They explain how a religion tied to a Mediterranean empire became the organising life of Ireland, the Frankish north, and other societies the Antonine emperors never ruled.

The connection back to Gibbon's verdict is direct and unsentimental. Gibbon treated Christianity as a force that drew talent away from the civic state and helped a great civilisation to end. Brown treats the same religion as the material out of which new societies were made after the western emperors had gone. The card is right that the two historians are not disagreeing about a date. They are disagreeing about what deserves the weight. Reading this book beside the 1971 essay shows the transformation argument at two scales: first as a portrait of a late antique world, then as a history of how that world's Christian habits were rebuilt, differently in each place, across the early Middle Ages. The material losses documented by archaeologists are outside its frame. The religious world those archaeologists sometimes set aside is here described in full.
