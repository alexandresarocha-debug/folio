A principle, not a feeling

Ernest Gellner’s Nations and Nationalism, published in 1983, defines nationalism as a political principle. It holds that the political unit and the national unit should be congruent. National sentiment is the anger, or the satisfaction, that follows when the principle is violated or fulfilled. The definition is deliberately dry. Gellner does not start from language, blood, or the love of a landscape. He starts from a claim about legitimate government, and he asks in what kind of society that claim becomes both plausible and urgent. His answer is industrial society. Nationalism is not the awakening of a nation that was always there. It is the form politics takes when a culture of literacy and standardised communication becomes the ticket of admission to work, dignity, and citizenship.

The book is short, argumentative, and openly theoretical. Gellner uses a handful of stylised historical types rather than a survey of every nationalism, and he invents a parable, the empire of Megalomania and the peasant culture of Ruritania, so that the mechanism can be seen without the distractions of a real proper name. Readers who want the archive of one national movement will not find it here. Readers who want to know why the nation-state, rather than the dynasty or the city or the faith, became the default political roof of modern life will find the clearest single answer of its generation.

Agrarian society, and why it did not need nations

The argument begins by describing the social world in which nationalism would have been a nonsense. Agrarian societies, in Gellner’s picture, are built on a large population of food-producers and a small elite of warriors, priests, and administrators. The elite has a high culture, often tied to a script and a clergy. The producers have local folk cultures, countless and varied. There is no need, and no means, for the state to make peasants share the high culture. Most people do not go to school. They do not change jobs. They do not have to send precise, context-free messages to strangers. Political boundaries and cultural boundaries can cross-cut each other indefinitely. An empire can rule many peoples, and a people can live under several rulers, because culture is not the basis of political membership. It is a local habit below, and a specialist literacy above.

Gellner insists on the harshness of this picture because romantic theories of nationalism treat the nation as the natural community from which empires were a departure. On his account the reverse is closer to the truth. Cultural difference was the ordinary condition. The demand that a state and a culture coincide is the historical novelty. Clerisies guarded high cultures and did not try to pour them into the whole population. The gap between the literate and the illiterate was a feature of the social order, not a defect waiting to be reformed.

What industry requires

Industrial society changes the requirement. Work changes within a lifetime. People move. Production depends on a capacity to understand instructions, manuals, and contracts exchanged between strangers. The messages have to be explicit, because sender and receiver do not share a village context. Gellner’s name for the culture that can carry such messages is a standardised, school-transmitted high culture. It is not anyone’s folk culture, and it is not the old clerical literacy. It is a culture the state can teach to everyone, because only a state can run a system of education on that scale, and because the labour market will not function if each district qualifies its young in a different idiom.

Once mobility and communication depend on possessing that culture, culture becomes the currency of full membership. A person whose education does not match the culture of the state that employs and governs them is not merely different. They are at risk of being confined to the worst jobs, or of being governed by people who do not regard them as interchangeable fellow citizens. Nationalism is the demand that the political roof be brought into line with the educational culture, either by giving the culture its own state or by converting the state to the culture. Nations, in the famous reversal, do not produce nationalism. Nationalism produces nations, out of the available raw material of language, memory, and grievance, under the pressure of an industrial order that punishes cultural mismatch.

The Ruritanian parable and the types of case

The parable of Ruritania makes the mechanism concrete. Ruritanians are peasants inside the empire of Megalomania, distinguishable by dialect and custom, and until industrialisation those differences are of little political consequence. They learn the high culture of the empire if they want to enter the towns, and the villages remain local. Industrialisation then makes the difference costly. The Ruritanian who arrives in the labour market with the wrong speech and the wrong schooling finds that the costs of assimilation are high and the rewards of the dominant culture are captured by people who already have it. Intellectuals codify the dialect, write the history, and turn a peasant category into a nation. Whether they succeed is contingent. Gellner’s point is that the situation, a mobile economy plus a cultural hierarchy, regularly generates the attempt.

He does not think every culture gets a state. There are more potential nations than there are viable political units, and which cultures win is a matter of history, power, and sometimes of who industrialises first. He distinguishes kinds of situation rather than offering a single biography of “the” nation. Where a high culture and a state already roughly coincide, as in some early industrialisers, the passage can be comparatively smooth. Where an empire sorts peoples into a hierarchy of cultures, the Ruritanian conflict is the likely pattern. Where a people has a high culture and a diaspora and lacks a territory, the nationalism that results has a different shape again. The typology is a set of structural predicaments. It is not a ranking of peoples.

Ideas are not enough

Gellner is arguing, often implicitly and sometimes by name, with accounts that treat nationalism as a doctrine invented by philosophers and then believed. Ideas matter in his story only once the social order has made them useful. A theory of self-determination written in a world of stable peasant cultures and clerical high cultures would have had nothing much to organise. The same theory, available to people who must share a standardised culture in order to work, becomes a political programme. This is why he is wary of definitions that begin with the will, or with a list of national characteristics. The will is real. It is produced by a change in the division of labour and in the organisation of education, not by a contagion of books alone.

The contrast with Benedict Anderson, whose Imagined Communities appeared in the same year, is instructive even though the two books are not a direct reply to each other. Anderson traces the nation to print-capitalism, the novel, the newspaper, and creole elites in the Americas. Gellner traces it to industrialisation and the monopoly of legitimate education. Both think nations are modern and made. They disagree about the machinery and about the sequence. A reader can use each as a check on the other. Where a national movement precedes industry, Anderson’s cultural forms, or some older ethnic identification, are doing work Gellner’s model fits poorly. Where a movement is strongest among people newly pulled into a shared labour market and a shared school system, Gellner’s machinery is hard to avoid.

Limits

The limits of the book are the limits of a functional argument. Gellner explains why industrial society needs some form of cultural standardisation under a state. He explains less well why this movement happened here and not there, or why the resulting nations took the borders they did. Critics have argued that he neglects the passions, the ethnic continuities, and the pre-industrial nations that do not fit a timetable keyed to the factory and the school. England and France, often treated as early and relatively coherent cases, industrialised on top of political identities that were already under construction. The theory can absorb some of this by saying that the raw material is older than the principle. It cannot, without strain, make every early nation a creature of industry.

There is a related harshness that is also a virtue. Gellner is unsentimental about folk culture. He thinks the cultures nations claim to protect are often the standardised high cultures that nationalism itself installs, not the village worlds it sentimentalises. That claim offended romantics and it still needs qualification, because invented traditions do not invent themselves out of nothing, and because local cultures are more than raw material. The qualification does not restore the older picture in which nations are natural. It says that Gellner’s machine needs a history of the particular materials it picks up. The 1983 book is the machine.

Why it matters here

The idea under discussion is that nations are imagined communities with a modern origin, closely tied to print. Gellner is the other great statement from the same year, and it shifts the weight from the forms of reading to the structure of industrial life. Anderson explains how strangers come to picture one another. Gellner explains why a state then has to make them interchangeable, and why the people left outside that standardisation become nationalists. Hobsbawm and Ranger show the rituals with which the new high culture is made to look old. Eisenstein shows how print made a standardised culture technically possible. Here, Nations and Nationalism is the argument that keeps the cluster from becoming only a story about books and symbols. The nation, on this page, is what happens when work and schooling require one culture, and when the state is the only institution that can supply it.
