Ernest Gellner opens Nations and Nationalism (1983) with a principle, not a mood. Nationalism, he writes, "is primarily a political principle, which holds that the political and the national unit should be congruent." The people who rule and the people who are ruled should share one culture, and the border of the state should be the border of that culture. He thinks the principle is modern. In agrarian societies most people were peasants in small communities. A court, a church, and a written high culture belonged to a small class. Those layers did not have to sound like one people, and usually they did not.

His account of the change is a parable, with made-up names. The Ruritanians are peasants in scattered pockets of the Empire of Megalomania, speaking related dialects that nobody else speaks. Officials use the court language. Many priests use a language descended from the liturgy. The traders in the market towns are another group. In the nineteenth century other regions of the empire industrialize. Ruritanians go there for work and do badly, because the language of the job is not theirs. A few young men, educated for the church, become teachers and journalists instead. Migration, elementary school, and military service give those writers a public. Some migrants assimilate, and the capital's directory fills with Ruritanian surnames spelled the imperial way. Others want a state whose school and offices use a standardized Ruritanian. The name Ruritania comes from Anthony Hope's novel The Prisoner of Zenda (1894). The situation Gellner is drawing is the Habsburg one.

That is a test for a national origin story. When a people is described as having slept for centuries and then woken, ask what new pressure made a school-taught culture worth a state of its own. Gellner's answer is industrial work. Strangers have to be able to read the same instructions and change jobs, and the state that runs the schools decides which language counts as a full public life. He adds a limit inside the theory. Nationalism sometimes turns a culture that already exists into a nation, sometimes invents one, and often destroys older cultures. It is not, in his words, a political version of natural kinds. Cultures overlap. States come in many shapes. The new thing is the demand that the two must match.

Anthony D. Smith, in The Ethnic Origins of Nations (1986), argued that this still leaves the choice of culture unexplained. National movements usually take hold of an older name, a story of shared descent, and a set of memories. A school can spread a high culture. Smith's evidence is that the culture which gets the state is rarely an arbitrary one, and that a theory which starts from industry has to say why that past was available to be used.
