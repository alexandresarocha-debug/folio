Ernest Gellner opens the hard part of Nations and Nationalism (1983) with a count, not a battle. A linguist had told him there were something like 8,000 languages on earth. The number of states was on the order of 200. He then gives nationalism every benefit of the doubt. Add the movements that want a state and do not have one, and drop the states that nobody seriously thinks of as nations, and he still refuses to put the number of effective nationalisms much above 200. For charity he multiplies by four and allows 800. Eight thousand into eight hundred is one in ten. His conclusion is the opposite of the usual speech about nationalism's terrible strength. For every group that fights to put one culture under one government, about nine groups with a comparable claim never stir.

He is explicit that language is only a rough stand-in for culture, and that the 8,000 is a rough figure. The ratio is the point. If nationalism were a natural awakening, waiting inside every people, the world would be far more shattered than it is. Most potential nations, on his arithmetic, fail to bark.

The story he uses to show how one of them does bark is openly made up. He calls the peasants Ruritanians and the empire they live in Megalomania, a sketch built to resemble the national movements of central and eastern Europe, not a country he found in an archive. The Ruritanians speak related dialects. Their priests and their law use a different language. For a long time they have griefs and songs and no nation. Then Megalomania industrializes. Ruritanian boys leave for work in the cities, and a few of them, trained for the church or the office, meet the cold fact that the language of the village will not get them a job. They start collecting the songs, writing a standard Ruritanian, and demanding schools. Some of their cousins simply learn the imperial language and disappear into the capital. The ones who do not, Gellner says, have to build a high culture and then a state to house it. "It is nationalism which engenders nations, and not the other way round." He adds, in the next sentence, that nationalism uses old cultural scraps, chooses among them, and usually transforms them. Any scrap might have served. The need for a literate, mobile, interchangeable population is what is not optional. An industrial society cannot run on a dozen village dialects and a clerk class that alone can read.

The count is what to keep when a movement says the nation was always there and only had to wake. Ask whether a school, a written standard, and a workforce that could be moved and retrained had to be built first. If they did, the awakening is the thing that was constructed. Gellner himself notes one awkward case. Scottish nationalism, he says, may contradict the model, because it does not track a separate language.

Anthony D. Smith pressed the larger objection in The Ethnic Origins of Nations (1986). National movements, he argued, do not grab any scrap. They gather around a people who already had a name, a myth of descent, and a stock of memories. The factories explain why a standardized culture becomes urgent. They explain less well why this past, and not a neighboring one, is the past a movement claims.
