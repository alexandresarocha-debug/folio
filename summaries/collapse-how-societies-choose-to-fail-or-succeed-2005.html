On Rapa Nui, the island European sailors later called Easter Island, people carved hundreds of giant stone statues and moved them across the island. Jared Diamond's Collapse (2005) tells that effort as a contest among clans. Each clan had a reason to raise a larger monument. The forest that supplied timber, canoe hulls, and cover for the soil came down. His title word is "choose." He treats the cutting as a social decision that paid the people doing it and became a disaster for the island, and he offers the finished case to readers watching forests and fisheries now.

His other full narrative is the Norse in Greenland. Settlers from Iceland, in the historical account led by Erik the Red, established farms there in the late tenth century, around 985. They kept cattle, a Christian church, and a European way of identifying themselves, at the edge of the climate where that package could work. The Western Settlement was abandoned in the fourteenth century. The Eastern Settlement lasted into the fifteenth. Diamond reads the ending as a refusal to take up the hunting methods of the Inuit in the same country, followed by colder decades and thinner contact with Europe.

Both stories are now argued with evidence that will not fit a single bad decision. Terry L. Hunt and Carl P. Lipo, in Pacific Science (2009), wrote that they did not find unambiguous evidence for a huge population that crashed from deforestation before European contact. They argued that the Polynesian rat, brought with the settlers, ate the nuts of the island's palms, so the forest failed from rats, cutting, and fire together, and that the clear population drop comes after Europeans reached the island in 1722. A 2024 paper in Science Advances, mapping Rapa Nui's rock gardens from satellite images, found those gardens too limited to have fed the very large populations the collapse story requires. For Greenland, Andrew Dugmore and colleagues, in PNAS (2012), treated the Norse as arctic adapters who had already intensified the hunt for seals and other marine mammals. They argued that the settlements then ended because that specialization, plus isolation, left the colony exposed to a run of fifteenth-century changes, climate among them. A flat refusal to learn the local skills does not cover the evidence.

A small society can damage its trees, and a colony can die out. Those facts still leave the warning only as good as the case. When Easter Island or Norse Greenland is used as a lesson about a present environmental choice, the lesson has to survive the rats, the contact period, the climate, and the trade routes. Diamond's question, whether habits and the interests of leading groups can lock a society onto a damaging path, remains a real question. His most famous answers are no longer the whole of the evidence.
