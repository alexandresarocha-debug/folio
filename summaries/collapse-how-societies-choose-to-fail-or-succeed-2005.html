Jared Diamond's account of Easter Island, in Collapse (Viking, 2005), is a society that used up its trees. Polynesians settled the island, on the chronology he uses, around AD 900, and the population rose to around 15,000. Chiefs competed by raising the great stone statues, the moai, and the forest, including a giant palm, came down. When the Dutch captain Jacob Roggeveen landed on Easter Sunday, 5 April 1722, he found an island with very few trees. Diamond reads the statues, the pollen, and the thin later population as a collapse that the islanders brought on themselves, before any European colony existed: deforestation, eroded soil, conflict, and a society that could no longer do what it had done.

His second case is Norse Greenland, and he is explicit about the checklist. Erik the Red led settlers from Iceland around 985. They farmed, kept cattle, and cut what wood there was. Diamond lists five pressures: they damaged the land they depended on, including the wood they needed to make charcoal and iron; the climate cooled in the late 1300s and the 1400s; trade with Norway dwindled; relations with the Inuit were hostile; and their own customs made it hard to switch to the Inuit way of living in the cold. The western settlement was abandoned by about 1400, and the eastern settlement by about 1450. In his telling, the Norse ended because they would not change a European way of life that the island could no longer support.

Both stories are used, far outside archaeology, as warnings: a society can choose the practices that destroy it, and the choice is visible in hindsight as a refusal to change. The value of the warning depends on the dates and the diet being right. If the forest was already doomed by an animal the settlers brought with them, or if the Norse were already eating seal, the moral is different.

Both cases have been challenged with measurements. Terry Hunt, in the Journal of Archaeological Science in 2007, dated the settlement at Anakena to about 1200 rather than 800 or 900, estimated a pre-contact population of perhaps 3,000 to 4,000 around 1350–1370, and argued that the Polynesian rat ate the palm nuts and stopped the forest from replacing itself. Deforestation, on his clock, ran for about four centuries, from roughly 1250 to 1650, without a social collapse before Europeans arrived. The documented population crash came later, with disease and the slave raids of the 1860s. On Greenland, Jette Arneborg and colleagues reported in Radiocarbon in 1999 that carbon isotopes in Norse bones show the diet shifting toward food from the sea. The settlers were eating more seal and fish as the centuries passed, which cuts against a simple picture of farmers who starved rather than touch local food.
