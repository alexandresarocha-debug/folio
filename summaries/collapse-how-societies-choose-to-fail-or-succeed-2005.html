On 5 April 1722, Easter Sunday, the Dutch navigator Jacob Roggeveen landed on an island his crew named for the day. The people of Rapa Nui had hundreds of huge stone statues, and very little wood. Jared Diamond's Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed (Viking, 2005) makes the island his model of a society that destroyed its own basis. Polynesian settlers, on his account, found a forest that included a species of large palm. They cut the trees to clear gardens, to build canoes, and to move the statues. Soil eroded. Without large timber they lost the boats that had tied them to other islands. By the time Europeans arrived, Diamond argues, the population had already crashed in conflict and hunger, and the forest was gone.

He sets a colder case next to it. Around 985 Erik the Red led Norse settlers from Iceland to Greenland. They built two communities, with churches, cattle, and sheep, near the edge of a medieval warm period. The western settlement was empty by the middle of the fourteenth century. The last document from the eastern settlement is a marriage at Hvalsey church in 1408. By the end of the fifteenth century that settlement was empty too. Diamond's explanation is a refusal to change. The Norse kept a European farm and a European identity while the climate cooled, and they did not adopt the Inuit skin boats and harpoons that made a living on that coast. From cases like these he draws a short set of pressures that show up together: damage to the environment, a shift in climate, hostile neighbors, the loss of trading partners, and the choices a society makes when those pressures arrive.

The Easter Island chronology has been redated. Terry L. Hunt and Carl P. Lipo, in Science in 2006, put the earliest reliable archaeological layers at Anakena at about 1200, centuries later than the long occupation Diamond used, which leaves less time for a slow fall from a golden age. Hunt has also argued that the Pacific rat, which came in the canoes and eats palm nuts, did a large part of the damage to the forest. The population collapse that is secure in the written record comes after European contact, through disease and, in 1862, Peruvian slave raids. The Norse evidence has moved as well. Jette Arneborg and colleagues, in a 1999 study of carbon in bones from Greenland cemeteries, found that later generations ate far more food from the sea than the first settlers had. The failure was not a simple refusal of seal. Diamond's list of pressures still organizes the question. The claim that has to be checked, island by island, is that people chose the path that ended them.
