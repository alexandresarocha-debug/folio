Failure as something a society does

Jared Diamond's Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed, published in 2005, is a comparative book about why some societies unravel and others get through comparable dangers. Diamond is a geographer and physiologist who had already argued, in Guns, Germs, and Steel, that deep geographic luck set the continental balance of power. Collapse is the sequel in mood if not in mechanism. It looks at the shorter run, at decisions made by people who could see their forests, their soil, their allies, and their enemies. The opening claim is that environmental damage is a recurring cause of social failure, and that it is never the only one. He offers a five-part framework. A society may damage its environment. The climate may change. Neighbours may be hostile. Trading partners may disappear. And, decisively for his title, the society may respond well or badly to the first four. The word choose is the controversial one. Diamond thinks institutions, values, and the interests of elites make the response a genuine choice rather than an automatic consequence of the landscape.

He is explicit about the present. The book is written for readers who are watching deforestation, soil loss, overfishing, and climate change, and the past cases are meant as a set of completed experiments. That purpose gives the book its urgency and its risk. A society recruited as a warning has to be described fairly, or the warning becomes a fable. Diamond's method is to tell a number of cases in enough narrative detail that the framework can be seen working, and then to return to modern examples where the outcome is not yet settled.

Islands, deserts, and the Norse

The sharpest cases are the ones where the boundaries are clear. On Easter Island, Diamond reconstructs a society that invested heavily in carving and moving great statues, and that cut the forest on which canoe-building, soil protection, and agriculture depended. Competition among clans for status, expressed in the statues, made the damaging behaviour rational for each group and disastrous for the island. The result, in his telling, was ecological impoverishment, political breakdown, and a much-reduced population living among monuments they could no longer have raised. Pitcairn and Henderson, smaller and more remote, failed when the wider network that had supported them failed. They are his illustration of the trading-partner factor. A place can be undone by the loss of neighbours it depended on, even if its own leaders make no spectacular mistake.

In the American Southwest he retells the Ancestral Puebloan abandonments, including the communities often called Anasazi, as a collision between population, a difficult dryland environment, and drought, with social institutions that could not spread the risk. The Classic Maya appear as a densely settled, politically competitive society in a landscape of fragile soils and uneven rainfall, where warfare and the demands of elites worsened a resource problem that climate then made acute. The Norse in Greenland are one of his fullest narratives. A medieval European society at the edge of the habitable world kept to cattle, to Christian identity, and to habits imported from Norway, failed to adopt Inuit techniques that worked in the same climate, and eventually starved or left when the cooling climate and the loss of contact with Europe removed the margin. Iceland, settled from the same cultural background, damaged its soils badly and then, over a long period, adjusted enough to survive. The pair is Diamond's cleanest demonstration that the same tradition can choose differently.

Successes, and the modern chapters

The book would be a catalogue of ruins if it stopped there, and Diamond is anxious not to stop there. He points to Tikopia, a small Pacific island that regulated population and managed its woodland tightly enough to persist. He points to the New Guinea highlands, where people developed sophisticated agriculture and local controls over a long period. He points to Tokugawa Japan, which faced serious deforestation and then, through a combination of official policy and changed practice, reversed it. Success, in these chapters, is not abundance without limit. It is the willingness to notice a limit and to enforce a rule that individual families would rather evade. The framework's fifth factor, the social response, is doing the explanatory work. Environment sets the problem. Choice decides whether the problem becomes a collapse.

The modern half of the book applies the same list to Rwanda, to the two halves of Hispaniola, to China, to Australia, and to Montana. Haiti and the Dominican Republic share an island and have had very different rates of forest survival, which Diamond uses to keep institutions in the story even when the climate is shared. Rwanda is discussed as a case in which population pressure and land scarcity formed part of the background to political catastrophe, a claim that has been disputed and that he presents as part of a larger set of causes rather than as a sufficient one. China and Australia are treated as large societies consuming environmental capital at a rate their own future citizens will have to live with. Montana is the local, first-person chapter, a landscape he knows, used to show that rich societies also defer costs and argue about who should pay. The method is the same as in the archaeological chapters. Describe the resource, describe the institutions that decide, and ask whether the decision-makers are insulated from the consequences.

Evidence, and the shape of the conclusion

Diamond relies on archaeology, palaeoecology, and the historical record, translated into a narrative a general reader can follow. Pollen, charcoal, bone, and settlement maps carry the environmental claims. Chronicles and ethnographies carry the choices. He is not shy about judgement. Statue-building on Easter Island and the Norse refusal of Inuit skills appear as errors, explainable by competition and by cultural identity, and still errors. The conclusion of the book is that collapse is not a fate attached to a particular kind of landscape. It is the outcome of a negotiation between what the environment will bear and what the society is willing to change. Societies that see the problem early, that have institutions capable of restricting their own elites, and that are not trapped by a hostile ideology of the resource, can pull back. Societies whose elites export the costs, or whose sacred commitments forbid the required change, continue until the base fails.

He distinguishes his approach from a simple environmental determinism, and the distinction is the point of the fifth factor. A determinist book would have needed only the first two or three items on his list. The title's emphasis on choice is a claim that knowledge and politics matter at the moment of stress. It is also a claim that values can be maladaptive. Diamond is willing to say that a cherished way of life can be the instrument of its own end, which is an uncomfortable sentence and, in his case studies, the sentence he thinks the evidence supports.

What the critics changed

The criticism has been severe enough that several of the famous cases should no longer be told exactly as the book tells them. The edited volume Questioning Collapse, assembled by Patricia McAnany and Norman Yoffee, argued that the word collapse hides continuity, adaptation, and the perspective of the people who remained. Easter Islanders did not vanish. The ecological story of the island is more contested than a single parable of competitive deforestation, and European contact, disease, and slaving belong in any honest account of the later demographic fall. The Norse in Greenland are similarly the subject of work that complicates the picture of a stubborn refusal to learn. Anthropologists have also objected to the moral pattern in which non-European societies are recruited as object lessons for a modern audience. A framework designed to teach can smooth a past that was more inventive, and more unlucky, than a bad decision.

The comparison with Tainter is the cleanest way to keep what is useful. Tainter explains failure by the rising cost of complexity and treats environmental damage as one stress among others. Diamond explains failure by resource damage plus a social response, and he is more willing to call the response a choice. Diamond's choice can look, in a harsh light, like a blame placed on the victims after the fact. Tainter's rationality can look like a refusal to see the forest for the tax rate. A reader of both can use Diamond's cases as questions, what happened to the trees, the soil, the trade routes, and the climate, without accepting every verdict he attaches to them. The framework survives more safely than the most famous morality tales.

Why it matters here

The card tainter-complexity presents Tainter's diminishing-returns theory and then points to this book as the environmental counterpart. Diamond supplies the five-part list the card is invoking when it says that resource damage and social choice are a different explanation from costly complexity. The same card notes Questioning Collapse as the anthropological reply, and that reply belongs to the way Folio should use the book. Collapse is the widely read statement of the environmental theory of social failure, and the source of case studies, Easter Island, the Maya, the Norse Greenlanders, Tokugawa Japan, that later arguments have to confirm or revise. It matters here as the other general theory on the card, the one that keeps soil, forest, climate, and the insulation of elites in the conversation whenever a collapse is blamed only on invaders or only on the cost of institutions.
