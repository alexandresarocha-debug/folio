Each parent passes on half of their DNA. The other half stays with the parent. Augustine Kong and the deCODE genetics team in Iceland asked a simple question: can the half a child did not inherit still affect that child?

If it can, it must work through the environment. Those untransmitted genes can only reach the child by shaping the parents, and through them the home. The team called this genetic nurture.

They studied 21,637 Icelanders born between 1940 and 1983 who had at least one parent genotyped. For each person they built two polygenic scores for educational attainment, the number of years spent in education. One used the gene versions the person inherited. The other used the versions the parents carried but did not pass on.

The untransmitted score still predicted how long people stayed in education. Its effect was 29.9% as large as the effect of the inherited score. In the preprint version, the inherited score explained about 5% of the differences in years of education. Once the nurture part was taken out, the direct effect explained about 2.5%, roughly half.

Fathers' and mothers' untransmitted scores had similar effects on education. For some nutrition- and health-related traits, mothers' scores mattered more.

What this means in real life: a parent's genes can help a child even when the child did not inherit them, for example through the home and habits those genes help create. It also means gene studies of education partly measure family environment.

Caution: the authors note that partners with similar education levels pairing up could create part of the effect. The results come from one small country, so the size of the effect may differ elsewhere. And the finding is about a score that sums many genetic variants, not about any single gene.

Source. Kong, A., Thorleifsson, G., Frigge, M. L., Vilhjalmsson, B. J., Young, A. I., Thorgeirsson, T. E., et al. (2018). The nature of nurture: effects of parental genotypes. Science, 359(6374), 424-428. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan6877
