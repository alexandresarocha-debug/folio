A test built for the argument about averages

A national experiment reveals where a growth mindset improves achievement, published in Nature in 2019 by David Yeager, Paul Hanselman, Gregory Walton, Jared Murray, Robert Crosnoe, Chandra Muller and a large group of collaborators, is an attempt to answer the question that meta-analyses leave open. If the average effect of a mindset intervention on grades is very small, is that because the idea does nothing, or because it does something modest for particular students in particular schools and very little elsewhere? An average can hide a reliable, limited effect. It can also be the footprint of a few optimistic studies. The way to tell those apart is a pre-registered experiment, large enough to look at subgroups that were specified in advance, delivered in ordinary schools rather than in a laboratory that specialises in the theory.

The team placed a brief online growth-mindset intervention in a nationally recruited set of American public secondary schools, aimed at students early in secondary school, when academic trajectories are still being set and when a belief about ability has a lot of new difficulty to interpret. Pre-registration matters here as part of the substance, not as a badge. The literature Sisk and colleagues reviewed was vulnerable to the charge that analyses were chosen after the results were seen, and that small positive findings were the ones readers heard about. A national trial that says beforehand what it will count as success, and that is large enough for a modest effect to be distinguished from noise, is a different kind of evidence from a classroom study analysed flexibly.

What the lesson contained

The intervention teaches the incremental theory in a direct form. Intellectual ability is not a fixed endowment. It can be developed. Struggle and confusion are presented as signs that learning is happening, rather than as signs that a student has hit a ceiling. The delivery is short and digital, so that the test is of a lesson a school system could actually give, not of a term-long curriculum that only its inventors can teach. Students do not only read the claim. They work with it, including exercises in which they explain the idea for the benefit of a future student. That step is there because a belief is more likely to be adopted when a person has spoken it in their own words than when they have merely recognised it on a screen. The comparison group receives a different activity, so that time, attention and the novelty of a schools programme are not mistaken for the content of the mindset message.

The outcomes match the practical claims that had been made for mindset programmes. One is grades, the distal and sceptical outcome, the place where previous evidence looked weakest. The other is enrolment in advanced mathematics, a choice that sits closer to the mechanism. A student who believes that ability can grow has a reason to take a harder course. A student who believes that ability is a fixed trait has a reason to protect a record by staying in a course that will measure them kindly. If the intervention changed nothing but moods, grades and course-taking would both sit still. If it changed the meaning of difficulty, course-taking is where the change should be easiest to see, and grades are where it should be hardest.

Where the improvement appeared

The results are an argument about location. The brief lesson modestly improved grades among lower-achieving students. It did not pretend to a large transformation of achievement across the whole sample, and the paper's interest is in the concentration rather than in a headline average alone. Lower-achieving students are the students for whom a change in the interpretation of struggle has the most work to do, and they are the students the 2018 meta-analyses had already flagged, cautiously, as the place where effects might be larger. The national experiment finds that pattern under pre-registration and at a scale the earlier studies could not reach. Modest is the correct word. The card's word is the paper's word. A modest improvement in grades is a real improvement, and it is not the reversal of educational inequality.

The lesson also increased enrolment in advanced mathematics. That result matters because it is a behaviour, not a self-report of having enjoyed the video. Students who had been told, and who had told someone else, that ability can be developed were more willing to enter courses that would be harder. The mechanism the praise studies suggested, that a growth theory licences challenge and a fixed theory punishes it, has a visible institutional consequence here. Advanced mathematics is exactly the sort of choice a fixed theory makes look dangerous and a growth theory makes look worthwhile. Finding the effect on that choice is evidence that the intervention did not merely teach students the words of a more acceptable theory.

The school as a condition, not a backdrop

The most distinctive claim in the paper is that the intervention's academic effect depended on the norms of the school. The improvement in grades was clearer where peer norms supported the behaviours a growth mindset encourages, such as taking on difficult work. In a school where those behaviours are socially expensive, a private belief has less room to become a grade. Yeager and his colleagues are arguing against a purely individual picture of mindset, including the picture that a careless reading of Dweck can produce. A lesson can change what a student thinks ability is. Whether that thought becomes persistence in a hard course depends on whether the people around the student treat persistence as sensible or as a way of looking stupid. Context is not a caveat added after a universal effect. Context is part of where the effect lives, which is what the title promises to reveal.

This is also the paper's reply to the meta-analytic average. An average that mixes schools where the peer culture supports challenge with schools where it does not, and that mixes students who are already achieving highly with students who are struggling, will look small even if a specified cell of that table looks larger. The national experiment was built to inspect the cell rather than to produce one more number for the average. It does not cancel Sisk and colleagues. It specifies them. Very small on average, and modestly positive for lower-achieving students, especially in supportive schools, can both be true. The public argument tends to cite one and drop the other. The scientific argument is the conjunction.

What modesty still forbids

Nothing in the trial restores the strongest claims made for mindset programmes in the years after Dweck's book. The intervention does not transform outcomes on its own. It does not raise achievement evenly. It does not licence a school to replace curriculum, teaching quality or a sane approach to failure with a lesson about the brain. The authors' design choices acknowledge those limits. The lesson is brief because a brief lesson is what can be tested cleanly and delivered widely, and the effects are reported as modest because that is what appeared. A reader who wants a revolution in attainment will be disappointed, and should be. A reader who wants to know whether the growth-mindset mechanism survives contact with a pre-registered national sample should come away with a qualified yes.

The trial also cannot speak for every version of the lesson. It tests a particular online programme, aimed at a particular age, in American public secondary schools, with grades and advanced mathematics as the outcomes. A poorly made mindset exercise, a lesson delivered as a scolding, or a poster that praises effort without strategy, is not this intervention and does not inherit its results. Dweck's warning about the false growth mindset remains in force beside the trial. The national experiment supports a careful practice. It does not support the slogan that travelled ahead of the evidence.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Growth mindset: big idea, small effects, uses this paper as the evidence on the hopeful side of a sceptical ledger. Opposite the weak correlations and the very small average intervention effects in the 2018 meta-analyses, the card sets a large pre-registered national study that found a modest improvement in grades among lower-achieving students and an increase in enrolment in advanced mathematics, especially where peer norms were supportive. The card's own summary, that mindset is a real but modest lever and more likely to help under the right conditions than to transform outcomes alone, is a reading of Yeager and of Sisk taken together. This paper is the one that makes the first half of that sentence possible after the second half had become hard to deny. It shows where a growth mindset improves achievement, and by showing where, it shows how much, and how little, the improvement is.
