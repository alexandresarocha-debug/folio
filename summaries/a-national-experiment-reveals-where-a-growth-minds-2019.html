David Yeager and colleagues, Carol Dweck among them, published the National Study of Learning Mindsets in Nature in 2019. The students were ninth graders in a nationally representative sample of 65 U.S. public high schools. Each student was randomly assigned to a short online growth-mindset program or to a control activity. The program took less than an hour and taught that intellectual ability can be developed. Grade results were available for 12,490 students.

The pre-registered grade comparison was for students at or below their own school's median before the study, 6,320 of them. By the end of ninth grade, their grade point average in math, science, English, and social studies was 0.10 points higher than that of comparable students in the control group. The standardized effect was 0.11. Students above the school median, 6,170 of them, showed a difference of 0.01 points, which was not distinguishable from no difference. Course-taking moved as well. In the 41 schools that reported it, covering 6,690 students, the share taking advanced mathematics in tenth grade, algebra II or higher, went from 33% in the control condition to 36%.

The grade gain was larger in some buildings than in others. Lower-achieving students in low- and medium-achieving schools gained 0.18 grade points when their school's peer norms, measured from what control-group classmates chose on a challenge-seeking task, were in the top quarter. The lesson showed up more clearly where other students already treated hard work as ordinary. Those norms were observed, not randomly assigned, so the school comparison is not itself an experiment on culture.

For a parent or a district, the size is the finding. A tenth of a grade point, for students who were behind their own classmates, after a lesson shorter than a class period, is a limited gain with a pre-registered test behind it. Students who were already earning higher grades did not get a grade boost from the same lesson. The schools in which classmates treated struggle as normal were the schools in which the lesson was still visible in the transcript at the end of the year.
