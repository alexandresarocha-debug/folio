When Scots gather and put on the kilt, the tartan is supposed to say which clan they belong to, and the whole outfit is supposed to be ancient. Hugh Trevor-Roper's essay in The Invention of Tradition (1983), edited by Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, says the outfit is largely a modern production. Before the Union with England, Highland dress was the mark of a poor, armed, cattle-raiding society that most Lowland Scots wanted nothing to do with. The old garment was the belted plaid, a long cloth. Trevor-Roper traces the short kilt, the philibeg, to Thomas Rawlinson, an English ironmaster who employed Highlanders at a furnace in Glengarry and wanted a garment they could work in. After the Jacobite defeat at Culloden, the Act of Proscription of 1746 banned Highland dress. The ban was lifted in 1782. The rehabilitation came next, and it was organized.

Sir Walter Scott stage-managed George IV's visit to Edinburgh in August 1822 and put the king, and the Lowland gentlemen who came to see him, into Highland costume. The clan-by-clan code hardened in the trade that followed. In 1842 two brothers from Surrey, John and Charles Allen, calling themselves the Sobieski Stuarts, published the Vestiarium Scoticum. It claimed to reproduce an old manuscript of clan tartans. The manuscript was a fake. Manufacturers used the book anyway. A pattern that a shop could sell as yours had arrived, with a story that placed it in the deep past.

Hobsbawm's own chapter in the same book moves from one country to a rush. He dates a mass production of new rites to roughly the forty years before 1914, when states and movements needed bonds that no longer came from the village or the church. France is his clearest case. In 1880 the Third Republic made 14 July, the anniversary of the fall of the Bastille, the national holiday. The day mixed the official parade with dancing in the street, and it taught a republic that was still young to celebrate itself as the heir of 1789. Primary schools did the daily work. The holiday did it once a year, in public, where a peasant's child and a minister were supposed to be celebrating the same France.

The useful question, when a ceremony comes with a long pedigree, is not the insult "fake." It is the date. When was this version fixed, and who needed a crowd to feel that it had always been so? A kilt at a wedding and a national day off can both be doing that job. The feeling of age is often the part that was built last.

The kilt's origin story has been pulled at since. Tartan cloth is older than the nineteenth-century clan code. Dorothy Burnham argued that the short kilt followed a change in the loom, which turned out narrower cloth, rather than one man's idea beside a furnace. The forgery has held. The Vestiarium Scoticum was not a sixteenth-century manuscript, and the trade sold its patterns all the same.
