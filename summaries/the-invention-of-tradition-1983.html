In 1727 Thomas Rawlinson, an English Quaker ironmaster from Lancashire, took a lease on woodland at Invergarry, near Inverness, so he could smelt ore shipped up from home. The Highland men he hired wore the belted plaid, one length of cloth that served as cloak and kilt. For felling timber and tending a furnace it was, in the account Hugh Trevor-Roper uses, a cumbrous habit. Rawlinson sent for the tailor of the regiment at Inverness and had the plaid cut down into a separate lower garment, with the pleats already sewn. That is the philibeg, the small kilt. Rawlinson wore it. So did Ian MacDonell of Glengarry, and the clansmen followed their chief. A Highland gentleman who had known Rawlinson wrote this down in 1768. It was published in 1785 and, on the record Trevor-Roper uses, excited no dissent. Trevor-Roper's essay in The Invention of Tradition (1983) takes the episode as proof that a garment later treated as ancient Highland dress was an eighteenth-century workshop fix.

David Cannadine's essay in the same book watches a ritual grow up in public. In 1820 The Black Book, a radical attack on the English establishment, called crowns, coronets, gold keys, and white wands ridiculous once people had decided that government was there to deliver happiness at the least expense. Forty years later Lord Robert Cecil, the future Marquess of Salisbury, watched Queen Victoria open Parliament and wrote that the English had no gift for ceremonial. By her Golden Jubilee in 1887 and her Diamond Jubilee in 1897, the same crown was the center of imperial pageants that audiences took for an old inheritance. Cannadine's sequence is the point. The ceremony became grand in the decades when the monarch's own power was shrinking, and a new performance, repeated, came to look as if it had always been done.

The practical test is the date of the first staging, and who gained by it. A kilt at a modern wedding, a jubilee, a national day, can be a real rite for the people in the room and still be new as a "tradition." Eric Hobsbawm, who introduces the book, defines the habit tightly: a set of practices, ritual or symbolic, that seek to teach values by repetition and that imply a continuity with the past which is often invented. Custom changes as it is used. An invented tradition tries to look frozen.

On the kilt, later Scottish historians have narrowed Trevor-Roper's claim. The Scottish Tartans Museum points to paintings and armorial bearings that show a short kilt before Rawlinson, and treats the philibeg as a gradual change that he helped along. Tartan cloth was already a Jacobite badge, which is why the Act of Proscription of 1746 banned Highland dress after Culloden. The ban was lifted in 1782. Where Trevor-Roper is on firmer ground is the clan tartan as a system. The assignments popularized by the Sobieski Stuarts in the Vestiarium Scoticum (1842), and the Highland costume worn for George IV's visit to Edinburgh in August 1822, a visit stage-managed by Walter Scott, fixed a code of clans and colors that the eighteenth-century Highlands had not worn as a national uniform.
