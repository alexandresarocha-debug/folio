A name for a modern habit

The Invention of Tradition, edited by Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger and published in 1983, is a set of historical essays gathered around one distinction. Hobsbawm’s introduction defines an invented tradition as a set of practices, governed by accepted rules and of a ritual or symbolic kind, which seek to teach certain values by repetition, and which claim continuity with a suitable historic past. The continuity is often factitious. He distinguishes this from custom. Custom is what judges, villages, and workshops actually do, and it changes while pretending only to be flexible. Invented tradition is stiffer. It presents itself as invariant, and the invariance is the point, because the point is to naturalise a community, an institution, or a status that is in fact new or newly anxious.

Hobsbawm locates a burst of such invention in the decades before 1914, when old legitimacies were failing and new ones, mass electorates, national states, empires, and labour movements, needed rituals that felt older than they were. He sorts the inventions into overlapping types: those that establish the cohesion of a group, those that legitimise institutions and authority, and those that socialise people into a belief or a value. The introduction is the theory. The essays are the evidence, and they are evidence about particular places, not about every tradition everywhere.

Scotland and Wales

Hugh Trevor-Roper’s essay on the Highland tradition of Scotland is the most famous, and the most polemical, piece in the book. He argues that much of what the nineteenth century presented as ancient Highland culture was assembled late. The kilt, in his account, was developed in the eighteenth century as a practical garment, and he associates the innovation with Thomas Rawlinson, an Englishman managing an ironworks, rather than with a timeless clan costume. Clan tartans, as a systematic code of patterns, were elaborated later still, helped along by George IV’s visit to Edinburgh in 1822, stage-managed by Walter Scott, and by the forged costume book of the Sobieski Stuarts. The essay is a demolition of a national image. It has been challenged by historians who think Trevor-Roper overstated the novelty and understated older Highland dress. The challenge is fair and it does not remove the essay’s use. A tradition can have older materials and still be an invention in Hobsbawm’s sense, a rule-bound performance that claims a continuity it has in fact constructed.

Prys Morgan’s essay on Wales is the necessary companion, because it shows invention as revival and as consolation, not only as forgery. In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, Welsh scholars, poets, and patriots rebuilt a national culture under pressure from anglicisation. Morgan follows the revival of the eisteddfod, the fascination with druids, and the inventions of Iolo Morganwg, including the gorsedd of bards, which wrapped a modern cultural nationalism in the costume of an immemorial order. Some of the materials were genuinely old. The institutional form that taught the Welsh public to experience them as a continuous national pageant was new. Read beside Trevor-Roper, Morgan’s essay stops the book being a catalogue of hoaxes. Invention can be a creative response to loss.

The British monarchy

David Cannadine’s essay on the British monarchy is the book’s central case of an institution inventing its own antiquity. He argues that the splendid, seemingly timeless ceremonial of the modern crown is largely a product of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Early nineteenth-century royal ritual was often clumsy, poorly organised, and unimpressive to contemporaries. As the crown’s real political power declined, from Victoria’s later jubilees through the reigns of Edward VII and George V, the pageantry was deliberately built up. The monarchy became more visible, more ornate, and more “traditional” as it became less powerful. Cannadine’s paradox is the one the whole volume likes. The performance of permanence was a solution to change. A mass electorate and a mass press could be invited to love a crown that no longer governed in the old way, provided the crown looked as if it had always been loved in exactly that costume.

The essay is also a warning about sources. Travellers and journalists who declared a ceremony ancient were often looking at something a committee had recently redesigned. Antiquity, in this field, is a claim made by the ceremony about itself. Cannadine’s method is to check the claim against the administrative record of funerals, coronations, and jubilees. The result is not an argument that the monarchy is fake. It is an argument that its modern public form has a date, and that the date is recent.

Empire, in India and in Africa

Bernard Cohn’s essay moves the same argument to British India. Colonial authority, he shows, was represented through rituals that claimed to be Indian tradition interpreted by a benevolent paramount power. The Imperial Assemblage of 1877, at which Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India, is the set piece. British officials borrowed, rearranged, and sometimes invented Indian forms of durbar, precedence, and gift, and then treated the result as the authentic order of India. The point was political. A foreign state needed a language of legitimacy, and it found one by codifying a tradition it could preside over. Cohn’s essay is about representation as power. The categories through which India was displayed were also categories through which it was ruled.

Terence Ranger’s essay on colonial Africa makes the further move, which is that invented tradition did not stay in the hands of the inventors. Europeans in Africa devised traditions for themselves, in the regiment, the school, the club, and the hunt, so that a small ruling class would have the rituals of a hierarchy. They also codified what they took to be African tradition: tribes, customary law, chiefly authority, defined and written down by officials and missionaries who needed a stable counterpart to govern through. Ranger’s point is that this codification froze practices that had been more fluid, and that Africans then took up the results, using the new “custom” in disputes over land, chieftaincy, and status. Invention, here, is not a European monologue. It is a colonial production that local actors learned to play. Ranger later softened the language of pure invention, preferring to speak of imagination and reshaping. The 1983 essay is the stronger, cleaner statement of the original claim.

Mass-produced traditions in Europe

Hobsbawm’s own historical essay, on the mass production of traditions in Europe between about 1870 and 1914, returns the argument to the metropole and widens it beyond courts. New national states and old ones facing new electorates built public rituals at speed: Bastille Day, the German imperial ceremonial, flags, anthems, statuary, and the cult of a national past taught in schools. Sport acquired its modern civic form in the same decades, with cup finals and revived Olympic games functioning as calendars of belonging. The labour movement, excluded from much of this official nation, produced a counter-calendar, above all May Day from 1890, with its own marches, flowers, and claims to antiquity. Hobsbawm’s point is industrial in a double sense. The traditions were produced in bulk, like other goods of the period, and they were produced because industrial society had broken the older habits by which people had known their place.

This essay is the volume’s bridge to the wider theory of nationalism. Invented traditions are not a curiosity of kilts and coronations. They are one of the ways a society that has become mobile, literate, and mass-political makes a shared past. They do not, by themselves, explain why nations exist. They explain how nations, classes, and empires make themselves feel obvious.

Limits

The volume’s limits are those of its method and of its title. A collection of essays on successful inventions has little to say about traditions that failed to catch, so the reader can come away thinking invention always works. The tone of exposure, especially in Trevor-Roper, can suggest that once a practice has been dated it has been explained away. Hobsbawm’s introduction is more careful. An invented tradition that people come to live inside is a real social fact, and the appropriate response is to ask what it was invented to do, not to congratulate oneself on not being fooled. There is also a risk of over-extension. Not every old practice is a late forgery. The book’s own distinction between custom and invented tradition is useful only if some customs are allowed to be genuinely transmitted rather than redesigned in the age of jubilees.

Ranger’s later reconsideration is a useful check on the African essay, and historians of Scotland have complicated the Highland chapter. The collection remains a model of how to study the recent past of supposedly ancient things. It is a set of cases with a sharp introduction, not a general theory of culture.

Why it matters here

The idea paired with this book is that nations are imagined, modern, and closely tied to the technologies and rituals that make a large community feel continuous. Anderson explains the imagined community of readers. Gellner explains the industrial pressure for a standardised high culture. Hobsbawm and Ranger show the symbolic work that follows: the production of a past that makes the new culture and the new state look as if they had always been there. Tartan, the Welsh gorsedd, the modern royal jubilee, the Delhi durbar, customary law in colonial Africa, and the national festivals of Europe before 1914 are the cases. Here, the book is the reason “imagined” does not mean “vaguely unreal”. It means built, often recently, often by people who wrote the rules down, and then forgotten as a construction because the repetition did its job.
