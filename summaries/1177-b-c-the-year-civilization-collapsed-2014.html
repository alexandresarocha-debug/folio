In the eighth year of Pharaoh Ramses III, which Eric H. Cline dates to 1177 BC, Egypt recorded a fight against a coalition the king listed by name: the Peleset, the Tjekker, the Shekelesh, the Danuna, the Weshesh, and, in a related text, the Shardana. The Egyptians did not call them the Sea Peoples. On the wall of Ramses's mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, the inscription says the foreign countries "made a conspiracy in their islands," and that no land could stand before them, from the Hittite kingdom on. Ramses claimed the victory. Egypt was the great power that held, and it held as a poorer country. Within a century the other palace powers of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean were gone or transformed.

A letter from the king of Ugarit, a trading city on the Syrian coast, shows the same years from a place that did not hold. It begins, "My father, now the ships of the enemy have come. They have been setting fire to my cities and have done harm to the land." For a long time the tablet was said to have been found in a kiln, baked and never sent, because the raiders returned before the clay could go out. Cline reports that a fresh look at the find-spot put the tablets in a basket that fell from an upper floor, not in a kiln. The letter is evidence of enemy ships and burning towns. It is not proof that it was written on the city's last afternoon, and it may belong to an earlier wave, the one Egypt fought in 1207 BC under Merneptah, rather than to 1177.

Cline's conclusion is that this was one trading world, and that it failed as a system. Greek palaces, the Hittite empire, and the coastal cities depended on one another for tin, copper, grain, and allies. Drought, hunger, earthquakes, revolt, broken trade, and raids had each been survived before. Arriving together, in a network with no healthy neighbor left to call, they were too much. The year in the title is a pin in a crisis that ran for decades. The practical lesson is about tight supply. A port that loses its metal, its grain, and its security at the same time cannot borrow them from a partner who has lost the same things.

Later archaeologists have pressed on how neat that picture is. A. Bernard Knapp and Sturt W. Manning, writing in 2016, accepted that several climate records show drier conditions in the eastern Mediterranean late in the second millennium BC. They also argued that those records still cannot be tied to particular destructions more closely than about a century, and that the region is not one climate. The fires, the abandoned palaces, and the end of the old diplomatic world are not in doubt. A single year is a way of telling the story, not a date on which everything fell at once.
