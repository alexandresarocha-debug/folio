A world that traded, and then a year

Eric Cline's 1177 B.C.: The Year Civilization Collapsed, published in 2014, is a history of the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean and of the break that ended it. The year in the title comes from an Egyptian inscription. In the reign of Ramesses III, a confederation of attackers whom the Egyptians called the Sea Peoples was fought in a great battle, and the date conventionally attached to that campaign is about 1177 BC. Cline does not think civilisation ended on a particular afternoon. He uses the year as a pin in a much wider collapse. Between roughly 1200 and 1150 BC the palace societies of the Aegean, the Hittite empire in Anatolia, and the trading cities of the Levant were destroyed or transformed, and Egypt, which survived, survived diminished. The opening claim is that this was one interconnected world, and that it failed as a system. No single invader, and no single drought, is a sufficient cause. A network that had grown efficient by depending on distant partners became fragile in exactly the same places.

The book is written for general readers and argued with specialists. Cline wants the Late Bronze Age to be understood as something closer to an early globalisation than to a set of isolated monumental cultures. Tin, copper, grain, timber, luxury goods, and diplomatic gifts moved across long distances. Kings wrote to each other as members of a club. When the club and the trade both failed, the failure travelled.

What the interconnected world looked like

The early chapters reconstruct the international age at its height. The Amarna letters, the diplomatic archive from fourteenth-century Egypt, show Great Kings addressing one another as brothers and arranging marriages, gold, and troops. The Uluburun shipwreck, a cargo of the period found off the Turkish coast, shows the material counterpart: a single voyage carrying copper, tin, glass, ivory, and other goods that no one region produced in combination. Mycenaean palaces in Greece administered territory in the script called Linear B and imported and exported on the same sea. The Hittite empire held Anatolia and reached into Syria. Ugarit, on the Syrian coast, was a commercial city whose archives record ships, grain, and anxiety. Egypt under the New Kingdom was the wealthiest of the powers and the one that left the most self-confident monuments. Cline's point in assembling these familiar exhibits together is scale. This was not a world of subsistence villages that happened to have a few palaces. It was a world in which palaces and merchants had come to rely on distant supplies, especially of metals. Bronze is an alloy. A region without tin, or without a reliable way to buy tin, cannot keep a bronze economy by wanting to.

He also describes the political style that went with the trade. Treaties, gift exchange, extradition of fugitives, and a shared diplomatic language made the powers legible to one another. Legibility is a kind of stability. It is also a channel. A famine, a revolt, or a rumour in one court became the business of the others, because the others needed the grain, the ports, or the alliance. Cline is preparing the later claim that the same links which spread prosperity were available to spread collapse. He does not sentimentalise the age. It was hierarchical, militarised, and dependent on forced labour and on the extraction of surplus by palaces. Complexity and inequality were both high. That combination is part of what he thinks made the fall so steep.

The break, and the Sea Peoples

The middle of the book is the crisis itself. Cities were burned. The Hittite capital and the Hittite state disappeared as a great power. The Mycenaean palaces were destroyed and Linear B ceased to be used, taking the administrative script of the Aegean with it. Ugarit was destroyed and its last letters, which include appeals for grain and warnings of ships, are among the most vivid documents of a society that could see trouble coming and could not stop it. Egypt records battles and a defensive war against the Sea Peoples, and then a long contraction. The attackers have attracted legends out of proportion to the evidence. Cline treats them as a real element of the crisis, a mixture of raiding and migration by groups some of whom may already have been known to the powers, and not as a mysterious race that appeared from nowhere and ended civilisation for sport. Ramesses III's inscriptions are a pharaoh's account of a victory. They are not a full explanation of why the Hittite empire or the Mycenaean palaces fell.

Older scholarship, he argues, reached too quickly for that single explanation because a named enemy is narratively satisfying. The archaeology does not support a one-cause story. Some sites were burned, some were abandoned, some continued in reduced form. The timing is a horizon of decades rather than a single campaigning season. A theory has to cover destruction, migration, the loss of writing in some regions, the survival of Egypt as a poorer state, and the later emergence of new peoples, including the Israelites and the Philistines, in a rearranged Levant. One battle in one year cannot do that work. The year remains useful because it marks, in a contemporary royal text, the moment when even the strongest survivor knew that the sea had become a threat.

A perfect storm

Cline's own causal chapter is an argument for combination. He lines up drought, for which pollen and other climate proxies from the eastern Mediterranean have been accumulating, earthquakes that clustered in the period and damaged cities, internal revolt of people pushed past what palace economies would bear, the cutting of trade routes that left bronze-using societies short of metal and of confidence, and the migration and raiding visible in the texts. Any one of these, he suggests, might have been survivable. The Late Bronze Age powers had survived droughts, quakes, and wars before. What was new was the arrival of several stresses while the system was tightly coupled. A palace that lost its tin, its grain surplus, and its security at the same time could not call on a neighbour who was losing the same things. Failure cascaded. The metaphor he reaches for is the perfect storm, and, more analytically, a systems collapse of the kind complexity theory describes. Tight coupling is efficient when every partner performs and dangerous when one partner fails, because the failure does not stay local.

The conclusion is deliberately modest about ranking the causes. Cline does not claim to have weighed a percentage of blame for climate against a percentage for the Sea Peoples. He claims to have shown that the search for a single culprit is the wrong search. He also draws the contemporary analogy with care and with enthusiasm. A globalised world that depends on long supply chains, just-in-time metals, and stable partners has structural rhymes with the Late Bronze Age, and should notice that complexity does not come with a guarantee. The analogy is a lesson, not a prediction. The ancient powers did not have modern states, modern science, or modern transport. They did have interdependence, and interdependence was not enough to save them when several shocks coincided.

What the evidence supports, and where later work has pushed

The book's evidence is the standard material of the field, handled by someone who wants the pieces in one place: excavation reports, the diplomatic and commercial tablets, the Egyptian reliefs and inscriptions, the shipwreck, and the growing palaeoclimatic literature. Its originality is the synthesis and the refusal of monocausation, not a new dig. Specialists already knew every individual fact. Many of them already doubted the Sea Peoples as a sole cause. Cline's service was to make the systems version the public version, and to be honest about remaining ignorance. We do not have a full narrative from the Hittite side of their own end. We do not have a census. We have a horizon of destruction and a set of partial texts.

Subsequent work has continued to pull climate into the story, and Cline has written a sequel, After 1177 B.C., about the societies that came through the aftermath, some of them by disappearing as palaces and reappearing as something else, some by adapting and flourishing. The sequel is a useful correction to the drama of the first title. Collapse was real for the palace systems. It was not the end of everyone who lived through it, and the alphabet that later Greek and Latin writing depends on has a history in the rearranged Levant of the early Iron Age. The 2014 book should be read as an account of a systemic failure, not as a claim that history stopped. Its lasting contention is the one the title slightly oversells and the text then qualifies. The Late Bronze Age ended because several things went wrong in a world that had organised itself so that they would go wrong together.

Why it matters here

The card bronze-age-collapse is this book. The interconnected economy, the Amarna letters, the Uluburun wreck, the Sea Peoples and the battle dated to about 1177, and the perfect storm of drought, earthquake, famine, revolt, broken trade, and raiding are the card's summary of Cline's argument. The card's lesson, that tightly coupled systems spread prosperity and collapse along the same links, is his systems point stated for a general reader. The palaeoclimate paper by Kaniewski and colleagues, which the card cites as one line of evidence pulling climate into the story, is one of the studies behind his drought chapter rather than a rival theory. Folio uses 1177 B.C. as the narrative that keeps the Late Bronze Age collapse from shrinking back into a single mysterious invasion, and that gives the path a worked example of complexity failing at a civilisational scale.
