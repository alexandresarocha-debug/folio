The question

By the early 1970s, the dominant picture of judgement under uncertainty still borrowed its prestige from statistics and from economics. A rational person, on that picture, weighs evidence the way a well-trained statistician would: prior odds, likelihoods, sample size, and the difference between a random fluctuation and a real signal. Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman did not deny that people can do this when the problem is set out as a textbook exercise and the incentives reward care. Their question was ruder. When people have to judge probability, frequency, or a numerical value in ordinary time, with ordinary information, what procedures do they actually use, and how do those procedures fail?

The 1974 paper in Science, Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases, is the short public statement of a programme they had already been running in a series of experiments and theoretical notes. It is not a single experiment with one dependent variable. It is a map. Tversky and Kahneman argued that people rely on a small number of heuristics, mental shortcuts that usually work and sometimes mislead in systematic ways. They organised the map under three headings: representativeness, availability, and adjustment from an anchor. Folio's card on anchoring lives in the third of those headings, but the paper only makes sense if the third is read as part of the same argument as the first two.

Representativeness

Representativeness is the habit of judging probability by similarity. Asked how likely it is that an object or a person belongs to a class, people ask how much the instance resembles the stereotype of the class, and they treat that resemblance as if it were the probability. The shortcut is often sensible. A description that sounds like a librarian is, other things equal, more likely to be a librarian than a description that sounds like a farmer. The failures come when other things are not equal, and people ignore them anyway.

The paper walks through several of those failures. People neglect base rates. If a description is a good match for a profession that is rare, the match can still be weak evidence, because the profession is rare. People are insensitive to sample size. A hospital that records the proportion of boys among newborns will produce extreme daily proportions more often if it is small than if it is large, because small samples wander. Asked which hospital records more days on which more than sixty per cent of the babies are boys, many people say the two hospitals are about the same, or they chase the story rather than the sample size. People also expect chance sequences to look representative of randomness. A short run of coin tosses that alternates neatly feels more random than a run with a clump of heads, even though both are compatible with a fair coin. And people misunderstand regression to the mean. An outstanding performance is likely to be followed by a less outstanding one, not because a cause has reversed, but because extreme scores are partly luck.

None of these are presented as proof that people cannot learn statistics. They are presented as the default. When the structure of the problem is hidden inside a story, similarity and narrative fit outrun the rules that a statistics course would recommend. That claim is the template for the rest of the paper. A heuristic is not a quirk that appears once. It is a procedure that is applied across many problems and that produces a family of biases with the same shape.

Availability

Availability is the habit of judging frequency or probability by the ease with which examples come to mind. If instances are easy to recall, the class feels large. If a risk is easy to imagine, it feels likely. Again the shortcut is often a friend. Frequent events really are easier to recall, so availability tracks the world much of the time. It fails when ease of recall is driven by something other than frequency: recency, drama, personal experience, or the way memory is organised.

Tversky and Kahneman illustrate the last of these with a letter-counting puzzle that has become a classroom staple. Is it more common for a word in English to begin with the letter K, or for K to be the third letter? Most people find it easier to search memory by the first letter, so words beginning with K come to mind more readily, and people conclude that they are more common. In typical text the opposite is true. The bias is not ignorance of the alphabet. It is the confusion of ease of retrieval with frequency in the world. The same confusion, they argue, inflates the judged likelihood of vivid causes of death and of risks that have been in the news, relative to quiet risks that kill more people.

The availability section matters for the anchoring card because it shows the authors' method. They are not collecting curiosities. They are showing that a single judgement procedure, applied where memory's filing system does not match the statistic being asked for, yields a predictable error. Anchoring will be the same kind of claim, with a number in the role that an easily recalled example plays here.

Anchoring and adjustment

The third heuristic is adjustment from an anchor. People often estimate a number by starting from a value that happens to be in front of them and then moving. The move is usually insufficient. The final estimate stays too close to the start. The anchor need not be a credible piece of evidence. It can be an obvious accident, and it can still move the answer.

The demonstration that Folio's anchoring card retells is the wheel of fortune. The wheel was rigged to stop on 10 or on 65. In front of the participants it was spun, so the number looked like a product of chance, which it was not. People were asked whether the percentage of African countries in the United Nations was higher or lower than the number they had just seen, and then they gave their own estimate of the percentage. Those who had seen 10 produced a median estimate of 25 per cent. Those who had seen 65 produced a median estimate of 45 per cent. A sham random number had shifted a judgement of a real quantity by twenty points at the median.

Two features of the design are easy to miss and are the reason the result is famous. First, the anchor was transparently irrelevant. Nobody thought the wheel knew anything about the United Nations. The bias is not a matter of trusting a bad source. It is a matter of being pulled by a number that the judge knows is junk. Second, the procedure splits the judgement into a comparative question and an absolute one. The comparative question, higher or lower, forces the person to consider the anchor rather than ignore it. Having considered it, the absolute estimate does not travel far enough away. Tversky and Kahneman treated insufficient adjustment as the working description. Later work, which the card also notes, has argued for a second mechanism, selective accessibility: thinking about the anchor makes anchor-consistent information easier to bring to mind, so the estimate is built from a biased sample of memory. The 1974 paper does not settle that later debate. It establishes the phenomenon and offers the adjustment account.

The paper is explicit that expertise is not a vaccine. People who live with numbers for a living still anchor when the problem is not handed to them in a form that triggers a formal calculation. That is why the result travelled out of the laboratory and into negotiations, valuations, and legal decisions. A first offer, a listing price, or a prosecutor's demand can function as the wheel did, with the difference that those anchors are strategic rather than rigged.

How the authors read their own evidence

Tversky and Kahneman did not claim that heuristics are irrational in the everyday sense of the word. They described them as adaptive procedures that reduce a hard problem to a simpler one. Representativeness reduces probability to similarity. Availability reduces frequency to memorability. Anchoring reduces estimation to adjustment. The biases are the price of the reduction. A reader who wants the paper to say that people are foolish will be disappointed, and a reader who wants it to say that the biases are rare party tricks will be disappointed too. The tone is diagnostic. These are errors you should expect, in a specifiable direction, under specifiable conditions.

They also marked the limits of what a Science article of this kind can show. Many of the demonstrations are short problems given to students or to other convenient groups. The paper's authority comes from the convergence of many such demonstrations, not from one definitive field study. Where a particular vignette is fragile, the programme's later history has said so. Kahneman himself later conceded that some striking priming results associated with the broader heuristics tradition did not hold up. The anchoring result, by contrast, became one of the more robust members of the family, a point Folio's card records by pointing to the Many Labs project. Robust is not the same as universal. Incidental anchors that people barely notice have produced smaller and less consistent effects than anchors people are asked to consider. The 1974 design sits in the stronger class, because the comparative question makes the number impossible to skip.

What the paper is not

It is not a theory of every mistake. It does not discuss motivated reasoning, group pressure, or the way desires bend belief. Those come from other traditions.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Anchoring: the first number sticks, is a close reading of this third heuristic and of what was done with it afterwards. The wheel, the two medians, and the moral for negotiation all come from this article and from the line of research it authorised.
