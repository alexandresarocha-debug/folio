In 1979 Lyall Watson published Lifetide and told a story about the macaques of Koshima. A young female had started washing sandy sweet potatoes in 1953. The habit spread slowly. Then, Watson wrote, in the autumn of 1958 something else happened. He said he was "forced to improvise the details." Suppose, he wrote, that ninety-nine monkeys were washing potatoes, and that on a Tuesday morning one more learned it in the ordinary way. By that evening almost everyone was doing it. The habit had also, he said, jumped the sea and appeared in other colonies, including the troop at Takasakiyama. He called the sources "personal anecdotes and bits of folklore among primate researchers," and he said the scientists who suspected the truth were reluctant to publish it for fear of ridicule.

Ken Keyes Jr. repeated the claim in The Hundredth Monkey (1982). After the hundredth Koshima monkey, he wrote, colonies of monkeys on other islands began washing their sweet potatoes. The book uses the story as a hope about nuclear weapons: you might be the person whose small act tips the world. The hope is separate from the monkeys. The monkeys are a factual claim, and Ron Amundson checked it.

Amundson's 1985 article in the Skeptical Inquirer goes back to Masao Kawai's 1965 paper in Primates, the source Watson cites. The Koshima troop numbered 20 animals when provisioning began in 1952, and 59 in 1962. In the numerical sense there was never a hundredth monkey on the island. Kawai's tables give a date for each animal. In March 1958, 2 of 11 monkeys over seven years old washed potatoes, and 15 of 19 monkeys between two and seven did. That is 17 of 30 animals who were no longer infants. By 1962, 36 of 49 had the habit. The troop and the number of washers had each grown, over four years, by animals born and animals taught one at a time.

The year Watson treats as the miracle was a bad year for new learners. Amundson counts Kawai's table: only two monkeys learned potato washing in 1958, the young females Zabon and Nogi. An average of about three a year had learned it over the five years before. Kawai is not puzzled by 1958. He splits the record into individual copying, up to 1958, and what he calls pre-cultural spread after that, when youngsters such as Imo's son Ika, born in 1957, grew up with washing as ordinary feeding. Nothing in the paper describes an evening on which the rest of the troop suddenly knew.

The practical point is about secondhand numbers. A story that says "once enough people know, everyone knows" is attractive precisely because it skips the slow part. On Koshima the slow part is the data. A habit moved along kin and playmates, juveniles got it, most of the older males did not, and other islands did not wake up knowing it because Koshima had crossed a secret count.
