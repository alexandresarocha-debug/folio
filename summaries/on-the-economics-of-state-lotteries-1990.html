By 1990 most US states ran lotteries, and Charles Clotfelter and Philip Cook of Duke University had spent years studying them. This review, written for a general audience of economists, asks what a lottery ticket is as a purchase and why so many people buy one.

Seen as a financial asset, they write, a ticket offers a prospect of prizes in return for 50 cents or a dollar. Because the expected value of the prizes is typically only half the ticket price, it is natural to ask why so many adults consider it worthwhile. Lotteries return far less than commercial gambling, where slot machines, for example, pay back about 89%.

They give a concrete price. In the straight three-digit numbers game, the chance of winning is 1 in 1,000 and most states pay 500 to 1, an average payout of 50%. If the unit you are buying is one dollar of expected prizes, its price is $2.00. In Massachusetts, which paid 700 to 1, the price was $1.43.

Careful players can sometimes do better. When a lotto jackpot rolls over through a series of drawings with no winner, it may even be possible to place a bet with an expected return greater than its cost. But such occasions are rare indeed, they write, and normally this asset has no place in the portfolio of a prudent investor.

Why do people play? For some it is a pastime, and states encourage the idea that playing supports good causes. When Californians were asked whether they played more for fun or for the money, they were about evenly divided, but among those with incomes below $30,000, 25% more cited money than fun. Surely, the authors conclude, the hope of private gain is what sells most tickets.

What this means in real life: on average, half of every dollar spent on tickets is gone before the draw.

Caution: payout rates and games have changed since 1990, and this is a review rather than a new study.

Source. Clotfelter, C. T., and Cook, P. J. (1990). On the economics of state lotteries. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 4(4), 105-119. doi:10.1257/jep.4.4.105. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.4.4.105
