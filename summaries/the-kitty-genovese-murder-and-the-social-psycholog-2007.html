Kitty Genovese was murdered in Kew Gardens, Queens, on 13 March 1964. Two weeks later, on 27 March, a front-page New York Times article by Martin Gansberg reported that 38 respectable, law-abiding citizens had watched a killer stalk and stab her in three separate attacks over more than half an hour, and that not one of them had telephoned the police. Rachel Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins asked how well that story holds up, and why it became the founding tale of bystander research.

Drawing on trial documents and the research of a local resident, Joseph De May, they found little evidence for the classic account. Only five witnesses testified at the trial, and only three of them were eyewitnesses who saw Genovese and her attacker together; the prosecutor later recalled finding only about half a dozen people who had seen anything useful. There were two attacks, not three, and the second, fatal one took place inside a building's stairwell, out of sight of most neighbours.

Some people did act. One neighbour shouted at the attacker and scared him off after the first assault. There are reports that calls were made to the police, including by the father of a fifteen-year-old witness, though no emergency 911 system existed then. According to the prosecutor, one resident, Sophie Farrar, was telephoned by a neighbour, immediately phoned the police and then rushed to Genovese's side.

The authors argue that the story became a modern parable. It helped launch valuable research, but it also fixed attention on the dangers of groups and drew attention away from the ways groups can help.

What this means in real life: a vivid news story can shape a whole field of science, and it is worth checking founding anecdotes against the primary record.

Caution: the paper is a historical and conceptual analysis rather than a new experiment, and some details of that night, such as who called the police and when, remain uncertain. It challenges the story, not the laboratory finding that responsibility can be diffused.
