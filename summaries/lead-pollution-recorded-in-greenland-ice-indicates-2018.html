Joseph McConnell and his colleagues melted 423 meters of the North Greenland Ice Core Project core, NGRIP2, and measured the lead in it continuously, about a dozen samples for each year of the Roman era. The chronology is good to about one or two years. In the paper they published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 2018, the lead that is not dust or volcanic background is converted, with an atmospheric transport model, into an estimate of European emissions from lead-silver mining. Annual estimates run from 0.3 to 3.8 kilotons. During the first-century peak they average 1.1 kilotons a year. Emissions rose sharply from 17 BC and stayed high until the AD 160s. They then fell with the Antonine plague, which began in 165, and they stayed low for more than five hundred years. The level during the Roman peace was nearly four times the level of the late Republic.

The same curve moves with wars in the mining districts and with the silver coin. Emissions dropped at the outbreak of the First Punic War, fell again during the Sertorian War in Spain (80–72 BC), and dropped during Caesar's civil wars. A decline from the highest first-century peak comes in AD 9, when Rome abandoned mines east of the Rhine after the defeat in the Teutoburg Forest. The silver content of the denarius tracks the same history: a debasement in AD 64 lines up with a fall in emissions, and in the third century the coin's silver content went under 4 percent at the low point of the lead record. An older Greenland study, using only 18 samples, had suggested that the Republic produced more lead than the empire. The new, year-by-year record reverses that.

The ice is a ledger of smelters, not of every farm in the empire. Most of the signal comes from Spain and from northwestern Europe, where the wind carries pollution efficiently toward this part of Greenland. A plague or a war that shut Spanish mines shows up in snow that fell thousands of miles away. A good harvest in Egypt does not. The authors put a factor-of-two uncertainty on the overall size of the emissions. The timing, tied to dated events, is the firmer result. Anyone using the curve as "Roman GDP" is reading a silver-and-lead industry as if it were the whole economy.

Source. Joseph R. McConnell, Andrew I. Wilson, Andreas Stohl, and colleagues, "Lead pollution recorded in Greenland ice indicates European emissions tracked plagues, wars, and imperial expansion during antiquity," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115 (2018). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1721818115
