Joseph R. McConnell, Andrew I. Wilson, Andreas Stohl, and their colleagues measured lead in the NGRIP2 ice core from Greenland, at less than a year's resolution, through ice spanning 1100 BCE to 800 CE. The lead is fallout from smelting lead-silver ore, mostly in Europe and above all in Spain. Roman silver was the metal of the denarius, so the pollution is a rough meter of one industry and of the coinage that industry supplied. Their chronology is good to about one or two years. Converting ice into tonnes of emissions is much rougher. They put the uncertainty on the absolute amount at about a factor of two. The shape of the curve is the result they ask you to use.

Emissions were low in the last decades of the Republic, during the civil wars, and nearly four times higher under the early empire. In the first-century peak the estimated annual emissions average 1.1 kilotonnes, inside a range from 0.3 to 3.8. The high plateau runs from a rise beginning in 17 BCE to the 160s CE. It dips in 9 CE, the year of the Teutoburg Forest disaster, when Rome abandoned mines east of the Rhine. It dips again when Nero debased the denarius in 64 CE. It then falls with the Antonine plague, which the authors date from 165 to 193, and stays low for more than five hundred years. The lowest stretch after 900 BCE falls in the crisis of 235 to 284, and especially in the plague of Cyprian, from 249 to 270, when the silver in the denarius dropped below 4 percent. The next clear recovery comes around 750, at places such as the mine and mint at Melle in France and Wirksworth in Britain.

The date of the drop is the awkward fact for anyone dating Rome's economic end to 410 or 476. In this core the break in silver mining comes in the later second century, with a pandemic, and the fifth-century west is already on the low plateau. Lead is one industry, not the whole economy, and the tonnage is uncertain by that factor of two. It is still a year-by-year record of the mining that fed the silver coin, and the fineness of the denarius moves with it. When the ice and the coins agree, the signal is about money as well as smoke.

Source. Joseph R. McConnell, Andrew I. Wilson, Andreas Stohl, and colleagues, "Lead pollution recorded in Greenland ice indicates European emissions tracked plagues, wars, and imperial expansion during antiquity," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115 (2018). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1721818115
