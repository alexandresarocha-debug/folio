Franz Messerli, a cardiologist at St. Luke's-Roosevelt Hospital and Columbia University in New York, started from a real finding: compounds in cocoa called flavanols have been linked to better cognitive performance in some studies. He then asked, tongue partly in cheek, whether a country's chocolate consumption is related to the number of Nobel prizes it wins per head of population.

He took a Wikipedia list of countries ranked by Nobel laureates per capita, including all prizes awarded up to 10 October 2011, and expressed it as laureates per 10 million people. Chocolate consumption per person came from industry sources and from different years for different countries.

Across 23 countries the correlation was close and significant: r = 0.791, P < 0.0001. Leaving out Sweden, which had more laureates than its chocolate intake predicted, raised it to 0.862. Switzerland came top on both measures. From the slope he estimated that about 0.4 kg more chocolate per person per year would go with one more laureate per 10 million people, which for the United States would mean 125 million kg a year. He added that the minimally effective dose seemed to hover around 2 kg a year, with no sign of a ceiling.

He did spell out the logic. A correlation between X and Y does not prove causation, he wrote, but means that X influences Y, Y influences X, or both are influenced by a common underlying mechanism. He then chose the most flattering reading anyway, and the journal noted his disclosure of regular daily chocolate consumption.

What this means in real life: country-level correlations are easy to find and almost impossible to interpret. Wealth, research spending and history all differ between countries at once.

Caution: the paper is a light-hearted note, not a serious study. It compares countries, not people, so even a real link would say nothing about whether chocolate helps any individual think.

Source. Messerli, F. H. (2012). Chocolate consumption, cognitive function, and Nobel laureates. New England Journal of Medicine, 367(16), 1562-1564. doi:10.1056/NEJMon1211064. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23050509/
