In the first year of the Sierra Leone Company, 1792–93, 72 percent of the European settlers died. At Bulama, from April 1792 to April 1793, 61 percent died. In the "Province of Freedom" in Sierra Leone, 46 percent died in the first year. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson use deaths like these as the hinge of a 2001 paper. Where malaria and yellow fever killed Europeans in large numbers, Europeans did not settle in large numbers. They set up extractive states, built to move wealth out. The Belgian Congo is their example. Where Europeans survived, they settled, and they built institutions closer to the ones they wanted for themselves. Those early choices, the authors argue, lasted. Settler mortality centuries ago should therefore predict how safe property is from seizure today, and that safety should predict income.

The diseases were a European problem more than a local one. Philip Curtin's figures, which the paper cites, put annual deaths among local troops serving with the British in Bengal and Madras at 11 and 13 per 1,000. British troops in Britain died at about 15 per 1,000. British troops in Bengal and Madras died at between 70 and 170 per 1,000. The instrument is meant to mark where outsiders could live, not a permanent tax on the people already there. In the base sample of 64 former colonies, their two-stage estimate says that moving Nigeria's protection against expropriation up to Chile's level could, in the long run, raise Nigeria's income as much as sevenfold. They call the sevenfold figure an upper bound, because the institutions score is noisy. Adding distance from the equator did not remove the result. In the abstract they report that, once institutions are accounted for, countries in Africa and countries closer to the equator do not have lower incomes.

Read it as a claim about colonies after the colonial shock, not as a history of the world since farming. It does not explain why Britain industrialized before the Yangzi Delta. It does say that a modern income gap can be the afterlife of a settlement decision. A mine, a plantation, or a forced-labor system organized so that a few people take the surplus will keep producing poverty after the flag changes. A settler society in which more people expect to live under the rules they write has a different afterlife. Knowing that does not tell a poor country which law to pass on Tuesday. It does say that advice which ignores who the rules are for will be captured by the people the rules already serve.

David Albouy, in the American Economic Review in 2012, went back to the death rates. He found that 36 of the 64 countries had been assigned a mortality figure taken from some other country, often on thin or conflicting evidence, and that rates for laborers, bishops, and soldiers on campaign had been pooled in a way that favored the result. Once he pressed those problems, the link from mortality to expropriation risk was no longer robust, and the instrumental estimates often had confidence intervals with no finite end. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson replied that discarding nearly 60 percent of the sample was not justified, and that capping mortality at 250 deaths per 1,000 per year, or dropping Gambia, brought the result back even in the small sample. The design is only as strong as a historical death register built from incomplete colonial records.
