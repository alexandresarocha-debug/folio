Background

By 2001 a long argument about why some countries are rich had settled into camps. One camp, refreshed for a wide public by Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel, emphasised geography. Climate, disease, plants, animals, and the shape of continents had given some parts of the world an early advantage, and that advantage could still be seen in modern incomes. Another camp emphasised the rules under which people produce and trade. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson were already developing the second camp into a historical theory of colonialism. Their paper in the American Economic Review is the empirical attempt to make the argument decisive. It takes the geographic story seriously enough to try to separate it from institutions with a method that, if it works, blocks the obvious reply that rich countries simply choose good rules because they are already rich.

The question

The question is causal. Do institutions that protect property and restrain arbitrary extraction cause higher national income, or are such institutions a symptom of income, of European culture, or of a favourable geography? A simple comparison of countries will not do. Places that are rich tend to have courts, credit, and safer property, and it is circular to congratulate the institutions on the wealth that may have bought them. Places that are poor and unhealthy may be poor because they are unhealthy, and also have predatory governments, so a correlation between bad government and poverty might really be a correlation between malaria and poverty. The paper asks whether a piece of historical variation, the mortality Europeans faced when they tried to settle a colony, can be used to isolate the institutional part of the story from the geographic part.

Design

The sample is countries that were colonised by European powers. The outcome is income per head in the late twentieth century. The institution they measure is a contemporary index of protection against expropriation, a score of how safe private property and investment were from arbitrary seizure, averaged over a period of years. That score is the thing whose effect they want. It is also entangled with income. Their instrument is the mortality of European settlers in the colonial period, drawn largely from records of soldiers and other Europeans in the tropics and elsewhere, assembled by historians of colonial medicine and then extended by the authors to a wider set of colonies. The logic has three steps. Where Europeans died in large numbers, they did not settle in large numbers, and they set up extractive institutions designed to take wealth out rather than to reproduce a European society of settlers. Where Europeans survived, they settled, and they built institutions closer to the ones that constrained government at home. Those early institutional choices, the authors argue, persisted after independence. Settler mortality therefore predicts today's institutions. If the mortality of Europeans centuries ago affects today's income only through those institutions, and not through some other channel, then the relationship between the mortality and income can be used to estimate the effect of institutions themselves.

This is an instrumental-variables design. Its credibility is the exclusion restriction, the claim that historical settler mortality is not influencing modern income by some route other than institutions. The authors argue that the diseases which killed Europeans, yellow fever and malaria prominent among them, are not a simple summary of the disease burden on indigenous populations who had acquired immunity, and that the geographic variables people usually cite, such as latitude, can be included alongside institutions and thereby tested. They report a first stage, mortality predicting institutions, and a second stage, institutions predicting income, with a battery of alternative samples and additional controls. They also compare their institutional measure with the geographic variables directly, and argue that once institutions are in the equation the geographic measures lose their apparent power.

Results they defend

The result they ask the reader to take is directional and large, and it is a result about former colonies. Settler mortality is strongly related to the institutional score. The institutional score, instrumented in this way, is strongly related to income. Countries where the historical logic predicts extractive institutions are poorer, by a margin the authors regard as big enough to account for a substantial part of the income gaps among these countries. Measures of geography that, on their own, seem to predict income do not survive as the main explanation once the institutional channel is allowed to operate. The paper's famous conclusion is that institutions, and not geography, are the deeper cause of the pattern of incomes they study. They present the finding as robust inside the checks they run, including attempts to control for the current disease environment, so that colonial mortality is not merely standing in for being sick today.

Two features of the result are easy to blur and should be kept distinct. First, the institution being credited is a bundle. The expropriation index is a modern summary of rules and of the expectations those rules create. The paper does not isolate an independent judiciary from a land registry from the franchise. It estimates the effect of moving along this bundle. Second, the sample is colonies. The design says nothing directly about why Britain industrialised before the Yangzi Delta, and it says nothing about countries that were never colonised. Its claim against geography is a claim about the colonial world after the colonial shock, not a claim that continents, crops, and germs never mattered.

Interpretation

Read against Diamond, the interpretation is a reassignment of the modern gap. Diamond's chain runs from domesticable plants and animals to dense societies, germs, and technology, and it is a theory of why Eurasia could conquer. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson are talking about differences among the conquered and the settlers after that conquest. Their story is that the disease environment sorted the colonists. High mortality produced predatory states, forced labour, and narrow elites, and those arrangements persisted and now keep incomes low. Low mortality produced settler societies with broader rights, and those arrangements persisted and now support high incomes. Geography enters as the historical sorter of institutions, and then, on their estimates, drops out as a direct cause of income today. A reader can accept Diamond's account of 1492 and still accept this account of why some colonies became Canada and others did not. The paper's own rhetoric sometimes offers itself as the replacement for geographic theories rather than as their sequel. The design supports the sequel more safely than it supports a claim that geography has been irrelevant since the invention of farming.

The persistence step is the one with the largest historical content. Something decided in the colonial period has to survive independence, local politics, and the twentieth century. The authors' later popular work fills that step with narratives of virtuous and vicious circles. In this paper the step is an assumption backed by the correlation, not a documented mechanism for each country. The instrument shows, if it is valid, that the institutional inheritance matters. It does not by itself narrate how a particular elite kept its grip.

Limits and later criticism

The exclusion restriction is the standard objection, and it is not a quibble. The disease environment that killed settlers may have affected the productivity of labour, the pattern of settlement, and the accumulation of human capital in ways that are not institutions in the sense of the index. Colonisers who could settle also brought skills, literacy, and a particular legal tradition, so mortality may be a proxy for who arrived and not only for the rules they wrote. The mortality data themselves became the subject of a sharp exchange. David Albouy argued that many of the rates were assigned from one country to another on thin evidence, and that rates for soldiers on campaign, bishops, and labourers were combined in ways that favour the hypothesis. He found the first stage fragile once those issues were pressed. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson replied that his corrections threw away too much of the sample and that the result survived a fairer reading of the sources. A reader does not have to adjudicate every rate to see the implication. The paper's authority is only as good as a historical dataset that was built, unavoidably, from incomplete colonial records.

There is a further limit of scope that matters for this card. Even a perfect instrumental estimate for former colonies would leave Diamond's deep history standing as an account of why Europe had the ships, the steel, and the germs. It would also leave open the possibility, which Diamond has insisted on, that geography shaped the institutions the instrument treats as the cause. Using settler mortality as an instrument is itself a geographic argument about the colonial moment. The paper shows, subject to the disputes above, that this geographic fact works through institutions to affect income. That is a refinement of geographic determinism as often as it is a refutation of it.

Why it matters here

The card diamond-geography ends by setting this paper against the book. Diamond explains the continental head start by domesticates, axes, and germs. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson argue, with settler mortality as their instrument, that colonial institutions explain modern income gaps better than geography does, once both are allowed to compete in the same equation. The card records Diamond's reply that geography shapes which institutions arise. This 2001 article is the reply's target and the source of the empirical claim. Folio uses it so that the geographic head start is forced to confront a research design, a sample of colonies, a measured institutional bundle, and a set of objections about data and about what, exactly, the instrument isolates. The path does not require the reader to declare Diamond refuted. It requires the reader to see that modern poverty and the Neolithic endowment are different questions, and that this paper is the strongest early attempt to answer the modern one with evidence rather than with a map.
