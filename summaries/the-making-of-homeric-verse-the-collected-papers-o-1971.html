Milman Parry's ideas about Homer are spread across a short list of papers, most of them published in the 1920s and early 1930s. After his death they were gathered by his son Adam Parry, also a classicist, in The Making of Homeric Verse, which came out from Clarendon Press, Oxford, in 1971.

The starting point is a definition. In his 1928 French thesis on Homer's traditional epithet, Parry defined a formula as an expression that is regularly used, under the same metrical conditions, to express a particular essential idea. That sounds dry, but the examples are easy to feel. Homer's dawn is rosy-fingered, his sea is wine-dark, and Achilles is swift-footed. These phrases are not chosen for the sake of the meaning each time. They occupy a certain stretch of the six-foot Greek line, and they fit into it like a part in a machine.

The same fit explains why the phrases can be swapped. Swift-footed Achilles and glancing-helmed Hector have the same metrical shape. A singer who needs to name the hero in that part of the line can pick whichever one suits the story. The singer is not stopping to think about the metre, because the stock of phrases has already solved it. That is what makes quick, spoken composition possible.

Parry did not invent the observation. In 1923 his teacher Antoine Meillet wrote that Homeric epic is entirely composed of formulae handed down from poet to poet. The folklorist Friedrich Krauss, who had done fieldwork with Yugoslav singers, believed they relied on fixed formulas, and Arnold van Gennep compared a good singer to a player handling a hand of cards. What Parry added, with the long essays Studies in the Epic Technique of Oral Verse-Making in 1930 and 1932, was a systematic demonstration of how far the Homeric text is made of such units, and then a plan to look for living singers who worked the same way.

What this means in real life: much of ordinary speech and writing works from ready-made units too, such as stock phrases, openings and sign-offs. Seeing them as tools, rather than as signs of laziness, is one way to understand how fluent people produce language quickly.

Criticism: whether the Iliad and the Odyssey were composed orally is still argued. Parry showed that the verse is formulaic, but formulaic style alone does not settle whether a poem was written.
