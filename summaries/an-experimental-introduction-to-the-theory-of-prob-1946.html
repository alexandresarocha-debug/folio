When Germany occupied Denmark during the Second World War, a number of British subjects there were caught, John Kerrich among them. According to a 1946 review in Nature, he was interned in a camp under Danish control and spent part of his enforced leisure tossing coins. He wrote this short book in the camp, built around his results, as a practical way into the theory of probability.

The coin experiment opens the book. Kerrich tossed a coin 10,000 times and recorded the running count of heads. A review in the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society noted that 2,000 consecutive tosses are printed in full, and that he also reported 5,000 draws of two balls from an urn of four, carried out by a fellow internee at about 400 draws an hour.

His running totals, reproduced in later textbooks, show the law of large numbers at work. After 10 tosses he had 4 heads. After 100, 44 heads, a share of 0.44. After 1,000, 502 heads. After 5,000, 2,533 heads. After 10,000, 5,067 heads, a share of 0.5067. The share of heads moved towards one half, but the count of heads above half did not: it was 2 after 1,000 tosses and 67 after 10,000.

Kerrich used these results to give readers a feel for what a probability is: the value around which the observed proportions cluster ever more tightly as the number of trials grows. From there he built up the addition and multiplication rules and, briefly, the binomial and normal distributions.

What this means in real life: the law of averages is about proportions. In a long run of fair tosses you should expect the share of heads to settle near half, and the raw difference between heads and tails to keep wandering.

Caution: the full book is not freely readable online, and the totals quoted here come from later textbook tables and a published dataset that cite it. One coin, one tosser and one run of tosses cannot show whether that particular coin was perfectly fair, and Kerrich let it bounce on a table.
