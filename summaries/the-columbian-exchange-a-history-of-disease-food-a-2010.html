Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian start from a collapse and a transfer. After 1492, Old World diseases reached populations with no prior exposure to them. The authors report the demographic literature's estimate that upwards of 80 to 95 percent of the Native American population died within the first 100 to 150 years, and they say the exact size of the loss may never be known. The figure they give for one well-studied region is more precise. Central Mexico's population fell from just under 15 million in 1519 to about 1.5 million a century later. Within 50 years of Columbus, the Taíno population of Hispaniola, whose pre-contact size has been estimated anywhere from 60,000 to 8 million, was virtually extinct. Noble David Cook's summary, which they cite, is that the least-affected regions lost 80 percent of their people, a typical society lost 90 percent, and the worst-affected lost everyone.

The same contact moved people in the other direction, under force. Nunn and Qian cite estimates that more than 12 million Africans were abducted and shipped to the Americas between the sixteenth and nineteenth centuries. The demand for that labor came from the destruction of Indigenous populations and from crops, sugar above all, that grew money on American soils. The exchange was not only a catastrophe in the Americas and a gift of new foods to Europe. It was also the reason a plantation economy went looking for captives in Africa. On the quieter side of the ledger, they note a calculation that the greater supply of sugar after 1492 had raised English welfare by about 8 percent by 1850, and coffee by about 1.5 percent. New staple crops, potatoes, maize, sweet potatoes, and cassava, mattered more for how many people the Old World could feed.

The practical point is that a voyage changes diets, disease pools, and labor markets together, and the gains and the deaths do not land on the same people. A European consumer got the tomato, the chili, the potato, and cheaper sugar. A Mexican town lost most of its people. An African port lost the captives the plantations then consumed. Counting only the new foods, or only the conquest, misses the system that tied them. Nunn and Qian also note why the body count stays fuzzy. Estimates of the population of the Americas in 1491 still run from about 8 million to more than 110 million, and if disease ran ahead of the explorers, the first written censuses already describe a population that had been cut down.
