An economist’s map of a historian’s idea

Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian’s 2010 essay in the Journal of Economic Perspectives is not a new archive and not a single statistical test. It is a map, drawn for economists, of a process historians had named and economists had mostly mined for one metal. American silver is the part of 1492 that trade theory already knew how to talk about. Nunn and Qian argue that the larger economic story sits elsewhere: in diseases, in food crops, and in the knowledge and habits that travelled with them. Their Old World is not Europe alone. It is the Eastern Hemisphere. Their New World is the source both of organisms the hemisphere had never farmed and of land on which Old World crops could be grown at a new scale. The paper’s ambition is to show that this biological traffic belongs in any serious account of long-run population, agriculture, and global inequality.

They begin from Alfred Crosby’s term and then sort the traffic into gains and losses that an economist can recognise without pretending the moral accounts balance. The Eastern Hemisphere acquired staple crops it had not evolved: potatoes, sweet potatoes, maize, and cassava, dense in calories, and a second tier of foods that changed how people ate even when they did not dominate the calorie budget, among them tomatoes, chilli peppers, cacao, peanuts, and pineapples. It also acquired, through American soils, a huge new platform for crops the Old World already loved, above all sugar and coffee. The losses were epidemiological and human. Communities isolated from Old World infections met them all at once. The destruction of indigenous populations, together with the profits of plantation crops, pulled enslaved Africans into the Atlantic in numbers the authors put at over twelve million between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries. A paper that stopped at silver would have missed both the calories and the kidnapping.

Disease, in both directions

The disease section is a review of mechanisms and of disputes, not a new body count. Nunn and Qian rehearse why the transfer was so unequal. Europeans brought infections such as smallpox, measles, typhus, and cholera to populations with no previous exposure and therefore no immunity of the kind that comes from growing up inside an endemic disease. The essay treats the resulting mortality as a shock on a scale that invites comparison with the worst epidemics of the Old World, and as a shock that did political work: it weakened states, emptied labour markets, and made colonial settlement and plantation slavery thinkable at the scale Europeans then attempted. They do not claim that every regional collapse had the same mix of virus and violence. They claim that any economic history of the early Americas that starts after the population loss has already skipped the event that set the factor endowments.

The return traffic gets a careful, narrower discussion, centred on venereal syphilis. The authors set out the two families of origin theory. One, the Columbian hypothesis, takes the disease as American, carried back across the ocean in the first decades of contact. The other looks for pre-contact Old World cases and for skeletal signs that might be syphilis or a close relative. They emphasise how hard retrospective diagnosis is, because several infections mark bone and skin in overlapping ways, and because early modern observers did not sort diseases as later medicine would. They then report the drift of biological evidence available to them, including work by Harper and colleagues published in 2008, which they read as favouring a New World origin for the bacterium’s relevant lineage. The economic point of the digression is modest and important. Even the disease that may have moved east was not the equivalent, in demographic force, of the cluster that moved west. The hemisphere that gained the crops was not the hemisphere that absorbed the virgin-soil shock.

Food, calories, and cuisine

The food section is the heart of the paper, and it is where Nunn and Qian are preparing the ground for their own later estimates. They distinguish two channels. The first is introduction: species unknown in Afro-Eurasia arrive and, where climate and custom allow, raise the calories and nutrients that a piece of land can yield. The second is extension: land in the Americas, much of it suddenly underpopulated, is turned over to Old World crops for which European, African, and Asian buyers already had a demand. Sugar is their clearest case of the second channel. It was carried to the Caribbean on Columbus’s second voyage, was being planted in Santo Domingo almost at once, and within a generation was tied to the importation of enslaved Africans and to exports back to Europe. The plantation is an Old World crop on New World land, worked by African labour, and it is as much a part of the exchange as the potato.

On the first channel they linger on the crops that can serve as staples. Potatoes, sweet potatoes, maize, and cassava are singled out because they produce more nutrition per unit of land than many of the grains they joined, and because they tolerate environments in which those grains are unreliable. The potato receives particular attention for a reason the authors state plainly: it can carry a human diet, with a little dairy fat to supply what it lacks, more nearly alone than almost any other single food. Maize does comparable work in Africa and southern Europe; sweet potatoes and maize become important in China; cassava spreads through the tropics. They are explicit that adoption was cultural as well as agronomic. A plant does not enter a cuisine because a calorie table recommends it. Tomatoes remade Mediterranean cooking. Chilli peppers became paprika in Hungary, a defining heat in Indian cookery, and part of Korean fermentation. Tobacco spread so far that it could function, in places, as a stand-in for money. The essay’s title promises ideas as well as food, and this is where ideas appear: not as abstract philosophy carried in books, but as knowledge of how to grow, store, and desire a new plant, and as the slow rewriting of what a proper meal is.

What they claim, and what they leave to the next paper

Because this is a Journal of Economic Perspectives essay, it is allowed to point at research rather than finish it. Nunn and Qian describe a difference-in-differences strategy for the potato: places in the Old World differ in how suitable their land is for the crop, and the crop arrives at a known historical moment, so one can ask whether more suitable places pulled away in population and urbanisation only after the introduction. They present that design as a way to move beyond the older historical observation, which they cite from writers such as Langer and McNeill, that potatoes and population growth went together. Correlation across a century is not a measurement of contribution. Suitability that predates the plant, interacted with the date of its arrival, is closer. The essay does not pretend to be the estimate. It tells the reader the estimate is possible, and why the naive story is not enough.

The same caution governs their treatment of numbers they inherit. They use the slave-trade total as a scale marker, not as a result they have re-estimated. They do not announce a single percentage for indigenous mortality, a field in which the high and low counts still differ by large factors. They do not claim that American crops caused the Industrial Revolution. They cite Crosby’s judgement that the joining of the continents was a prerequisite for the modern population explosion and that it played an important role in industrialisation, and they leave the size of the role as a question their own empirical work, and other people’s, would have to discipline. A reader who wants one coefficient will be sent onward. A reader who wants to know which margins matter, disease against the Americas, calories for the Eastern Hemisphere, coerced labour as the link between the two, will find them drawn more clearly here than in most economic histories of the period.

Limits of a survey

A survey has the vices of its virtues. It cannot settle the syphilis argument, which turns on phylogenetic and palaeopathological evidence that has continued to move. It cannot, in one essay, follow every crop into every region, and the Asian career of maize and sweet potato is sketched rather than reconstructed. Ideas, promised in the title, are the thinnest of the three heads: the paper is much more precise about pathogens and cultivars than about technology transfer, religion, or political models. That is a fair reflection of where measurement was feasible, and it is also a gap. The Columbian Exchange carried legal ideas, missionary institutions, and new racial classifications as well as tubers. Nunn and Qian know this and choose to spend their pages on the channels economists had most neglected.

There is a further limit of audience. Written for economists, the essay sometimes translates a catastrophe into the language of endowments and productivity. The translation is useful if it brings the biological facts into models that would otherwise contain only capital and labour. It is a distortion if it makes the population collapse sound like a change in a variable rather than the death of societies. The authors’ own framing, gains on one side of the ocean and devastation on the other, is the right guardrail. The paper is a guide to a literature, and a prospectus for a measurement, not a substitute for the archives or for Crosby.

Why it matters here

On Folio’s Columbian Exchange card, this essay is the bridge between Crosby’s 1972 book and the numerical claim about potatoes. The card lists crops, diseases, and the slave trade in a single paragraph. Nunn and Qian are the reason that paragraph can be read as an economic argument rather than only as natural history: they sort the same transfers into channels, they say which ones bear on Old World population, and they explain why silver, the economists’ usual American export, is not the whole ledger.
