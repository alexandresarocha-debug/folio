Does heritability change when the environment changes? One of the few studies that can speak to this for Africa compared three populations of the African diaspora, related by ancestry but living in very different conditions: Nigeria, Jamaica and metropolitan Chicago in the United States.

Amy Luke and colleagues measured 1,868 family members in Nigeria, 623 in Jamaica and 2,132 in Chicago. Height and weight were measured rather than self-reported. The team also estimated body fat with a bioelectrical device and measured the hormone leptin. They then split the variation in each trait into an additive genetic part and an environmental part, using how similar relatives were.

The environment clearly shaped the averages. Mean values for every body measure rose along the gradient from Nigeria to Jamaica to the United States, and obesity rose from 5 percent in Nigeria to 23 percent in Jamaica and 39 percent in the US.

For weight, body mass index, fat mass and percentage body fat, heritability was about 50 percent in all three groups. Height was different. Its heritability was 0.62 in Nigeria, 0.74 in Jamaica and 0.87 in the United States. That is the pattern you would expect if growing conditions vary more from family to family in Nigeria, so that more of the differences in height there come from the environment.

The authors themselves stressed the consistency rather than the difference. Their conclusion was that family patterns of body size and fat storage look similar in these related populations across a wide range of environments.

What this means in real life: a heritability figure measured in one country should not be copied to another. Where food, illness and health care differ a lot between families, the environment can explain a bigger share of the differences.

Caution: this is a family study, not a twin study. Relatives share homes as well as genes, and the model used had no separate shared-environment term, so the estimates may mix the two. Some later reviews quote different height figures for this study; the numbers here are from the original abstract. And a much larger 2016 twin analysis found no general rule that height heritability is lower where living standards are lower. One study should not be stretched into a law.

Source. Luke, A., Guo, X., Adeyemo, A. A., Wilks, R., Forrester, T., Lowe, W., et al. (2001). Heritability of obesity-related traits among Nigerians, Jamaicans and US black people. International Journal of Obesity, 25(7), 1034-1041. https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.ijo.0801650
