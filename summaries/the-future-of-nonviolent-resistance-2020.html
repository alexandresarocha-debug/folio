The year 2019 saw what may have been the largest wave of mass nonviolent antigovernment movements in recorded history. Yet in this essay Erica Chenoweth shows that, even as civil resistance became more popular, it was becoming less effective.

Scholars of civil resistance usually define success as the overthrow of a government or the winning of territorial independence within a year of a campaign's peak. Among the 565 campaigns that began and ended over the past 120 years, about 51% of nonviolent campaigns succeeded outright, compared with about 26% of violent ones, a two-to-one advantage.

But the trend has turned. From the 1960s until about 2010, success rates for revolutionary nonviolent campaigns stayed above 40%, climbing as high as 65% in the 1990s. Since 2010, fewer than 34% of nonviolent revolutions and a mere 8% of violent ones have succeeded. Nonviolent resistance still outperformed violence, now by about four to one, because armed struggle became even less successful.

Chenoweth offers two kinds of explanation. Governments have learned and adapted: regimes that were once surprised by sudden mass uprisings have found ways to suppress them without triggering as much sympathy. And movements themselves have changed. In the digital age, street protests can draw huge numbers quickly without a structured coalition to plan and coordinate, so movements increasingly launch mass demonstrations before they have developed staying power or a strategy. Mass demonstrations are not always the most effective way to pressure elites.

What this means in real life: for movements, building organisation, relationships and strategy may matter more than getting large crowds onto the streets quickly.

Caution: the post-2010 figures cover only about a decade, so they may shift as more campaigns end, and the causes of the decline are argued from trends and examples rather than tested directly.
