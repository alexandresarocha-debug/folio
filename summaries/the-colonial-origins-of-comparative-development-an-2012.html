David Albouy went back to the death rates that Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James A. Robinson had used in 2001 to argue that colonial institutions still govern national income. Their base sample had 64 former colonies. Albouy found that 36 of the 64 had been assigned a settler-mortality rate taken from some other country, often on mistaken or conflicting evidence. A rate measured in one place had been copied onto another, and then used as if it were a fact about where Europeans could settle. He also found that the dataset mixed death rates for laborers, bishops, and soldiers, and that many of the soldier rates came from military campaigns rather than from ordinary garrison life. Pooling them, he argues, favored the original hypothesis.

The original paper's logic needs those rates to be comparable. High mortality is supposed to mark places where Europeans could not settle and therefore built extractive states. Low mortality is supposed to mark places where they settled and protected property. If the high rates are campaign deaths, or deaths borrowed from a neighboring territory, the comparison is no longer a comparison of settlement risk. Albouy drops the 36 borrowed cases and adds a marker for campaign rates. In that reworking, the first step of the argument, the link from mortality to a modern index of protection against expropriation, loses its robustness. The second step, using mortality as an instrument for institutions, becomes unreliable. He reports that the resulting confidence intervals are often unbounded.

The practical stake is large, because the 2001 result has been used as a number, not only as a story. It has been cited to say that property-rights institutions, rather than latitude or disease today, account for a big part of the income gap among former colonies. If the instrument is a patchwork of incomparable deaths, that number is not something a government, a lender, or a textbook should treat as measured. The historical claim can still be true in particular places. A plantation colony and a settler colony really did leave different rules behind. Albouy's point is that this dataset does not successfully separate that institutional effect from geography, climate, and everything else that came with the colony.

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson replied in the same journal. They argued that throwing out almost 60 percent of the sample ignored a large body of nineteenth-century evidence on European deaths overseas, and that Albouy's campaign marker was coded inconsistently. They reported that the result returns if the highest mortality figures are capped at 250 deaths per 1,000 per year, or if Gambia, a high-mortality outlier, is dropped, even inside his smallest sample. A reader can hold both points. The original rates are messier than the famous regression suggested, and the messiness does not, by their reply, erase the pattern under every reasonable coding.
