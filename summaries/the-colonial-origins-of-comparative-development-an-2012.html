Background

In 2001 Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson published an article that became one of the most cited empirical papers in economics. They argued that institutions which protect against expropriation are a major cause of differences in national income among former colonies, and they claimed to have isolated that cause by using the mortality of European settlers as an instrument. Where settlers had died in great numbers, extractive institutions were built and then persisted. Where settlers had lived, more inclusive institutions were built and then persisted. Geography, they concluded, was not the operative explanation once this institutional channel was taken into account. The paper was influential enough that a challenge to its data was a challenge to a whole way of talking about development. David Albouy's comment, published in the American Economic Review in 2012, is that challenge. It does not begin by proposing a different theory of prosperity. It begins by asking whether the mortality table can bear the weight that the theory had placed on it.

The question

Albouy asks whether the 2001 result survives a stricter accounting of how the settler-mortality variable was built. The question is technical and, because of what rests on it, historical. If the instrument is a faithful record of the death rates Europeans actually faced in each colony, the original design can be debated on its exclusion restriction and still remain a serious design. If the instrument is in large part a set of assignments, guesses, and mixtures of unlike populations, then the first stage, the relationship between mortality and institutions, may be an artefact of those research decisions. His question is not whether institutions matter in the world. It is whether this celebrated estimate is reliable evidence that they do.

Design

The design of a comment is a reconstruction. Albouy went back to the sources behind the mortality rates, including the colonial medical literature on which Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson had relied, and sorted the sample into rates that were observed for a place and rates that were imputed to it from somewhere else. His central factual claim, stated in the abstract of the comment, is that 36 of the 64 countries in the sample were assigned a mortality rate from another country, often on the basis of mistaken or conflicting evidence about which environments were comparable. An instrument that was presented as a historical fact about each colony was, for a majority of the sample, a conjecture about which other colony it resembled.

He then looked at who was dying in the rates that were observed. The underlying numbers come from different kinds of people, soldiers, labourers, and bishops among them, and from different circumstances, including military campaigns. A campaign death rate measures disease plus the privation of being on the march. A barrack death rate measures the background of a garrison. A bishop's mortality measures yet another social world. Albouy argues that these were combined in a way that favours the hypothesis, because the high campaign rates tend to fall in places that the theory already wants to code as extractive. His reanalysis restricts attention to data he regards as properly comparable, uses indicators for campaign mortality, and examines what happens to the first stage and to the instrumental-variables estimate when the imputed countries are not treated as if their death rates had been counted on the spot. He also attends to the statistical problem that a weak first stage produces instrumental estimates with very wide uncertainty, wide enough, in his report, that the confidence intervals often become infinite in substance, which is another way of saying that the data no longer answer the question.

Results he reports

The result of the comment is negative. Once the data problems are taken into account, the relationship between settler mortality and expropriation risk is not robust. It depends on the imputed countries and on the treatment of unlike mortality rates. Without those supports, the first stage is weak, and a weak instrument cannot deliver a precise estimate of the effect of institutions on income. Albouy states the conclusion in the language of the design. The instrumental-variables estimates become unreliable. He does not publish, in place of the original paper, a different number for the return to institutions that he invites the reader to trust instead. The trustworthy statement, on his view, is that this research design, with the mortality data as they stand, cannot disentangle institutions from the other things that vary across the same countries.

That negative result has a specific shape which later argument sometimes smears. Albouy does not claim to have shown that institutions are irrelevant. He claims to have shown that one influential demonstration of their relevance does not survive scrutiny of its key variable. A theory can be true and a particular estimate false. The comment is an argument for the second possibility. Readers who treat it as a disproof of the whole institutional research programme are making a larger claim than the tables make. Readers who ignore it because the original paper is famous are making a smaller claim than a comment of this kind requires.

Interpretation

Read as a piece of economic method, the comment is a lesson about instruments that come from archives. An instrument is only as good as the measurement of the instrument. Settler mortality looked, in 2001, like the rare historical fact that was both clearly exogenous and clearly connected to institutional choice. Albouy's reconstruction makes it look like a composite, part observation and part the authors' own theory of which places were medically alike. An imputation guided by a sense of the disease environment is not a scandal by itself. Colonial records are full of holes, and every historical dataset requires judgement. The problem is circularity. If the judgement about which countries share a mortality rate is influenced by the same geographic and historical beliefs that the regression is supposed to test, the instrument is no longer an independent lever. It is the theory, entered as a number.

Read as history, the comment is a reminder that soldiers on campaign are not a random sample of the European encounter with the tropics. Campaigns were sent to particular places, at particular seasons, with particular logistics. A high death rate in a disastrous expedition is evidence about that expedition. Using it as the deep cause of a country's modern institutions is a long inferential leap even when the number is right. Albouy shortens the leap's apparent precision. He does not replace it with a narrative of his own about the encomienda or the settler assembly. The comment leaves the historical story of colonial institutions where it was, as a set of cases, and removes the comfort that a single regression had ranked those cases in a definitive order.

The reply, and what the exchange settled

Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson answered in the same issue of the journal. Their reply argues that Albouy obtained his negative result by discarding too much information, including the Latin American and a large share of the African observations, amounting to most of the original sample. They defend the practice of using evidence from comparable disease environments where a local series does not exist, and they argue that his campaign indicator is coded in a way that does not match the historical circumstances consistently. They report that a more careful treatment of outliers strengthens, rather than weakens, their original estimate, and that even versions of the critique which they regard as extreme do not remove the finding. The reply is as much a part of the episode as the comment. A reader who stops at Albouy has not finished the exchange. A reader who stops at the reply has not felt the force of the data problem.

What the exchange settled is narrower than either side's strongest sentence, and it is worth settling for. It established that the mortality variable is a constructed object, that reasonable scholars can disagree about dozens of its entries, and that the famous result is sensitive to those disagreements in a way a casual citation does not suggest. It did not produce a consensus death rate for each colony. It did not end the broader evidence that colonial institutions differed and that the differences have had long lives, evidence which includes a great deal of history that does not depend on this instrument. The mature use of the 2001 paper, after 2012, is to cite it together with the comment and the reply, and to match the strength of one's language to a result that its own authors had to defend at the level of individual rates.

Why it matters here

The card acemoglu-institutions presents the colonial-origins paper as the empirical foundation of the inclusive-and-extractive theory, and it names Albouy's comment as the challenge a serious reader has to follow. This is that comment. It matters here because Folio's card would be incomplete if it offered the 2001 result as a settled demonstration and hid the argument about how the demonstration was built. Albouy supplies the concrete objections, imputed rates, incomparable populations, a weakened first stage, and estimates too imprecise to use. The authors' reply supplies the defence, a larger sample and a different reading of the same sources. The card's promise, that following the exchange is a lesson in how economic history is tested, is kept by reading the comment as a test of a variable rather than as a rival manifesto. Institutions may still be the deepest reason nations differ. After this paper, that sentence cannot be rested on the original mortality table as if the table had never been opened.
