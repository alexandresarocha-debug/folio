Background

In 2005 John Ioannidis published an essay in PLoS Medicine with a title that sounds like an accusation: Why Most Published Research Findings Are False. It is not a survey of fraud, and it does not count papers. It is a short argument in probability, aimed at the question a reader should ask of any claimed discovery. Given how this field chooses questions, how large its studies are, how flexible its analyses are, and how strongly it prefers positive results, what is the chance that a finding which has just crossed the threshold of statistical significance is actually true? Ioannidis called that chance the positive predictive value of a research finding. His claim was that in many branches of contemporary research the chance is low, sometimes low enough that a newly published positive result is more likely to be false than true.

The essay appeared a decade before the Open Science Collaboration's attempt to repeat a hundred psychology studies. Folio's replication card places it first for that reason. The replications were a shock to people who had not done the arithmetic. Ioannidis had done the arithmetic in public, for medicine as much as for psychology, and had said what the shock would look like.

The question

Suppose a field tests many possible relationships and publishes the ones that come out significant. Before any given test, some of those relationships are real and some are not. Let the pre-study odds stand for that mix: how many true relationships there are for each false one that a team might bother to test. A study has a certain power to detect a true relationship if it is there, and a certain rate of declaring a relationship when it is not, the familiar significance threshold. If that were the whole story, one could already compute how many of the significant results are true. Ioannidis added the pieces that ordinary textbook calculations leave out. Bias: the ways a result that would have been negative is turned into a positive one, by selective reporting, by analytic flexibility, by an interest in a particular answer.

The question of the paper is what happens to the positive predictive value when those ingredients take the values that Ioannidis thought realistic. The title is the answer he reached for a wide range of those values. The body of the paper is the map from ingredients to answer, so that a reader can disagree with a particular assumption without losing the machinery.

How the argument is set up

Ioannidis writes the positive predictive value as a function of a few quantities. One is the prior odds that a tested relationship is true. A study that asks a question already supported by a strong body of evidence starts with a higher prior than a study that scans a large space of weak possibilities. Another is power, which falls when samples are small and effects are small. Another is the significance level, the rate at which chance alone produces a positive call. Another is bias, formalised as the proportion of findings that would not have been positive, but become positive because of the way the research is designed, analysed or reported. A further extension considers several independent teams testing related questions and the distortion that appears when the first team to reach significance is the one the field hears about.

He then states the argument as a series of corollaries, each one a comparative claim that follows from the same function. The smaller the studies in a field, the less likely its published findings are to be true. The smaller the effects being chased, the less likely. The greater the number of relationships tested, and the less those relationships were selected because theory made them probable, the less likely. The greater the flexibility in designs, definitions, outcomes and analyses, the less likely. The greater the financial and other interests, and the stronger the prejudice in favour of an answer, the less likely. The hotter the field, meaning the more teams are competing to publish on the same question, the less likely a claimed finding is to be true. These are not six unrelated opinions. They are one model, read six ways.

What the argument implies

Under the assumptions Ioannidis regarded as common in exploratory fields, a significant result is a weak warrant. Most of the hypotheses entering the testing machinery are false or negligible. Power is modest. Bias is not zero. Many teams or many comparisons generate a stream of apparent hits. In that regime the published record, which consists largely of the hits, is dominated by false positives and by exaggerated true positives. The essay's sharpest sentence follows: for many scientific fields, claimed research findings are more often false than true. He singled out areas where samples were small, effects were expected to be small, analysis was flexible, and the prior odds of any particular claim were low. Molecular epidemiology and other discovery-oriented biomedical literatures of the period were among his examples. The logic is not confined to them. Any field that shares the ingredients shares the prediction.

The same function says what would raise the chance that a published finding is true. Larger studies raise power. Pre-specified outcomes and analyses reduce the flexibility that Ioannidis folds into bias. A higher prior, the sort that comes from a replicated phenomenon or a precise theory, raises the base rate of true relationships among those tested. A large confirmatory trial aimed at one question is a different machine from a scan across hundreds of questions. He was careful, though, about a remedy that was already popular. A meta-analysis of a biased literature can concentrate the bias and then stamp it with a larger sample size. Pooling significant findings that were themselves the product of flexibility does not recover the positive predictive value. It can make a false conclusion more precise.

How to read a model like this

The essay is often remembered as an empirical claim that most papers have been shown to be wrong. That is a misreading, and it is worth blocking because it lets critics dismiss the paper for failing a test it did not take. Ioannidis did not audit a random sample of findings and determine their truth. For most published claims the truth is exactly what is in dispute, so an audit of that kind cannot be the method. He offered a generative account: if a field works in such and such a way, its significant results will have such and such a chance of being true. The corollaries can be challenged by showing that a field's priors are higher, its power greater, or its bias lower than the scenarios suggest.

The bias term is the part most easily misunderstood. Ioannidis was not claiming that scientists usually invent data. He was claiming that a system which rewards positive results, and which allows many analytic paths, will convert a share of otherwise negative studies into published positives. That is the same phenomenon Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn later demonstrated with simulations and with an impossible effect of a song on age. Ioannidis left the mechanism of bias partly unspecified on purpose. Selective reporting, outcome switching, stopping rules and conflicts of interest are different routes into one mathematical slot. Anyone who wants to argue that bias is negligible in a particular field has to argue that those routes are closed, not that researchers are decent people. Decency is compatible with a literature that overstates results, if the overstatement is what the publication rule selects.

Limits

A model this compact flattens real fields. Prior odds are not a number one can look up, and two readers will plug in different values. Power is not a single property of a discipline. The scenarios are illustrations, and a critic can say that an illustration was loaded. The algebra forces the criticism to be specific: which ingredient is different, and how different would it have to be before most published findings would be expected to be true?

There is a further limit in the logic of replication itself. If repeats are rare, small and also subject to bias, they will not clean the record. A single underpowered failure can be dismissed, and a single underpowered success can be recruited as confirmation. The prescription is to change the mix of ingredients. Fewer unconstrained comparisons, larger and better-specified tests, less tolerance for hidden flexibility, and more weight on findings that were already likely because evidence had accumulated. Fields that did some of this would earn higher positive predictive values. The title describes a tendency under prevailing conditions.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the replication crisis begins with a fact from 2015, then steps backwards. In 2005 Ioannidis had argued that most published research findings are false, given small samples, small effects and flexible designs. This essay is that argument. It does not mention a Beatles song and it does not replicate a hundred studies. It tells you why a literature can be almost entirely significant and still be mostly wrong, which is the pattern the Open Science Collaboration later reported in miniature: the originals were overwhelmingly significant, and only about a third of the replications were. The gap is what the model predicts when power is limited and bias is not.

Read against the rest of the card, the corollaries are a checklist. Ego depletion and the hormonal side of power posing were small, surprising effects, pursued in flexible designs, in a hot period when positive results travelled fast. A large preregistered multilab replication of ego depletion, Hagger's in 2016, did not recover the original effect. Ioannidis would not have needed the details to know that this class of finding was at high risk. He would also have predicted the other half of the card. Pre-registration, registered reports, larger multi-lab studies and open data are ways of raising power and cutting bias, the two levers his function says matter most. The essay is the reason those reforms are not a fad. They are an answer to a probability.
