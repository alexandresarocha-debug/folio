In 2005 John P. A. Ioannidis, then at the University of Ioannina and Tufts, published a short essay in PLoS Medicine with a hard claim: under the rules laboratories actually use, a published “significant” result is often more likely to be false than true. He did not audit a stack of papers. He wrote down the arithmetic. Imagine a team tests 100,000 gene variants for a link to schizophrenia, and about 10 of them are truly linked, each with an odds ratio around 1.3. The chance that any one variant you pick is a real link is about 1 in 10,000. Give the study 60 percent power to catch an effect of that size, and use the usual cutoff of p = 0.05. A hit that just crosses that line has a chance of being true of about 12 in 10,000. The study raised the odds, and the odds are still terrible.

Bias makes the odds worse. Ioannidis lets a fraction of analyses that should have stayed negative get written up as positive, through dropped patients, new subgroups, or a changed definition of the disease. If that fraction is 10 percent, the chance the reported hit is true falls to about 4.4 in 10,000. If ten independent teams run the same search and one of them announces a significant association, the chance that announcement is true is about 1.5 in 10,000, close to the chance you had before anyone started. The same formulas give a very different answer for a large randomized trial of a treatment that already had a 50 percent chance of working: that finding is true about 85 percent of the time. An underpowered early-phase trial is true about one time in four, and less often once bias is allowed in. A well-powered hunt through many possible exposures, of the sort common in nutritional epidemiology, can be true only about one time in five when real links are outnumbered ten to one.

In ordinary life this is a filter for headlines. A story that a food, a gene, or a supplement “is linked” to a disease is usually the first kind of search: many things were tested, the effect on offer is small, and a positive result is easier to publish than a negative one. A large trial of a treatment doctors already had a reason to test is a different bet. Before you change what you eat, what you take, or what you tell a patient, ask how many comparisons were run, how big the study was, and who gains if the result comes out positive. A single p-value under 0.05 does not answer those questions.

The essay was challenged with data. Leah R. Jager and Jeffrey T. Leek, in Biostatistics in 2014, pulled 5,322 p-values from the abstracts of 77,430 papers in five major journals between 2000 and 2010, and estimated that 14 percent of the reported significant results were false discoveries. Ioannidis answered that those abstracts were a selected slice, heavy with trials and reviews, and that published p-values cannot stand in for every test a field actually ran.
