Background

Leon Festinger and James Carlsmith published Cognitive consequences of forced compliance in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology in 1959. It is the experiment that made cognitive dissonance something more than a provocative idea from Festinger's book and from the field study of a failed prophecy. The claim to be tested was specific. If a person has said something he does not believe, the discomfort of the inconsistency depends on whether he had a good reason to say it. A large reward is a good reason. The inconsistency is explained, and the belief can stay. A trivial reward is not a good reason. The inconsistency remains, and one way to remove it is to decide that the thing he said was true after all. The prediction is the opposite of the obvious reward story, in which the larger payment should produce the larger change of mind.

The question

The question is what people conclude about a dull experience when they have been paid, either very little or a great deal, to describe it as interesting. Reinforcement thinking, the kind then dominant in many laboratories, expected the larger payment to stamp in the compliant behaviour and to leave the person more positive about the task, or at least more willing to say so. Dissonance thinking expected the smaller payment to produce the more positive private attitude. The person paid a token amount has done something awkward, telling a lie about a boring hour, and has almost nothing to point to as justification. Changing the attitude removes the lie. The person paid handsomely has a justification sitting in his pocket. He can think that the task was dull and that he said otherwise because anyone would, for that money. His attitude need not move.

A third group, who did the same dull work and were asked for their attitude without having lied to anyone, gives the baseline. If the small payment pushes ratings above that baseline, and the large payment does not, the result is difficult to tell as a story about rewards making people happy. It is a story about insufficient justification.

How the study was set up

Male students came to the laboratory and spent a long session on deliberately tedious manual work. One task was loading spools onto a tray and off again. Another was turning pegs in a board, a quarter turn at a time, over and over. The point of the work was the boredom. Everyone had a dull hour to explain later, and the dullness was not subtle. When the tasks were finished, most participants were recruited into a small further deception, presented as a favour the experimenter badly needed. The usual assistant who briefed the next student had not turned up. Would the participant tell the waiting student that the tasks were enjoyable, interesting and worthwhile? The waiting student was part of the arrangement. The favour being asked was a lie, told face to face, about an experience the participant had just had.

Payment was the experimental contrast. Some were offered one dollar to do the favour. Some were offered twenty dollars, a large sum for a student at the end of the 1950s. A control group did the tasks and were not asked to lie. Afterwards, in what was presented as a separate evaluation of the department's experiments, everyone rated the tasks. The questions that mattered were how enjoyable the hour had been, how much they felt they had learned, and whether they would be willing to take part in a similar study. The ratings were the attitude. The lie, or its absence, and the size of the pay were the conditions that, on Festinger's account, should determine how much dissonance the lie created. The design only works if the one-dollar participants really did feel under-justified, and the twenty-dollar participants really did feel that the money explained their behaviour.

What they found

The one-dollar group rated the tasks as more enjoyable than the twenty-dollar group. The twenty-dollar group looked much like the controls, who had never said anything pleasant about the work and had no inconsistency to resolve. The small payment, not the large one, went with the sunnier attitude. The same pattern appeared in the willingness to do something similar again. People who had lied for a dollar were, afterwards, more inclined to treat the experience as one they might repeat. People who had lied for twenty dollars had no comparable need to revise the hour they had just spent. They had been paid properly to praise it, and they could leave their private view alone.

The direction is the whole result. A theory that says attitudes follow incentives in a simple way has to work hard to explain why the smaller incentive produced the larger private change. A theory that says attitudes move to justify what one has done, when nothing else will justify it, predicts that pattern in advance. Festinger and Carlsmith present the ratings as evidence that the students had brought their beliefs into line with a counter-attitudinal speech, and had done so only when the speech was poorly paid. The lie itself was held roughly constant. The justification varied. The belief moved where the justification was missing.

How the authors read it

They read the study as a confirmation of the forced-compliance chapter of dissonance theory, and as a direct embarrassment for a reward account of the same facts. Forced compliance, in their terms, means saying or doing something contrary to one's attitude because of pressure that falls short of compulsion. The pressure here was social and financial: a request from an experimenter, a waiting stranger, a fee. It was enough to get the lie. It was not, in the one-dollar case, enough to explain the lie to the person who had told it. Dissonance is the name for the discomfort of holding "the task was dull" beside "I just told someone it was interesting" when no third cognition makes the pair reasonable. Reducing the dissonance by deciding the task was, in fact, reasonably interesting is cheaper than going back to the waiting student and taking the lie back. The twenty-dollar case adds the third cognition: the payment itself explains the lie.

They also draw the boundary the result requires. If the payment had been so small, or the request so weak, that people refused to lie, there would have been no counter-attitudinal act and no dissonance of this kind. If the payment is large enough to justify the act, there is compliance without attitude change. The interesting region is in between: pressure sufficient to produce the behaviour, and insufficient to justify it. That region is where Festinger expected private attitudes to move into line with public acts. The one-dollar condition is offered as a specimen of the region, not as a universal tariff. The general claim is about the relation between justification and change, and it is the claim the card summarises when it says that twenty dollars was sufficient justification for lying and one dollar was not.

Limits

The study is small, it uses male students, and it uses a boredom that an experimenter manufactured. Generalising from peg-turning to the way people justify jobs, marriages or political speeches is a leap the theory invites and the data do not complete. The amounts of money are doing a lot of work. Twenty dollars had to feel like a real justification, and one dollar had to feel like an absurd one. In a different economy, or with a different request, the same sums might sit in different places relative to the threshold. There is also the possibility, discussed for years afterwards, that twenty dollars made participants suspicious, or made them feel they were being bought, so that their ratings reflect annoyance or a wish not to look purchased, rather than the calm absence of dissonance.

Alternative theories were built to explain the same pattern without a special state called dissonance. Daryl Bem's self-perception account says people infer their attitudes from their behaviour and the circumstances, much as an observer would. A person who knows he praised the task for a dollar infers that he must have liked it. A person who knows he was paid well infers that the payment was the reason. The predictions match Festinger's in this design, which is why one experiment could not crown a winner. Later arguments turned on physiological arousal, on whether attitude change requires a feeling of discomfort, and on whether the lie has to feel freely chosen. The 1959 study establishes the pattern more firmly than it establishes a single mechanism.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on cognitive dissonance uses this experiment as the classic demonstration that actions rewrite beliefs. The dull tasks, the one dollar, the twenty dollars, and the higher enjoyment after the smaller fee are the card's story, and they are this paper. The wider theory, that inconsistent cognitions are uncomfortable and that people often change the belief rather than the behaviour, is what Festinger had set out in 1957 and what the prophecy study had illustrated in the field. Forced compliance is the version that can be run in an afternoon and pointed at. It is also the version a sceptical reader can check.

The card's caution is the check that arrived much later. A large multi-site registered replication of the induced-compliance paradigm, published by Vaidis and colleagues in 2024, did not find the classic attitude change. That failure does not edit the 1959 results out of history. It does mean the card is right to treat the famous pattern as something that has not travelled cleanly into a pre-registered, multi-lab world. Read Festinger and Carlsmith for the logic of insufficient justification and for a beautifully aimed comparison. Hold the general claim about attitude change more lightly than the textbooks did, which is exactly the position the card takes. The companions on the card, the prophecy book, the 1957 theory, and Tavris and Aronson's later essay on self-justification, show how far the idea was taken. This paper is the hinge, and a hinge is allowed to creak.
