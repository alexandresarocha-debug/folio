In 1959 Leon Festinger and James M. Carlsmith paid Stanford students to describe an hour of boredom as fun. Seventy-one men from the introductory psychology course, who had signed up for a two-hour study of “Measures of Performance,” spent thirty minutes loading spools onto a tray and emptying it, then thirty minutes turning each of forty-eight pegs a quarter turn, over and over. Afterward the experimenter said the assistant who usually told the next person that the tasks were enjoyable had not shown up. Would the student do it? Some were offered one dollar. Some were offered twenty dollars, a large sum for a student at the end of the 1950s. A control group did the tasks and was not asked to say anything. Eleven men were dropped from the analysis, leaving twenty in each condition. A interviewer who did not know which condition a student was in then asked how enjoyable the hour had been, on a scale from −5 to +5.

The control group, who had never praised the tasks, rated them at −0.45. The twenty-dollar group rated them at −0.05, close to the controls. The one-dollar group rated them at +1.35. The gap between one dollar and the controls was significant (t = 2.48), and so was the gap between one dollar and twenty dollars (t = 2.22). The twenty-dollar men had a reason in their pocket. The one-dollar men had told a waiting student that a dull hour was interesting, and a dollar did not explain the lie. Their rating moved toward what they had said.

The pattern shows up wherever someone has talked themselves into a weak position for a thin reward. A salesperson who has just described a mediocre product as excellent, for a commission too small to boast about, is a candidate for actually coming to rate the product higher. A clinician who has soft-pedaled a side effect to keep a patient in a study, or an employee who has defended a bad decision in a meeting because it was easier than disagreeing, can walk out believing the defense. A large, obvious payoff leaves the original opinion in place. You can say, “I did it for the money,” and keep your view.

A direct descendant of this method, the induced-compliance essay, did not hold up in a preregistered multilab test. In 2024 David Vaidis and colleagues, in 39 labs and 19 countries, had 4,898 people write an essay. Writing a counterattitudinal essay under high choice did not change attitudes more than writing one under low choice. Writing against one’s view did shift attitudes relative to writing a neutral essay, with or without the feeling of choice. The one-dollar result is still the result of this study. The broader claim that felt freedom is what rewrites the belief has not traveled cleanly.
