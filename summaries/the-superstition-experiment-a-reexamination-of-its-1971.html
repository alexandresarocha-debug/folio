John Staddon, then at Duke University, and Virginia Simmelhag of the University of Toronto repeated Skinner's superstition experiment, this time recording in detail what each pigeon did, second by second, between feedings.

They used six pigeons kept at 80% of their free-feeding weight. Food came either every 12 seconds or at varying intervals averaging 8 seconds, regardless of what the birds did, and on a third schedule only after a key peck. A trained observer coded the birds' activities, such as facing the wall where food appeared, turning in quarter circles, or pecking.

In the steady state, behaviour fell reliably into two classes. Interim activities came just after food and were rarely followed directly by it. A terminal response began later in the interval, about 6 to 8 seconds after food on the fixed 12-second schedule, and continued until food arrived. With one exception, the terminal response on the free-food schedules was pecking, which is how pigeons feed. One bird, for example, gradually shifted from putting its head in the food magazine to pecking. No bird switched to a different terminal response once it began pecking, even after 45 to 140 further sessions.

The authors argued that Skinner's account of accidental reinforcement does not fit these results well. Instead they proposed two sets of principles, one generating behaviour suited to the situation, such as food-related pecking when food is expected, and one selecting among those behaviours, drawing a parallel with variation and selection in evolution.

What this means in real life: some apparent superstitions may be natural anticipation of a reward on a timer, rather than a mistaken belief about cause and effect.

Caution: the study used few birds, and later researchers debated how to classify these behaviours. It does not show that accidental reinforcement never happens, only that it is not the whole story.
