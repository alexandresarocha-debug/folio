A question about expertise at its most serious

Birte Englich, Thomas Mussweiler, and Fritz Strack published “Playing Dice With Criminal Sentences” in Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin in 2006. The question is stated in the first lines of their case. Judicial sentences ought to be determined by facts and by law. If the anchoring effects found with students and general-knowledge questions also move the decisions of trained legal professionals, and if they do so even when the professionals know the anchor is irrelevant, then anchoring is not a classroom curiosity. It is a feature of a decision that sends people to prison. The paper reports four studies. All of them use realistic case materials, developed with practising lawyers so that the files would contain the sorts of facts a court would actually have. All of them use legal professionals as participants. The progression of the four studies is a progression of scepticism. Each one makes the anchor harder to defend as a piece of genuine information, until, in the study that gives the paper its title, the anchor is a number the judge has just produced by throwing dice.

The theoretical stake is the one Mussweiler and Strack had been developing in other domains. Selective accessibility proposes that considering an anchor, even to reject it, brings anchor-consistent arguments to mind. A high sentencing demand makes incriminating features of the case easier to think of. A low demand makes the exculpatory features easier. The sentence is then built from a biased sample of the file. The alternative, insufficient adjustment, says that the judge starts at the demand and fails to move far enough. The paper is designed to show the effect under conditions that are embarrassing for either mechanism’s critics, and the last study is designed to make the selective-accessibility account visible in the time it takes a lawyer to classify an argument. Expertise is not treated as a side issue. It is the objection the experiments exist to meet.

A journalist, and then a random demand

The first study asks whether a sentence suggested by a source that ought to be ignored can move experienced professionals. Participants took the role of a trial judge in a fully written case of alleged rape, several pages of incident, statements, and expert opinion, assembled so that practising judges would regard it as complete enough to sentence on. During the procedure they were exposed to a possible sentence contained in a journalist’s question, the sort of number a judge might hear from the press and ought, on any normative account, to set aside. The suggested sentences were high or low. The sentences the participants then passed differed in the direction of the journalist’s number. A comparison with other work, in which the same materials were paired with a prosecutor’s demand, let the authors ask whether a normatively relevant anchor moves people more than an irrelevant one. In that comparison the source mattered for the overall level of the sentence, and the size of the anchoring shift did not depend on it. Relevance, which is the heart of the legal objection, was not the condition the effect required.

The second study removes the last respectable reason to listen. The case is a shoplifting file, a woman who has stolen from a supermarket for the twelfth time. The participants were thirty-nine legal professionals, nearly all of them judges, with an average of a little over thirteen years of experience in court. They were told that the prosecutor’s sentencing demand had been generated at random. They considered whether that demand was too high or too low, heard a defence demand that was held constant, and passed sentence. Those who had received the high random demand gave higher sentences, on average a little over six months, than those who had received the low demand, on average four months. They also reported being reasonably certain about their decisions, and their certainty did not differ by condition. The study that feels most like a refutation of the “but judges are different” objection is this one: long experience, a realistic file, an explicit statement that the number was random, and a shift in the sentence anyway.

The dice

The third study is the one the title names, and it closes a remaining escape. Perhaps, in the second study, professionals did not quite believe the experimenter who told them the demand was random. Perhaps some residual authority of the prosecutor’s role survived the instruction. Englich and her colleagues put the randomisation in the participants’ own hands. Fifty-two junior lawyers, recently qualified and already sitting as judges, with an average age in the late twenties, worked through the same shoplifting materials. They were then asked to determine the prosecutor’s demand themselves by throwing a pair of dice. The dice were loaded. One pair always showed one and two, summing to three. The other always showed three and six, summing to nine. The participant computed the sum and entered it as the prosecutor’s demand, having been told that the demand was determined at random precisely so that it would not influence the later questions. It influenced them. The high anchor produced sentences averaging 7.81 months. The low anchor produced sentences averaging 5.28 months. Certainty, again, did not track the condition. A comparison with a study in which the identical demands came from a prosecutor found a similar shift. Knowing one had just thrown the dice did not distinguish the judges from judges who had been given a demand in the ordinary way.

The authors address the obvious complaint about the missing control group, the complaint that without a sentence passed in the absence of any anchor one cannot tell whether the high number raised sentences, the low number lowered them, or both. They explain why a no-anchor condition is less informative than it sounds. People bring their own anchors, and a judge given no number is not a judge free of numbers. Using pretest sentences as a rough baseline, they argue that both the high and the low anchors moved sentences, the high anchor more clearly. The claim the paper needs is the comparative one, and it is secure: the same file, the same kind of judge, a different roll of loaded dice, a different sentence. Absolute purity about a world with no anchors at all is not available, and they do not pretend it is.

What comes to mind

The fourth study asks whether the selective-accessibility mechanism can be seen at work in this material. Fifty-seven junior legal professionals threw the same kind of loaded dice and then, instead of passing sentence, classified short statements about the shoplifting case as incriminating or exculpatory, as fast as they could. The statements had been chosen, on the basis of a separate rating by lawyers, to include arguments that actually bear on this case, previous convictions and the violation of probation among the incriminating ones, diminished responsibility and a willingness to accept treatment among the exculpatory ones. The prediction from selective accessibility is that a high sentencing anchor makes the incriminating arguments easier to recognise as incriminating, and a low anchor makes the exculpatory arguments easier. The study measures that ease in response times. It finds the interaction the mechanism predicts. The paper’s interpretation is that the dice do not tug the sentence by magic. They change, for a few minutes, which parts of a complicated file are mentally nearest, and the sentence follows the nearest parts.

Across the studies, expertise did not confer protection. Where the authors could compare criminal-law specialists with legal professionals whose daily work was elsewhere, the specialists were not spared the shift, though they were sometimes more confident. Confidence and susceptibility were not the same thing. That dissociation is the professionally awkward result. A system of justice that relies on the felt certainty of experienced judges will not detect this bias from the inside, because the bias does not announce itself as doubt. It announces itself as a clearer and clearer view of the features of the case that happen to fit the number just encountered.

What the paper does not show

The limits are the limits of an experiment that is deliberately, and ethically, not a real sentencing. The cases are hypothetical, the consequences are professional embarrassment at most, and the junior lawyers of the dice study are not the same population as the senior judges of the second study, though both groups showed the effect. German sentencing practice, with its particular mix of prosecutorial demand and judicial discretion, is the context, and a jurisdiction that constrains sentences more tightly by guideline would give an anchor less room. The dice were loaded, which is the point, and a critic can say that a real courtroom contains no such prop. The critic then has to explain why a prop the judge knows to be random should be more powerful than a demand the judge believes to be informative, when the paper’s own comparisons suggest the random and the informative shifts are of a piece. The right generalisation is not that every sentence on earth is a dice roll. It is that a salient number, processed by a professional who is doing a careful job, moves the outcome in the number’s direction, and that telling the professional the number is meaningless has not been a sufficient cure.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on anchoring uses this paper as the severe real-world case: judges, realistic files, and anchors that are irrelevant by design, up to and including dice. The card’s claim that sentencing decisions are affected by the numbers put in front of decision-makers rests on this line of work. Tversky and Kahneman’s 1974 paper supplies the original wheel and the idea of insufficient adjustment. Furnham and Boo’s review supplies the map of domains and the conclusion that experts are not exempt. Many Labs supplies the evidence that a standard anchoring result replicates across laboratories. Englich, Mussweiler, and Strack supply the demonstration that the effect survives the strongest informal rebuttal, the rebuttal from professional seriousness. Read them when the card’s advice about first offers and preset numbers seems like a lesson for negotiators only. The same structure, a number considered and then insufficiently escaped, appears in a decision whose stakes are not a bargain. The dice are a provocation. The journalist’s question and the random prosecutorial demand are the findings closer to the way numbers actually walk into a courtroom.
