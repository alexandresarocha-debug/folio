Birte Englich, Thomas Mussweiler, and Fritz Strack asked legal professionals to sentence a fictional defendant, a woman called Lena M. who had been caught shoplifting for the twelfth time. In the third study, 52 junior lawyers, 28 of them men, ages 24 to 33, threw a pair of dice and were told to write the total down as the prosecutor's demand. The dice were loaded. Half the pairs always showed 1 and 2, so the demand was 3 months on probation. The other half always showed 3 and 6, so the demand was 9 months. The defense always asked for 1 month. The lawyers had been told the dice were there so the number would not influence them.

It did. The average sentence after a roll of 3 was 5.28 months. After a roll of 9 it was 7.81 months. Sentences in the whole group ran from 1 month to 12, with an average of 6.6. The lawyers felt moderately sure of the decision either way.

A second study used the same file with more experienced people and made the randomness even plainer in words. Thirty-nine legal professionals, 37 of them judges, with an average of 13.38 years in court, were told that the prosecutor's demand had been picked at random. Those who saw a demand of 9 months gave an average sentence of 6.05 months. Those who saw 3 months gave 4.00 months. A pretest with no anchor, using other legal professionals, had produced an average of 5.62 months. Experience did not close the gap. In the first two studies combined, 41 criminal-law specialists were pulled by the irrelevant number about as hard as 40 lawyers from other fields.

A live sentence is not a dice roll, but a number still arrives before the decision: a prosecutor's request, a story in the paper about "a term of X years," a colleague's guess in the hallway. In these studies the number did not have to be informed, or even believed, to matter. The lawyers who rolled the dice themselves, and who were told why the roll was irrelevant, still sentenced closer to the roll. The first figure became the sentence they then found reasons for.
