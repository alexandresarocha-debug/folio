Joseph A. Tainter's The Collapse of Complex Societies (Cambridge University Press, 1988) defines collapse as a rapid loss of an established level of social and political complexity. Societies add complexity to solve problems: more officials, more soldiers, more records, more roads. The first additions pay well. Later ones cost more and return less. When staying complex costs more than it returns, people stop supporting the center, and a simpler arrangement is what is left. He argues the model through three cases he can document in some detail: the western Roman Empire, the Classic Maya, and the Chacoan towns of the American Southwest.

On Rome, the phase that paid was conquest. New provinces brought land, slaves, and tribute that more than covered the army. Once the frontiers stopped moving, the army and the administration were a standing cost with no new windfall. The third-century emergency, and then Diocletian's larger bureaucracy and heavier taxes, were attempts to buy the same security with more machinery. Tainter's claim is that the machinery hit diminishing returns. Keeping the late Roman state cost peasants more than the state gave them back in protection, and the Germanic kingdoms that replaced it in the west were cheaper to live under. He tells a related story for the southern Maya lowlands, where the cities and their inscribed monuments stop across the eighth and ninth centuries: more investment in intensification, a falling return, and then a rapid simplification.

The theory travels well, and it is hard to count. No Roman budget survives that would show the return on the next official. What survives is a narrative of rising fiscal pressure and a political end, which can fit more than one cause. For the Maya, later measurement cut across a story told mainly in costs. Douglas J. Kennett and colleagues, in a 2012 paper in Science, used a stalagmite from a cave in Belize to date a series of droughts in the lowlands, including a severe drying in the ninth century, lined up with the political collapse. Drought does not erase diminishing returns. It does mean a cost curve was not the only thing that broke those cities. Tainter's demand on the evidence still stands. If you say a society collapsed, say what complexity it lost, how fast, and why people stopped paying for it.
