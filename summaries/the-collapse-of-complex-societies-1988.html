In 167 BC, on the figures Joseph Tainter assembles, Rome seized the treasury of the king of Macedonia and stopped taxing Romans themselves. In 130 BC the annexation of Pergamon doubled the state budget, from 100 million to 200 million sesterces. After Pompey took Syria in 63 BC, Tainter puts the budget at 340 million. Conquest was paying for the next conquest. He notes that Julius Caesar's gold from Gaul was abundant enough that gold fell 36 percent in value. The Collapse of Complex Societies (1988) is built on payoffs of that kind. Societies add officials, soldiers, and roads because the early additions solve problems and more than cover their cost. Later additions yield less. Eventually the society is spending more to maintain what it already has.

Rome is the case he develops for historians of the classical world. Augustus, emperor from 27 BC to AD 14, largely stopped expanding. The one-time plunder was spent. The army and the offices remained, and they had to be paid from each year's harvest. Tainter judges that later conquests, Claudius's Britain and Trajan's Dacia among them, probably never paid for themselves. He defines collapse as a rapid, significant loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity, on the order of a few decades, and he is explicit that this is a loss of structure, even where many people survive. In the West the last emperor was deposed in 476. Fewer specialists, weaker states, more local subsistence: that simplification is the collapse. For some of the people who had been paying the late Roman tax, withdrawing support could look like getting out of a bad bargain.

Chaco Canyon is his check that the logic is wider than Rome. In the San Juan Basin of New Mexico, Chacoan towns were tied together by roads, and roof timbers were carried as far as 50 kilometers across the desert to the canyon. Tainter calls them the most complex prehistoric society on the Colorado Plateau, and also the least complex of his three main cases, never quite a state. By 1300 the sedentary towns were abandoned or had been stripped for building stone, and the people who remained had shifted to a simpler, mobile life. Tainter claims a shared pattern. Complexity that had once paid became a cost the society stopped carrying.

The definition is clear enough to strain. A loss that takes longer than a few decades is, by his own rule, weakness rather than collapse, and the western Roman unraveling is hard to fit in that window. Constantinople remained a complex state for centuries after 476. Measuring the return on one more office is soft: the same late Roman bureaucracy can be described as a cost that still paid or a cost that did not. Patricia A. McAnany and Norman Yoffee, editing Questioning Collapse (2009), argued that the word hides continuity. People adapt, move, and keep skills that a clean drop would erase. Ask what the next layer of government costs, and what problem it still solves. The skyline will not answer that.
