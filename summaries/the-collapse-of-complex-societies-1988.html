A general theory, not another story of one ruin

Joseph Tainter's The Collapse of Complex Societies, published in 1988, is an anthropologist's attempt to say what collapse is and why it happens, in a form that can be used on more than one civilisation. Tainter had looked at the menu of explanations historians and archaeologists usually offer. Societies run out of resources. Invaders arrive. A class conflict tears the state apart. Rulers mismanage. A catastrophe strikes. A mysterious loss of nerve sets in. Each of these, he argues, is sometimes part of the story and none of them is a theory. They do not say why a society that has survived earlier droughts, earlier wars, and earlier bad rulers fails at a particular moment, and they do not say why the failure takes the form it does. His opening claim is that collapse is an economic event in the broad sense. It is what happens when a society that has been solving its problems by adding complexity meets diminishing, and then negative, returns on that complexity.

He defines collapse narrowly, which is a service. Collapse, for Tainter, is a rapid and substantial loss of an established level of social, political, and economic complexity. Population may fall. It does not have to be the definition. A state that breaks into simpler polities, a society that loses specialists, literacy, and large-scale exchange, has collapsed in his sense even if many people survive and even if later ages romanticise the result. The definition lets him compare cases that a historian of one empire would keep apart.

Complexity as a way of solving problems

The theoretical chapters treat complexity as an investment. Societies add bureaucracy, specialists, infrastructure, and hierarchical control because those things solve problems: how to store grain, how to defend a border, how to coordinate irrigation, how to keep the peace among strangers. The early investments tend to pay well. The first roads, the first professional officials, the first organised army, open up gains that a simpler society could not reach. Later investments run into a constraint that any economist would recognise. Each new layer costs something, and the next problem is often harder than the last. More complexity yields less additional benefit. Eventually the society is spending more to maintain the structure it already has, and the benefit of one more office, one more fort, or one more tax no longer justifies the cost.

At that point a shock that would once have been absorbed becomes dangerous, not because the shock is unique but because the margin has gone. Collapse can then be rational for the people who have been paying. If the centre no longer returns security or justice or access to goods in proportion to what it extracts, withdrawing support is an economising decision. Tainter is deliberately cool about this. He does not treat collapse as a moral failure or as a mystery of lost will. He treats it as a change in the costs and benefits of participation in a complex system. Peasants who abandon land, provincial elites who stop remitting tax, and soldiers who stop obeying a distant paymaster can all be instances of the same calculation. The centre experiences these choices as disaster. The choosers may experience them as relief from a bad bargain.

Rome, the Maya, and Chaco

The Roman case is the one he develops for historians of the classical world. The early empire, on his account, still enjoyed the returns on complexity built up during the conquests. Expansion had paid for itself by bringing in land, slaves, and treasure. Once the frontiers stabilised, the costs remained and the windfall stopped. The late empire answered its problems, Persian war, European frontiers, succession, urban supply, by adding soldiers, officials, and fiscal machinery. Those additions were rational responses and, past a point, poor investments. Taxation rose. The currency was debased. Farmers, who ultimately paid, were squeezed hard enough that some left the land or accepted the protection of powerful local patrons rather than of the state. In the west the system eventually simplified. Fewer specialists, weaker states, more local subsistence. Tainter reads that simplification as collapse in his defined sense, and as a result that could look like a benefit to people who had been carrying the late Roman load. The east, with a stronger fiscal base, kept a high level of complexity for longer, which is what his theory would expect if the returns had not yet turned negative in the same way.

The Classic Maya and the Chacoan society of the American Southwest are the comparative controls. He uses them to show that the logic is not a Roman special case smuggled in through classical sources. Maya polities invested in a competitive complexity of courts, monuments, and intensive agriculture, and then lost that complexity in a relatively short period, with population and political hierarchy both affected. Chaco Canyon organised a wide landscape through a ritual and economic centre and a network of roads and outlying communities, and then ceased to do so. Tainter does not claim that the three cases have the same trigger. He claims that in each, a strategy of solving problems by increasing complexity ran into costs that the society could no longer meet, and that the aftermath was a drop to a simpler organisation rather than the prompt replacement of one complex system by another of equal scale.

What he rules out, and the evidence he relies on

The long review of earlier theories is part of the argument, not a preface. Resource depletion, he thinks, explains little unless you can say why the society did not switch resources or lower its costs, which is the complexity question again. Invaders explain little unless you can say why the defence that had worked before failed, which is often a question about the cost of the army. Mystical explanations, a people growing tired of civilisation, explain nothing he is willing to count as a cause. His own evidence is comparative and archaeological as much as textual. For Rome he uses the fiscal and administrative record that ancient historians had already assembled, read through the economic lens. For the Maya and Chaco he uses settlement patterns, monuments, and the chronology of abandonment. The method is not a single new excavation. It is a redescription of known sequences in a common vocabulary of costs and returns.

The conclusion is a warning about the late stages of a complex system. A society near the point of diminishing returns can look very impressive. It has the most specialists, the most procedure, the most monuments. That impressiveness is compatible with fragility, because the cost of keeping the display going leaves little spare capacity. Collapse, when it comes, is then surprising to anyone who thought that accumulated institutions were the same thing as accumulated strength. Tainter's practical moral is diagnostic rather than prophetic. Ask what problems the added complexity is solving, and what the next increment costs. A system that adds cost without adding capability is further along his curve than its skyline suggests.

The limits he has been made to face

The theory is clear enough to be criticised, which is an advantage. Measuring complexity, and measuring its returns, is hard. Two historians can look at the late Roman bureaucracy and disagree about whether it was a costly adaptation that still paid or a parasitic growth that did not. If every surviving institution can be described as a rational cost and every fallen one as a failed investment, the theory becomes a vocabulary rather than a test. Critics also note that collapse, in the archaeological record, is often less total than the word suggests, and that recovery can be faster. People reorganise, move, and keep skills that a clean drop in complexity would erase. Environmental historians, Jared Diamond among them, argue that Tainter treats ecological damage as just one more stress, and thereby understates cases in which the resource base itself contracted. Tainter's reply is that environmental stress still has to pass through a social structure whose costs determine the response. The two approaches do not actually exclude each other. They choose different points on the chain as the place to do the explaining.

There is a political risk in the idea that collapse can be rational for the participants. Used carefully, it stops historians from assuming that everyone inside a falling state wished it would continue. Used carelessly, it can sound as if the people who suffered invasion, lost literacy, or died in the accompanying violence had made a shrewd consumer choice. Tainter's definition is about the costs of complexity, not about the justice of the outcome. A simpler society can be preferable to an extractive one and still be poorer, more violent, and less capable. The theory tells you to look at the marginal return. It does not tell you to applaud the fall.

Why it matters here

The card tainter-complexity is this theory in miniature: societies solve problems by adding complexity, the returns diminish, and collapse can be the economising response of people who stop paying for a centre that no longer delivers. Rome, the Classic Maya, and Chaco are the card's cases because they are Tainter's. The card pairs him with Jared Diamond's Collapse, which weights environmental damage and collective choice more heavily, and it notes the criticism that complexity and its returns are hard to measure and that real collapses are often partial. Folio uses Tainter when a later card is tempted to explain a fall by one shock, one villain, or one resource. The shock may be real. His question is why the society had no affordable answer left, and his Roman chapter is the version of that question which the third-century reforms and the western unravelling both have to face.
