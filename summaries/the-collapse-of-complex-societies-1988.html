Joseph Tainter's numbers for the Roman army, in The Collapse of Complex Societies (Cambridge University Press, 1988), are a rising bill. Under Augustus the army was 25 legions, and a legionary was paid 225 denarii a year. By the early third century he counts 33 legions, and pay that had climbed to 750 denarii. The cheap part of the empire, the seizure of other people's stored wealth, was over. What remained were garrisons, a civil service, roads, and the dole, paid for by taxing people who were already inside the frontier. Tainter's theory is that societies solve problems by adding complexity, and that each added layer eventually returns less than it costs. Collapse, in his definition, is the rapid drop to a simpler and cheaper way of living, chosen because it pays better than propping up the old structure.

He uses one comparison to show the change in margin. In the late third century BC, during the war with Hannibal, Rome took losses that were, relative to its size, enormous, and it recovered, because conquest still paid. In AD 378 the emperor Valens was killed at Adrianople with a smaller army, and the western empire never built the margin back. By the fifth century, Tainter argues, the western state was consuming its own capital, farmland and peasants, to pay for a level of army and office that no longer bought security. The Germanic kingdoms that replaced it faced invasions too. They met them with less permanent administration and at lower cost, which is why, in his terms, they were the rational outcome rather than a mystery.

The idea travels easily to any institution that keeps adding staff, rules, and overhead to solve the same problems. The test is whether the next layer of cost buys more safety or grain than it consumes. When it does not, people drift toward arrangements that do less and cost less. Tainter thinks that drift, in the west, is what the word "fall" is pointing at.

The eastern empire is the case his Roman chapter has to absorb. Constantinople kept a professional army, a tax bureau, and a complex court for centuries after the west had simplified, and an emperor was still there in 1453. Complexity by itself did not force a collapse wherever it was expensive. Tainter's answer is that returns differed: the west reached the point where simpler kingdoms outcompeted the old bill, and the east did not, or not yet. The pay scales and the legion counts are his illustration. They are not a measured curve of marginal returns, which ancient tax records are too thin to draw.
