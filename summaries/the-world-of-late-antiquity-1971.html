A different name for the same centuries

Peter Brown's The World of Late Antiquity, published in 1971, is a short book with a large ambition. It asks the reader to look at the years from about 150 to about 750 and to see a civilisation in the process of remaking itself, rather than a civilisation in the process of dying. The Mediterranean world of Marcus Aurelius, the Christian empire of Constantine and Augustine, the post-imperial kingdoms of the west, the eastern Roman state, and the first century of Islam are placed in one frame. Brown did not invent every piece of this picture. Historians of law, administration, and the church had already shown that the later Roman centuries were busy rather than empty. What he did was to give the period a name that stuck, late antiquity, and to make culture and religion the centre of the story instead of the fall of a political office.

The opening claim is therefore a claim about what counts. If the important fact is the removal of the last western emperor in 476, then the fifth century is an ending. If the important facts are the rise of new forms of religious life, the persistence of Roman law and Latin, the prosperity of eastern cities, and the creation of societies from Ireland to Mesopotamia that still organised themselves with Roman materials, then 476 is one political event inside a much longer transformation. Brown chooses the second emphasis. He does not pretend that armies stopped fighting or that kingdoms were gentle. He argues that a narrative organised only around decline misses the creativity of the age and misdescribes what ordinary elites, and increasingly ordinary believers, thought they were doing.

From civic paganism to a Christian sacred world

The first movement of the book stays inside the Roman empire and watches a religious and social change. The public life of an earlier Roman city had been built around councils, festivals, and a civic paganism in which local notables paid for buildings and games and were repaid in honour. Brown describes the erosion of that bargain. The imperial state taxed more heavily and intervened more often. The old compact between a cultivated elite and its city weakened. Into the space left by that weakening came new religious commitments, Jewish, Christian, and also new forms of pagan philosophy and devotion. The third century, which political histories treat as a crisis of emperors, appears here also as a crisis of meaning, in which people looked for patrons and explanations the city could no longer supply.

Christianity, in this account, is not Gibbon's solvent of civic virtue. It is the most successful of several attempts to build a community that reached past the classical gentleman. Brown is interested in how the church became a social institution: bishops as urban leaders, charity as a form of power, doctrine as something worth fighting over because it defined who belonged. He is equally interested in the change in the self. The literature of the period, above all Augustine, shows people examining conscience, sexuality, and the will with an intensity that classical public culture had not required. Late antiquity, for Brown, is a period in which inner life became a historical subject. That is one reason the book spends so much time on texts that an administrative historian might treat as marginal.

The holy man and the cult of the saints belong to this same shift. Brown had argued, in work of the same period, that the Syrian holy man became a new kind of rural patron, resolving disputes and standing between village and powerful outsider. The book carries that insight to a wider audience. Saints' bodies, relics, and festivals reorganised sacred geography. Protection that had been sought from civic gods and civic magnates was sought from dead holy people and from living ascetics. Whether or not every region produced holy men of the Syrian type, the general claim is that religious specialists acquired a social function once held by the civic elite. A world that looks, from the senate house, like a loss of culture, looks, from the shrine, like a redistribution of it.

East and west, and the political end of the western emperors

Brown does not tell the fifth century as one undifferentiated collapse. He distinguishes a western story from an eastern one. In the east, cities, Greek education, and an imperial court centred on Constantinople remained vigorous. The eastern empire had a deeper fiscal base and a capital that was extraordinarily hard to take. In the west, imperial government did give way to kingdoms ruled by Gothic, Vandal, Frankish, and other elites. Brown's argument is that this political change did not equal the end of Roman ways of living among the people who had the means to leave a record. Latin remained the language of law and worship. Roman landowning patterns and Roman titles persisted. Bishops sat in Roman cities and did a growing share of the work of government. The newcomers often wanted to be recognised inside a Roman order, not to erase it.

The year 476, on this reading, matters less to most inhabitants than older national histories suggested. An emperor in Italy had already become a weak figure. The real rulers were generals and kings, some of them Roman and some of them barbarian, and the distinction was not always the one that later ideology preferred. Brown asks the reader to measure change by continuity of institutions and by the texture of religious and literary life, not by the presence of a man calling himself Augustus in the west. That is a deliberate reversal of Gibbon's emphasis. Where Gibbon heard barbarism arriving, Brown hears barbarian rulers adapting Roman administration because it was the most effective tool available.

He also refuses to close the story at the conventional western date. The book runs on to the seventh and eighth centuries, through the wars between the eastern empire and Sasanian Persia and into the Arab conquests. The eastern provinces of Syria and Egypt pass under a new religious and political order. Even that rupture is described with an eye to what was inherited: administrative habits, urban life, and a late antique religious intensity that Islam both challenged and, in Brown's wide frame, succeeded. The Mediterranean of 750 is not the Mediterranean of 150. It is also not a blank that begins only when medieval Europe is ready. Late antiquity is the name for the passage between them.

What kind of evidence this book trusts

The method also has a bias. Texts and fine objects are produced by minorities. A history of the holy man tells you a great deal about the imagination of patronage and rather less about the tax load of a peasant or the availability of a decent cooking pot. Brown knows that the late Roman state was demanding. He does not centre the possibility that, in parts of the west, the end of imperial supply and imperial demand made ordinary material life dramatically poorer. That omission became the opening through which later critics, especially archaeologists, attacked the whole late-antique paradigm. They argued that a beautiful story about culture had talked people out of noticing a fall in standards of living. The attack is not a reason to discard Brown's evidence. It is a reason to see what his evidence is for. He is a better guide to religious and intellectual change than to the history of consumption.

What the book set in motion

The concept of late antiquity became a field, with journals, posts, and a shared chronology that historians of Europe, Byzantium, and early Islam could inhabit together. That was a scholarly achievement and a fashion, and like any fashion it overshot. Some work written under Brown's influence treated every loss as a cultural choice and every barbarian king as a respectful heir of Rome. Political violence, the end of mass-produced western ceramics, the thinning of literacy, and the sheer fear recorded in fifth-century texts could be smoothed into a language of transition. Brown's own later books, on the making of western Christendom and on wealth and the church, are richer and more guarded than a slogan about transformation. The 1971 book remains the place where the slogan acquired its classical statement.

Read carefully, the book does not say that nothing fell. It says that fall is the wrong organising word if it makes you ignore what was built. New law codes, new monastic rules, new ideas of the holy, and new connections between regions were not consolation prizes. They were the period's own projects. The useful discipline Brown imposes is to ask, before using the word decline, decline of what, for whom, and measured against which earlier height. Gibbon had an answer: the height was Antonine civic and military order, and the decline was everything that moved away from it. Brown's answer is that a different height had come into being, and that many of the people who lived in it would not have traded it for a return to the second century even if they could have.

Why it matters here

On the card gibbon-decline, this book is the transformation story set beside Gibbon's verdict. It is the alternative frame: religion as invention rather than as betrayal, 476 as a political episode rather than as the death of a world, and the Mediterranean as a place that stayed connected long after the western emperors had gone. On the card brown-late-antiquity, the book is the main text. That card's warning, that the language of transformation can hide violence and economic loss, is a warning about how Brown was used as well as about what he wrote. The later works listed beside it, including Brown's own Rise of Western Christendom and Guy Halsall's narrative of the migrations, and the rebuttal by Bryan Ward-Perkins, all assume the reader has grasped this 1971 argument. Folio's point is that the labels fall and transformation decide in advance which evidence is allowed to count. Brown is the historian who made transformation the respectable choice.
