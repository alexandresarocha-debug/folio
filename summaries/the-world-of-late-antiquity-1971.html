Peter Brown's The World of Late Antiquity, published by Thames and Hudson in 1971, begins with a Mediterranean that around AD 200 still functioned as one world and ends with three. In 476 the last western emperor was removed, and Roman government in the west gave way to new kingdoms. Brown does not stop there. In the seventh century Arab armies took Syria, Egypt, and North Africa from the eastern Roman state and overthrew the Persian empire. The last Sasanian king, Yazdegerd III, was killed in 651. By the early Middle Ages the old seacoast had divided into a Catholic west, a Greek Christian empire ruled from Constantinople, and an Islamic Near East. Brown's claim is that this was a long transformation, crowded with new religious institutions, and that 476 was one political break inside it.

The social change he puts in the middle of that story is the fading of the old civic notable. Classical cities were run by local councillors who paid for buildings, festivals, and the tax quota. War and taxation in the third century made the job crushing, and the towns lost much of their political weight. Bishops took over patronage, justice, and care of the poor. In Syria and Egypt, holy men living at the edge of settlement settled disputes for villagers who no longer had a reliable town patron. A life of self-denial had become a public role. Brown reads that as a new kind of authority, and as evidence that the period was inventing institutions, not only losing old ones.

The name of the period decides which of those facts leads. Call it a fall, and the end of the western emperors is the story. Call it late antiquity, Brown's term for roughly 150 to 750, and the bishop, the monastery, the law code, and Islam are the story. Bryan Ward-Perkins's The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization (2005) answers from the rubbish pits. In much of the west, mass-produced pottery, tiled roofs, and everyday coin disappeared, a loss that a history of holy men does not register. Both records exist. Brown's book is the one that made the religious record the one historians had to argue with.
