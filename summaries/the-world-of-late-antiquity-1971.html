For more than thirty years in the fifth century, Symeon, a holy man from the Syrian countryside, lived on top of a pillar beside the road that ran east from Antioch. He was born in the 390s and died in 459. Villagers walked out to the column with quarrels, debts, and requests for protection. Peter Brown used crowds of that kind, in The World of Late Antiquity (Thames and Hudson, 1971), to show how religious authority had moved. The book runs from the age of Marcus Aurelius, who died in 180, to the Arab conquests of the seventh century and the Mediterranean of about 750. Brown's claim is that these centuries were busy making a new kind of religious and social life.

The older public life of a Roman city had depended on local notables who paid for buildings, festivals, and games and were paid back in honor. Brown describes that bargain weakening as the imperial government taxed more heavily and intervened more often. Bishops, monks, and holy men took on work the civic magnate had done: settling disputes, feeding the poor, standing between a village and a powerful outsider. In the west, the last emperor in Italy was removed in 476. Brown treats that year as a change of regime inside a longer story. Latin, Roman landholding, and Christian bishops continued in the new kingdoms. In the east, Constantinople, Syrian cities, and Egyptian tax grain were still functioning. By the middle of the eighth century the map had three heirs: Latin kingdoms, a smaller eastern Roman state, and a caliphate that held Syria, Egypt, and North Africa.

The choice of where to look changes what a person thinks happened. A history that starts from the imperial title ends in the fifth century. A history that starts from Symeon's pillar, from relics, and from the bishop's court is a history of new institutions that people actually used. Both can be true of different parts of life. Brown is a guide to the second, the religious and literary record left by monks, bishops, and the people who traveled to them.

He is a weak guide to the cooking pot. Bryan Ward-Perkins, in The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization (2005), pointed out that wheel-made pottery disappeared from Britain in the early fifth century and was not made there again for almost three hundred years. A story organized around holy men does not predict that kind of loss, and it does not explain it. The texts show what was built. The sherds show what households stopped being able to buy.
