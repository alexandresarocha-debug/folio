Waiting as a judgement about the adult in the room

Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri and Richard Aslin published Rational snacking in 2013, with a subtitle that states the claim: young children's decision-making on the marshmallow task is moderated by beliefs about environmental reliability. The marshmallow task, in the form Mischel made famous, offers a child one treat now or two if the child can wait. The usual reading treats the wait as a revelation of self-control, a capacity the child brings into the room. Kidd and her colleagues treat the wait as a decision under uncertainty. The second treat arrives only if the adult who promised it comes back and pays out. A child who has reason to doubt the adult is facing a different gamble from a child who has reason to trust. Eating the first treat, in the doubtful case, can be the sensible act. The paper's title calls that act rational snacking.

The claim is a direct challenge to the parable that grew out of Shoda, Mischel and Peake's 1990 follow-up, in which longer preschool waits predicted higher adolescent competence ratings and higher SAT scores. If the length of the wait depends on what the child has just learned about the experimenter's reliability, then the wait is a belief about the situation as well as a trait of the child. Folio's card puts the point in one sentence. Waiting is also a rational response to whether adults are trustworthy. The 2013 study is the evidence for that sentence, and it is the reason the card refuses to let the marshmallow stand only for willpower.

The study is small in its theatre and large in its implication. It does not follow children into adolescence. It changes, in a single session, what a child has seen an adult do, and then it starts the waiting task. A difference at that point is a difference the trait story has trouble absorbing, because the children were not sorted by character. They were sorted by an experience the experimenters assigned.

An adult who does, or does not, keep a small promise

Before the snack, the children took part in an art project that was really a lesson about the experimenter. In one condition the experimenter was reliable. She made small promises about art supplies, better materials than the disappointing ones first on offer, and she returned with what she had promised. In the other condition she made the same promises and broke them, leaving the child with the inferior supplies and an apology that did not repair the fact. The promises were ordinary and concrete, the sort of promise a child can check. Then the experimenter introduced the delay task. The contingency was the familiar one. A modest treat now, or a better treat if the child could wait for her to come back.

The result the card reports is unambiguous in direction. Children who had met the unreliable experimenter waited much less than children who had met the reliable one. A few minutes of broken promises about crayons and stickers changed how long a child was willing to hold out for a second snack. The trait story predicts that two groups of children, unselected for self-control, will wait similarly once the snack task begins. The rational-snacking story predicts that the group with evidence against the adult will cash in sooner. The second story is the one the waits supported. Children were reading the room, and the room had been written, on purpose, in two different hands.

The design is a model of what a mechanism experiment can do for a famous correlation. Shoda, Mischel and Peake showed that waiting lined up with later outcomes in one cohort. They could not show why a given child waited. Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin do not show the later outcomes. They show a cause of the waiting itself, and the cause is outside the child's supposed reservoir of will. Reliability is assigned. Waiting moves. Anyone who wants to keep willpower in the explanation can still say that self-control matters once trust is held constant, that the reliable condition is the condition in which classic delay is visible. The paper allows that. What it does not allow is a reading in which the environment's trustworthiness is an irrelevant backdrop to a drama about character.

What the child is estimating

The decision can be described without romanticising children as little statisticians. A promised reward has a value and a chance of arriving. If the chance falls, the value of waiting falls with it. Eating now keeps a sure, smaller reward. Waiting keeps a chance of a larger one and a chance of nothing, plus the discomfort of the wait itself. A child who has just watched promises fail has a lower estimate of the chance. Shortening the wait is the response an adult would praise as prudent if the stakes were money and the promiser were a stranger with a bad record. The parable withholds that praise when the stakes are a sweet and the child is four, and calls the shorter wait a failure of control. Kidd and her colleagues return the praise. They ask the reader to see the same structure in the nursery that the reader already sees in any other unreliable market.

The art supplies are well chosen because they are diagnostic and small. A child does not need a theory of human nature to notice that the better crayons did not come. The evidence is recent, repeated, and about the very person who will shortly make another promise. That is the situation in which beliefs about reliability should be most visible, and it is the situation the study creates. A limit follows. The study shows that children can use fresh, obvious evidence about a particular adult. It does not show how children weigh a long history of mixed reliability at home, or how they behave with an adult they already love and cannot easily reclassify. Those are the harder cases, and they are the cases a longitudinal study of family background is partly about. The 2013 experiment is the clean demonstration that the capacity to notice is there.

There is a humane reversal inside the result. Programmes that scold children for failing to wait, or that treat a short wait as an early mark of a feckless character, may be scolding children for having learned their actual world. If the adults around a child are unpredictable, a preference for the sure reward is an adaptation. Training the child to wait anyway, without changing the reliability of the world, trains the child to ignore evidence. Mischel's strategy research, distraction and abstract thought, remains a useful account of how a wait is accomplished when the child has decided the wait is worth it. Rational snacking asks the prior question. Worth it according to whom, and on what record of kept promises?

How this sits with the larger revisit

Watts, Duncan and Quan's 2018 paper found that the long-run association between delay and later achievement was about half the size of the original follow-up, and that it shrank by roughly two thirds more when family background, early cognitive ability, and home environment were controlled. Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin supply a piece of psychology that makes the shrinkage intelligible. Background and home environment are where a child learns whether adults deliver. If that learning affects the delay score, then the delay score shares variance with background, and a control for background will take a bite out of the score's apparent power. The bite is what the 2018 analysis found. The 2013 experiment is a way of seeing the bite happen in miniature, with art supplies instead of years of household life.

The two papers together also protect the original strategy findings from being thrown out with the parable. Nothing in rational snacking says that attention is irrelevant once a child trusts the adult. A reliable experimenter and a child who stares at the sweet is a different pair from a reliable experimenter and a child who sings and looks away. Mischel's experiments varied the second half of that pair. Kidd's experiment varies the first half. A full description of a minute in the waiting room needs both. The parable described only a trait, and located it entirely inside the child. The revision locates a decision, and locates the evidence for the decision in the child's recent experience of adults.

Folio's card is named for the revision. Willpower or circumstance is a question the 2013 study refuses to answer with willpower alone. Circumstance, here, is specific. It is the reliability of the person who made the promise, demonstrated or betrayed before the promise about food was even offered. A reader who remembers only that unreliable adults make children eat sooner has remembered the result. A reader who also sees why that result shrinks a famous correlation has remembered the reason the result belongs on the card, next to a paper about nine hundred children and a paper about SAT scores.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The marshmallow test: willpower or circumstance?, uses this study as the evidence that waiting tracks trust. Children who first experienced an experimenter breaking promises about art supplies waited much less than children who experienced a reliable experimenter. The wait is a belief about the environment, measured in the same kind of task that Shoda, Mischel and Peake used as a predictor of later competence and SAT scores. Watts, Duncan and Quan then showed, in a larger and more diverse sample, how much that predictive story shrinks once family background and the home are taken into account. Read Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin for the experimental lever, reliability, that helps explain the shrinkage. The lever is ordinary and, once seen, hard to leave out of any honest account of a child alone with a promised treat.
