Celeste Kidd, Holly Palmeri, and Richard N. Aslin asked whether a child who eats the marshmallow is failing at self-control or making a reasonable bet about the adult who promised a second one. Twenty-eight children, ages 3 years 6 months to 5 years 10 months, average 4 years 6 months, came to a lab at the University of Rochester. Fourteen were assigned to each condition, nine boys and five girls, matched on age. Before any marshmallow, the researcher offered a choice. The child could use a sealed jar of worn crayons now, or wait 2.5 minutes for a new set of art supplies. Then the child could use one small sticker now, or wait 2.5 minutes for a set of better ones. Every child waited out both delays. In the reliable condition the researcher came back with the art supplies, and then with five to seven large stickers the parent had picked as desirable. In the unreliable condition she came back empty-handed both times, said she had made a mistake, and told the child to use the worn crayons and the small sticker.

The marshmallow choice came immediately after. One marshmallow now, or two if the child waited while the researcher went for more, up to 15 minutes. Children who had just watched two promises fail waited 3 minutes and 2 seconds on average, and 1 of the 14 lasted the full 15 minutes. Children who had just watched both promises kept waited 12 minutes and 2 seconds, and 9 of the 14 lasted the full time. The gap was large, about nine minutes. Age and gender did not account for it, and ratings of how cheerful or fidgety the children looked in the first 30 seconds did not differ by condition. The same children had been willing to wait for crayons and stickers. What changed was the adult’s record.

A parent, a teacher, or a caseworker who treats the marshmallow minute as a measure of character is missing this result. A child who has learned that promised extras do not arrive, at home, in a shelter, or in a classroom with a high rate of broken plans, is doing the arithmetic the unreliable group did. Training that child to “wait anyway,” without making the next promise true, asks the child to ignore evidence. The practical lever in this study was the experimenter’s last two trips out of the room, not a lecture on grit.

Kidd and her colleagues are explicit that self-control may still matter. What the 28 children show is that a few minutes of evidence about whether the adult comes back can move the wait from about 3 minutes to about 12, a swing at least as large as the difference usually credited to willpower.
