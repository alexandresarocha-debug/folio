The question the book opens with

Philip Zimbardo's The Lucifer Effect, published in 2007, asks how ordinary people come to do harmful things. The title takes the story of Lucifer as a figure who was not born a monster and became one. Zimbardo's claim is that the transformation is usually situational. We prefer dispositional stories, in which cruelty is the nature of a few bad individuals, because those stories leave the rest of us alone. He thinks the preference is a comfort and a mistake. The book is the long case for looking at the barrel rather than the apple, and then at the people who made the barrel. Folio's Stanford card is, in part, a reconsideration of that case. A fair walkthrough has to give the case as Zimbardo makes it, before the archive is allowed to answer.

The book has three movements. The first is a day-by-day account of the Stanford Prison Experiment, told by the man who built it and who stopped it. The second generalises from that study to a psychology of social roles, deindividuation, dehumanisation and moral disengagement, and applies the generalisation to the abuses at Abu Ghraib. The third is a turn towards resistance. If situations are powerful, the useful response is a practical account of how ordinary people refuse them, which Zimbardo calls the banality of heroism. The sections below follow that order.

The prison, as he tells it

Zimbardo narrates August 1971 as a descent he did not intend and could not, at the time, see clearly. Student volunteers were arrested by real police, booked, and delivered to a basement dressed as a jail. A coin flip, in his telling, sorted guards from prisoners. Prisoners were issued numbers, smocks and caps, and a chain at the ankle. Guards were issued uniforms and mirrored sunglasses, and told that they had to maintain order without physical violence. Across the days, he describes sleep deprivation, arbitrary punishments, sexualised humiliation and a coarsening of the guards' language and conduct. Prisoners became passive, depressed or frantic. One was released early in a state that looked like a breakdown. Others followed. Zimbardo presents his own delay in ending the study as evidence for his thesis. He had taken the role of superintendent, and the role captured the researcher. The study stopped after six days, of a planned two weeks, when Christina Maslach, a psychologist he was close to, visited, was appalled, and told him that what he was presiding over was terrible.

He is not, in these chapters, claiming that every guard was equally cruel. He describes differences, and he is interested in the men who became harsh. The theoretical use he makes of them is still general. Normal young men, screened for obvious pathology, became capable of abuse because the situation gave them a role, a uniform, anonymity, permission and a group. Personality did not predict who would be the cruel one in any way that would have let him pick the guards in advance. That unpredictability is the point he wants. If you could not have seen it in their characters, you have to see it in the place.

From persons to situations

The middle of the book is a psychology textbook written as an argument. Zimbardo gathers mechanisms that let a person do harm and still sleep. Deindividuation: uniforms, sunglasses, night, a group, the loss of a name, all reduce the feeling of being a personally accountable individual. Dehumanisation: numbers, bags, filth and a vocabulary that treats prisoners as things make harm easier, because the usual restraint is a response to a person. Obedience: he places his study next to Milgram's and treats both as evidence that authority and role can override private discomfort. Moral disengagement, drawing on Albert Bandura, covers the justifications that redescribe harm as duty, as something the victim deserved, or as something the actor is not really the author of. Conformity and the fear of being the one guard who breaks ranks do the rest. None of these mechanisms is original to the book. The original move is to stack them on the Stanford story and then carry the stack to a real prison scandal.

The dispositional habit is the enemy of this stack, and Zimbardo returns to it often. After a scandal, institutions and publics look for a few aberrant people, punish them, and declare the system sound. He thinks that ritual protects the system. A situational analysis is uncomfortable because it implicates arrangements, orders, timetables and the rest of us, who might have done the same in the same place. He asks the reader to accept the discomfort as the price of an explanation that can prevent a repeat. An explanation that stops at a villain cannot say what to change.

Abu Ghraib and the system

The Abu Ghraib chapters are the book's public purpose. Photographs from the night shift at the Baghdad prison had, by the time Zimbardo was writing, been treated as pictures of a few sadists. He had served as an expert witness for Staff Sergeant Ivan Frederick, one of the reservists convicted over the abuses, and the book is shaped by that brief. Zimbardo argues that Frederick and his colleagues were ordinary soldiers set down in a chaotic, overcrowded, badly run prison, with vague and improper pressure to soften detainees up for interrogation, poor training, long shifts, anonymity and a climate in which the usual rules had been suspended. The apples, he says, were not the distinctive cause. The barrel was. He then adds a third term. Someone makes barrels. Policies decided far from the tier, legal opinions that narrowed the meaning of torture, and a chain of command that wanted intelligence and did not want to know how it was got, are the system. A complete account has to include persons, the situation on the tier, and the system that built the situation.

This is the strongest and the most contested part of the argument, and the book does not pretend to be a neutral history of the occupation. It is a brief for a kind of explanation. The photographs are real. The convictions were real. Zimbardo's claim is that punishing a handful of night-shift soldiers, while leaving the policies unexamined, repeats the dispositional error he has spent the book attacking. A reader can accept the structure of that claim, the three levels, and still argue about how much weight each level bears in this particular scandal. The book is at its most useful when it keeps the levels distinct, and at its most vulnerable when the Stanford basement is asked to carry evidentiary weight for a war.

Heroism as the way out

The final movement refuses to leave the reader inside the barrel. Zimbardo's view is that if situations pull people towards harm, they can also be built, and resisted, so that they pull towards decency. He is sceptical of the idea that heroes are a rare breed with a special character. He prefers what he calls the banality of heroism: ordinary people who, at a particular moment, notice what is happening, take responsibility, and act. Maslach's refusal to treat the basement as acceptable is his own example. He offers a practical guide for resisting unwanted influence. Question the frame you have been given. Do not assume that a role suspends your judgement. Look for an ally, because dissent is far easier when it is not solitary. Be willing to bear a social cost early, before the escalations have made retreat feel impossible. Practise the small disobedience that keeps a conscience usable, so that the large one is not a brand new act. And design institutions that expect whistleblowing and that do not concentrate unwatched power in exhausted people on a night shift.

There is a tension here that the book acknowledges and does not fully dissolve. The situational thesis says that character is a weak predictor and a weak protection. The heroism chapters ask ordinary people to become, in the moment, the sort of person the thesis says not to count on. Zimbardo's way through is to treat heroism as a practice and a situation, a set of preparations and allies, rather than as a trait you either have or lack. Whether that is enough is left as a demand on the reader. The book ends as a campaign for a certain kind of attention, not as a proof that attention will be forthcoming when the sunglasses are on.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on the Stanford Prison Experiment gives this book as the fullest statement of the lesson that was taught for decades, and as the bridge from the basement to Abu Ghraib. Reading it part by part shows what the lesson was. Roles and situations can pull ordinary people into harm. Dispositional outrage at a few villains protects the arrangements that did the pulling. Resistance is possible and is itself ordinary. Those claims are the book's real argument, and they are broader than the six days. They can be discussed, and in several cases supported, with evidence that does not depend on Stanford.

The card's caution is that the six days are not the evidence they were said to be. Le Texier's archival work, which a reader of this book should take as the paired text, argues that the guards were guided, that toughness was encouraged, and that some participants later said they had been acting. Haslam and Reicher's BBC study, the other paired text, assigned roles without that coaching and did not watch guards become a tyrannical unit. Their alternative, engaged followership, says people do harm when they identify with a leader and a cause. Zimbardo's own narrative contains more of that leadership, the superintendent's authority, the briefing, the group, than the slogan "the situation did it" keeps in view. The Lucifer Effect remains the place to meet the situationist case at full strength. The card's view is that the case has to stand, if it stands, without the Stanford legend doing the proving.
