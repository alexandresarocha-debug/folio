In The Lucifer Effect, published by Random House in 2007, Philip Zimbardo tells the Stanford Prison Experiment as a warning about situations. In August 1971 he and colleagues Craig Haney and W. Curtis Banks turned the basement of a Stanford psychology building into a mock prison. From more than seventy men who answered a newspaper ad, they kept twenty-four college students who looked healthy on interviews and tests, and paid them fifteen dollars a day. A coin flip made half guards and half prisoners. The prisoners were picked up by Palo Alto police, booked, and brought in blindfolded. The study was planned for up to two weeks. Zimbardo was both the researcher and the prison superintendent. On the sixth day he stopped it. He credits Christina Maslach, who had come to look at the prison and told him the treatment of the young men was terrible. In his account, ordinary students had become cruel or broken because the place, the uniforms, and the roles carried them there.

The second half of the book sets that basement next to Abu Ghraib. After photographs of abuse by American military police became public in 2004, Zimbardo testified as an expert witness for Staff Sergeant Ivan Frederick, one of the soldiers court-martialed for what happened on the night shift. His argument was the same one he drew from Stanford. The abuse was what a bad system does to ordinary people: vague orders, a hostile place, permission from above, and a label that divides the person with the keys from the person in the cell. The “bad apple” story, he wrote, hides the barrel.

The lesson people take into work, policing, and hospitals is that roles and rules can move behavior fast, and that the person designing the shift, the uniform, and the chain of command owns part of what follows. That lesson survives only if the Stanford story is told with the record attached. In 2019 Thibault Le Texier, working from the Stanford archives, the orientation tape of August 14, 1971, and interviews with fifteen participants, showed that the guards were told the study was meant to produce fear, frustration, and powerlessness, and were coached on how to run humiliating counts. Eleven of the seventeen prison rules were copied from an earlier class project. The book’s account of a role that ran away with people leaves out a briefing that aimed them there.
