A soldier at the end of the crisis

Stephen Williams's Diocletian and the Roman Recovery, published in 1985, is a political narrative of the reign that closed the third-century crisis. Diocletian took the throne in 284. He was a Dalmatian soldier of humble origin, not a senator and not the heir of a dynasty. That origin is part of Williams's point. The empire that emerged from fifty years of short, violent reigns was saved, in so far as it was saved, by a professional military man who treated the state as a problem of organisation. The book follows him from the seizure of power to the abdication in 305, and it asks what the recovery consisted of, what it cost, and why the system he designed began to fail as soon as he left it.

Williams writes as a biographer of a government as much as of a man. Diocletian's personality is only partly knowable. The sources are official, hostile, or late. What can be known is the sequence of measures and wars, and the logic that connects them. A huge empire had been pulled apart by simultaneous threats and by the habit of civil war. Any recovery had to be a recovery of the frontiers and a recovery of the succession at the same time. One without the other would not hold. That double problem is the spine of the book.

Frontiers, Persia, and Britain

The early reign is a story of campaigns. The Rhine and the Danube had to be made into frontiers again rather than open roads for raiding. In the east, the Sasanian monarchy was the rival that had humiliated Rome in the middle of the century, and Diocletian's government fought a major Persian war that ended in a settlement favourable to Rome and in a fortified, organised eastern frontier. Williams presents this not as a quest for glory but as the restoration of a defensible map. A state that is still losing provinces cannot reform its offices in peace. The wars come first because the recovery is, before anything else, military.

Britain is the clearest western example of the same logic. The island had fallen under the breakaway regime of Carausius and then Allectus, a naval and political secession that the central government could not talk away. The recovery of Britain by Constantius, Diocletian's colleague, is told as a necessary piece of reunification. Williams's Diocletian is an emperor who understands that prestige and logistics are the same problem. A province outside the tax system and the appointment system is a standing invitation to the next usurper. Bringing it back is administration conducted by army and fleet.

The Tetrarchy and the new administration

The constitutional invention, and the centre of the book, is the Tetrarchy. Diocletian shared the empire with a fellow Augustus, Maximian, and then added two junior emperors, the Caesars Constantius and Galerius. Two seniors and two juniors, with a plan of succession by resignation and promotion, were meant to end the cycle in which every general thought the death of an emperor was his opportunity. The college also put a ruler within reach of each major frontier. Williams explains the arrangement as a practical answer to distance. One man could not be on the Persian front and the Rhine in the same season. Four men could, if they accepted a hierarchy. The ideology that dressed the college, with Diocletian and Maximian linked to Jupiter and Hercules and with a more sacred, ceremonial court, was the public face of that hierarchy. Later writers called it oriental. Williams treats it as a technology of obedience.

Under the ceremony sat a reorganization of the state. Provinces were cut into smaller units, grouped in dioceses, and governed by men whose civil careers were separated from military command. The army was enlarged. Taxation was rebuilt so that the larger army and the larger bureaucracy could be paid in kind and in money. Williams's account of this is the account of a state becoming more present in its subjects' lives. More officials meant more capacity and more cost. The recovery was real because the frontiers quietened and the civil wars, for a time, stopped. It was a heavier recovery than the principate of the second century would have recognised.

Prices, persecution, and abdication

Two policies near the end of the reign show the limit of what decree could do. The currency was reformed in an attempt to restore confidence after decades of debasement. The Edict on Maximum Prices, issued in 301, tried to stop inflation by naming maximum prices and wages for an enormous list of goods and kinds of labour, with severe penalties. Williams, in line with the usual judgement on the edict, treats it as a failure. Prices could not be talked down by inscription while the underlying money and supply remained strained. Fragments of the edict survive across the eastern provinces, which is evidence of how widely the government could publish an order, and also of how widely an order could be ignored. The episode is useful in the book because it marks the difference between Diocletian's administrative reach and his economic understanding. He could multiply officials. He could not command the market.

The Great Persecution of the Christians, launched late in the reign, is the other failed act of unity. Williams sets it inside the drive for a restored, traditional Roman order, not as an accident of cruelty. Public cult was, for Diocletian, part of the discipline that held the empire together. Christians who refused it looked like a tear in that discipline. The persecution was uneven, harsher in the east, and unsuccessful. It created martyrs, damaged the government's reputation, and did not produce the religious uniformity it sought. Constantine's later alliance with the church would reverse the policy entirely. In Williams's narrative the persecution is a clue to the reign's cast of mind. Unity was the aim, and force was an acceptable tool, and some kinds of unity cannot be forced.

The abdication in 305 is unique in Roman imperial history. Diocletian and Maximian resigned, the Caesars were promoted, and new Caesars were named. Williams presents this as the logical last step of the system and as its severe test. A college designed to make persons replaceable was now asked to function without the person who had held it together. It did not. Ambition, family interest, and the army's preferences tore the arrangements within a few years, and a new round of civil wars produced, eventually, Constantine. The recovery of the frontiers and the offices proved more durable than the succession scheme. Diocletian died in retirement while his design for a peaceful transfer of power was already wreckage.

Why it matters here

The Folio card third-century-crisis ends with the reboot: Aurelian reunites the empire, Diocletian rebuilds it, Constantine continues the overhaul. Williams is the close study of that reboot, and of the man whose name the card gives to the whole repair. The multiplication of provinces, the split between civil and military command, the larger army, the Tetrarchy of two senior and two junior emperors, and the attempt to fix prices by decree are not a list the card invented. They are the programme this book narrates, in the order a government under pressure actually moved: frontiers first, then the college, then the fiscal and administrative machine, then the unsuccessful attempts to command prices and beliefs.

The card's lesson is that a state can survive a near-disintegration by reinventing itself, and that the reforms which save it can make it more rigid and more expensive. Williams supplies the narrative detail that makes the lesson specific rather than proverbial. The Tetrarchy worked while Diocletian's authority held the college. The edict on prices showed a government confusing the power to publish a law with the power to set a market. The persecution showed the same government confusing unity with uniformity. The abdication showed a solved succession on paper and an unsolved succession in the camps. The late empire that came out of his reign was stronger than the empire of 260, and it carried the costs the card describes. Reading Williams is how those costs acquire a reign, a set of colleagues, and a date of resignation, instead of remaining a diagram of institutions.
