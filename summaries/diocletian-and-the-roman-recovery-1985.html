Late in AD 301 the emperor Diocletian posted a law that tried to stop inflation by naming the highest legal price for more than 1,200 goods, wages, and services. A measure of wheat, the army modius, was capped at 100 denarii. Barley was capped at 60. The preamble set a capital penalty for anyone who charged more, for a buyer who colluded with the seller, and for anyone who held food back in hope of a better market. Copies cut in stone survive from the eastern provinces. Stephen Williams reprints the edict and its setting in Diocletian and the Roman Recovery (Batsford, 1985), a life of the soldier who had been proclaimed emperor on 20 November 284 and who spent twenty years pulling a broken state back together.

The price list failed in the way price lists with death penalties often fail. Lactantius, a Christian writer who hated Diocletian, says goods vanished from the market, blood was shed over cheap items, and the law was abandoned because it had to be. The recovery that lasted was administrative. On 1 March 293 Diocletian completed a four-man college, the Tetrarchy: two senior emperors, himself and Maximian, and two junior emperors, Constantius and Galerius, each with a region to defend. On 23 February 303, at Nicomedia, he and Galerius opened the Great Persecution. Soldiers demolished the church that stood in sight of the palace, and the first edict ordered scriptures burned and stripped Christians of legal standing. On 1 May 305 Diocletian and Maximian both resigned, he at Nicomedia and Maximian at Milan. Diocletian retired to the palace he had built at Split, on the Dalmatian coast. Later writers say that when he was urged to take the throne again, he answered that if the messenger could see the vegetables he had planted with his own hands at Salona, the request would not be made.

The useful split is between the edict and the college. A ceiling on every wage and every loaf, enforced by execution, did not hold prices down, and the inscriptions that survive are eastern, so it is unclear how far the list was even posted in the Latin west. Four emperors, a new tax assessment, and provinces cut down to a size a governor could watch did hold the frontier long enough for the empire to outlive the third-century collapse. Anyone looking at a modern price freeze can see the 301 edict clearly. The part that changed the Roman state is the duller one: who commanded which army, and who was allowed to collect the tax.
