In 301 the emperor Diocletian tried to stop inflation with an inscription. His Edict on Maximum Prices set a legal ceiling for about 1,400 goods and services, grain and wine, freight rates, the wages of teachers, and ordered the list posted where people could read it. Pieces survive from more than forty sites, all in the eastern provinces, some in Latin and some with the prices in Greek. The preamble blames the greed of merchants. The penalty for selling above the ceiling was death. Lactantius, a hostile witness who lived through it, says goods disappeared from the market, blood was shed over cheap articles, and the government had to abandon the law. By the time Diocletian left office in 305 the edict was a dead letter. Stephen Williams's Diocletian and the Roman Recovery (1985) uses that failure as the boundary of an emperor who otherwise did put a broken state back together.

The recovery is the setting. Diocletian was proclaimed emperor on 20 November 284, after fifty years in which most rulers died by violence. In 293 he completed a college of four: two senior emperors, himself and Maximian, and two juniors, Galerius and Constantius. He multiplied the provinces, separated civil posts from military commands, and rebuilt the tax assessment so a larger army could be paid. On 1 May 305 he did what no emperor had done. He abdicated, at Nicomedia, and Maximian abdicated the same day at Milan. The juniors were supposed to step up without a war. Constantine and Maxentius, the adult sons of reigning emperors, were passed over. By 28 October 312 Constantine had defeated Maxentius at the Milvian Bridge, and the four-emperor plan was finished.

Williams's subject is the reboot that made a late Roman state possible: a government that could again hold the frontiers and collect the tax. The price list is the reminder of where command stopped. A ceiling cut into stone did not make grain cheaper, because sellers withdrew the grain. The abdication is the reminder that a succession plan is only as strong as the people it leaves out. The reformed empire was more heavily staffed, and more heavily taxed, than the empire of the second century. It held. The edict did not.
