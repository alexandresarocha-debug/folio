Background

After the Open Science Collaboration reported in 2015 that only about a third of replications in psychology were statistically significant, a natural question was whether the trouble was local. The studies in that project had come from three psychology journals, many of them small, and the word crisis was already being treated as a verdict on a whole style of work. Colin Camerer and a large team asked a harder, narrower question. Take experimental papers that had cleared the highest gates in science, papers published in Nature and Science between 2010 and 2015, and try to repeat them with more power, a public plan and laboratories that did not own the original result. If those papers also shrank or vanished, the problem was not confined to an obscure corner of the journals.

The project is usually called the Social Sciences Replication Project. It was published in Nature Human Behaviour in 2018. It is the study Folio's replication card cites when it says that experiments from Nature and Science succeeded more often than the 2015 psychology set, and that researchers, betting in prediction markets, could forecast which results would hold. The rate is more encouraging than a third. It is not a return to the old assumption that a famous paper can be trusted because it is famous.

The question

The question was replicability in a strict sense. Would the same experimental contrast, analysed as specified in advance, produce a statistically significant effect in the same direction as the original paper? Alongside that yes-or-no, the team wanted the size of the effect. A result can be significant and still much smaller than the published original, and a literature that systematically overstates sizes will mislead even when it is not pure noise. They also wanted to know whether other scientists could see fragility coming. If peers, given the papers and allowed to bet, can sort the replicable from the brittle, then the community already possesses information that the publication decision did not capture.

They were not asking whether the theories named in the original papers were true in general. A failed replication can come from a hidden difference in procedure, a sample that does not match, or a true effect that is simply smaller than the original study was built to detect. A successful replication can confirm a narrow contrast and leave the grand theory untouched. The project is evidence about published experiments, read one finding at a time.

How the studies were set up

The team began with experimental studies in the social sciences that Nature and Science had published in that six-year window, and they applied explicit inclusion rules so that the set would not be a gallery of pet targets. The studies that entered were ones a new laboratory could actually repeat: a treatment, a comparison, an outcome that could be measured again. For each chosen study the replication was designed to have more statistical power than the original, which meant larger samples than the papers that had made the claim. Analyses were specified before the results were known. The work was spread across laboratories rather than handed to a single sceptic. That structure matters. A one-lab failure is easy to attribute to clumsiness. A pre-specified, high-powered repetition is harder to wave away, and it is also a fairer test of the original, because an underpowered repeat can miss a real effect and then be used, unfairly, as a refutation.

In parallel, the project asked researchers to forecast. Surveys collected judgements about which studies would replicate. Prediction markets let participants buy and sell contracts that paid off if a replication succeeded. A market price, read at the right moment, is a crowd's probability. The comparison of interest was whether those prices, and the survey judgements, lined up with what the laboratories later found. Forecasting is not a decoration. If markets can tell robust from fragile before the data are collected, then replicability is partly visible in the papers themselves: in the size of the original evidence, the plausibility of the effect, the room for flexibility. The crisis, on that showing, is not only a matter of waiting years for a repeat. Some of the information is already in front of the readers.

What they found

A majority of the replications produced a significant effect in the original direction. Folio's card records the success rate as about 62 per cent. That is well above the roughly one-in-three rate from the Open Science Collaboration's psychology project, and it is the right figure to remember if someone claims that nothing in the flagship journals survived. It is also far below the impression a table of contents gives, because the original papers had presented their results as established. Readers who treated Nature and Science as a guarantee of repeatability were overconfident, just less overconfident than readers of a typical psychology issue from the same era.

Effect sizes in the replications were smaller than the effects the original papers had reported. Significance, where it returned, often returned in a diminished form. That pattern has become one of the signatures of these projects. Selective publication and flexible analysis inflate the first published estimate even when something real is present, so the repeat, if it is honest and adequately powered, tends to land lower. Complementary ways of scoring a replication, not only the single significant-or-not cut, told a similar story: a solid fraction of the set held, a substantial fraction did not, and the successes were quieter than the announcements. The forecasts had real information in them. Prediction markets sorted studies better than a coin toss would, which is the result the card summarises by saying that researchers could forecast which experiments would replicate. Surveyed beliefs were informative too, and the markets were a disciplined way of pooling them.

How the authors read the results

Camerer and colleagues read the project as a correction of confidence, not as a collapse. Experimental social science, even at the most selective journals, had been publishing a mix of durable and brittle findings, and the mix was not obvious from the prestige of the outlet. The practical implication they emphasised is institutional. High-powered, pre-registered replications can be done. Forecasts can tell editors and readers where to spend that expensive effort first. A literature that wants to be believed has to make room for the repeats and for the smaller estimates they tend to produce. Treating the original paper as the last word was the habit that had failed.

They also read the result against the incentive story told by Ioannidis and by Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn. Small samples, surprising effects and a premium on novelty make a first paper look stronger than a careful repeat will. Nature and Science select for interest. Interest is not the enemy of truth, but it is a biased sample of truth: the studies that reach those pages are the ones with a striking result to tell. The replication project is what happens when striking results are asked to appear again under less forgiving rules. Some do. The ones that do are the ones worth building on. The forecasting result adds a further point. The profession is not entirely in the dark. Collective judgement, when it is elicited as a bet rather than as a polite review, already discounts some findings that journals have certified.

Limits

The set is small by the standards of a whole discipline, and it is a particular slice: experiments, social science, two journals, six years. A rate from that slice should not be pasted onto economics field journals, or onto developmental psychology, or onto neuroimaging. Within the set, each replication is one attempt. A single failure does not prove the original finding was a false positive. Populations differ, experimenters differ, and a treatment that works through a cultural reference can age badly. A single success does not prove the original interpretation. It shows that the contrast can be obtained again. Mechanisms, boundary conditions and practical importance are further questions, and this project was not designed to settle them for every paper in the list.

Forecasting has its own limit. A market can be right that a study will fail without anyone being able to say, from the price alone, whether the cause is a tiny true effect, a procedural quirk, or a result that was never more than flexibility. Prediction is not diagnosis. There is also a selection issue in the other direction. Studies that could not be repeated under a common protocol never entered the denominator. A literature's hardest-to-repeat methods are invisible in a replication rate. The encouraging reading and the harsh reading both need that caveat. Sixty-two per cent is about the studies this design could reach.

Why it matters here

On Folio's replication card, this paper is the comparison case. The Open Science Collaboration's one-in-three is the figure that named the crisis. Camerer and colleagues show that the rate is not a constant of nature. Prestigious experimental papers from Nature and Science replicated more often, effects were still smaller the second time, and other researchers could tell, better than chance, which was which. That is the deep-dive claim of the card, and it is easy to misremember as either "the crisis was a panic" or "even the top journals failed". The walkthrough supports neither slogan. It supports a graded view. Trust is something a finding earns by surviving a pre-specified repeat, not something a masthead confers.

The card also says that classic effects such as anchoring and framing have replicated in many-lab work, while many social-priming effects have not. This project is part of that sorting. It gives a reader permission to keep a result that repeats, to shrink a result that returns smaller, and to hold lightly a result that does not return, however celebrated the journal. Paired with the false-positive paper, it answers a different question. Simmons, Nelson and Simonsohn showed how a significant result can be made. Camerer and colleagues measured how often a significant result, already published at the top, can be made again.
