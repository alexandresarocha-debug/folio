Colin Camerer and a large team repeated 21 experimental social-science papers that Nature and Science had published between 2010 and 2015. For each paper they took the first study that reported a significant treatment effect, and within that study the result that carried the paper's main claim. The original authors could review the analysis plan. The plan was registered before the new data were collected. The replication samples were, on average, about five times as large as the originals, so an effect of the published size should have been easy to see.

Thirteen of the 21 repeats, 62 percent, found a significant effect in the same direction as the original. Other ways of counting a success landed between 12 and 14 papers, 57 to 67 percent. The work was done in two stages. After the first stage, 12 of 21 were significant in the original direction. Raising the sample further brought the count to 13, with one study that had succeeded in stage 1 dropping out once its extra data were included. The effects shrank. As correlations, the originals averaged 0.460 and the replications averaged 0.249, about half, a difference that was itself significant. A Bayesian estimate put the share of true positives near 67 percent, and the size of those true effects at about 71 percent of what the journals had printed. The misses were false alarms and inflated true effects, both.

Other researchers, asked before the results were known which findings would come back, lined up with the outcomes. The failures were not a coin flip the community had no way to anticipate. Publication in Nature or Science lifted the hit rate above the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 figure for a broader set of psychology papers, where 36 percent of repeats were significant. It left about a third of these 21 without a significant effect, and it cut the average effect roughly in half. For a decision that rests on one high-profile experiment, prefer a result that has been run again, in a larger sample, with the analysis fixed first. If it has not been, treat the published number as one that may shrink by half.
