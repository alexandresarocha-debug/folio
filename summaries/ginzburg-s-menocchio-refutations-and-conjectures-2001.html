Twenty-five years after The Cheese and the Worms appeared, two historians went back to Menocchio's story and asked what Ginzburg had left out. David Levine and Zubedeh Vahed published their review essay in 2001 in the journal Histoire sociale / Social History. They begin with the facts of the case, and these are worth having straight. Menocchio was born in 1532 in the foothills of the Alps about 100 kilometres north of Venice. He was banished for two years for a brawl, one year in Arba and one in Carnia. He was arrested in October 1583, abjured in May 1584 and spent 20 more months in the bishop's prison at Concordia, where his jailer said his behaviour was exemplary. In 1586 he asked for release, saying his continued imprisonment was a hardship for his wife and seven surviving children.

The authors then give the end of the story. After 12 years back in the village he was tried again as a relapsed heretic. They write that the 67-year-old miller was burned at the stake by the Venetian secular authorities in 1599, with Pope Clement VIII himself pressing the case. They admit there is no direct evidence of the execution, only a notarial act of 16 August 1599 naming his son Stefano as the son of the deceased Domenico Scandella.

Their criticism is aimed at how Ginzburg built his argument. They say that Ginzburg's wish to support his own view of peasant culture led him to give a partly constructed picture of Menocchio's thought, and that he did not follow some obvious leads, even though by his own admission his findings were often inconclusive or weak. They point to one passage in the book, where Ginzburg writes that the idea of a remote oral tradition behind Menocchio's cosmos is unproven and perhaps destined to stay so. In their view the extra evidence Ginzburg promised had not appeared by 2001. They also quote a reviewer of his later book on witches' sabbaths saying it said little about the people who might have passed such stories on, such as traders or wet nurses.

Their own aim is modest: to follow some of the missing links hinted at in Menocchio's testimony and in Ginzburg's account, and to answer some of the unanswered questions about where his ideas came from.

What this means in real life: a vivid single case is persuasive, which is exactly why it needs checking. When a story about one person is used to explain a whole group, ask what else could explain it and what evidence is still missing. Levine and Vahed do not say Ginzburg was wrong about Menocchio. In their view the case is less settled than the book makes it look.

This is a published review essay by two historians, so it is one reading among several. Ginzburg's answer and later work on microhistory should be read alongside it.

Source. Levine, D., and Vahed, Z. (2001). Ginzburg's Menocchio: Refutations and Conjectures. Histoire sociale / Social History, 34(68), 437-464.
