This judgment gives the Court of Appeal's full reasons for quashing Sally Clark's convictions. It opens with the history. On 9 November 1999 she was convicted at Chester Crown Court, by a majority of 10 to 2, of murdering her baby sons, Christopher and Harry. Her first appeal was dismissed on 2 October 2000. Records were later found of microbiological tests on samples of Harry's blood, tissue and cerebrospinal fluid taken at his post mortem. They had played no part at trial, because all the lawyers on both sides were unaware of them.

On 2 July 2002 the Criminal Cases Review Commission referred the case back. The court heard the appeal on 28 and 29 January 2003, found the convictions unsafe, and Mrs Clark was released after the Crown chose not to seek a retrial.

The new evidence was that the pathologist had submitted samples on 27 January 1998, the day of the post mortem, and that Staphylococcus aureus had been grown from several sites, including the stomach, lungs, throat and cerebrospinal fluid. Expert evidence now suggested Harry may have died of natural causes. The court noted that Christopher's death, standing alone, would not have justified a finding of murder.

The judgment also deals with the statistics. Meadow had agreed at trial that 1 in 8,543 reflected the risk of a single cot death in a family like the Clarks, and said you have to multiply 1 in 8,543 by 1 in 8,543, giving about 1 in 73 million. Professor Berry, an editor of the study the figure came from, told the jury that squaring it was an illegitimate oversimplification. The trial judge warned the jury that we do not convict people on statistics. The court said it thought it very likely that the figure grossly overstated the chance of two natural sudden deaths in one family, and that had its admissibility been challenged, the wisest course would have been to exclude it.

What this means in real life: a dramatic number can carry great weight with a jury, which is why the court said this one should probably have been kept out.

Caution: the appeal was allowed mainly on the undisclosed medical evidence, not on the statistics alone.
