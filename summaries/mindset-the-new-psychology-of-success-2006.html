A book that turns a research programme into a vocabulary

Mindset: The New Psychology of Success, published by Carol Dweck in 2006, is not a report of one experiment. It is the public statement of a programme that had been running for years in journals, written so that parents, teachers, managers and athletes could use it. The academic language of that programme spoke of implicit theories. Some people hold an entity theory of ability: intelligence, or talent in some domain, is a fixed trait that you either have or lack. Others hold an incremental theory: ability is developable through effort, strategy and help. Mindset is Dweck's name for the same contrast, and the book is an argument that the contrast organises a great deal of what people do when work becomes hard.

The claim is easy to mistake for a slogan about trying hard. Dweck's argument is more specific. A mindset, in her sense, is a belief about what ability is. From that belief other things follow, often without being chosen: what counts as a worthwhile goal, what effort means, what a mistake implies, and what to do when someone else succeeds. The book walks those consequences through school, sport, business and relationships, and then argues that the belief itself can be changed. The later argument about evidence, including the meta-analyses and the national trial that Folio's other cards discuss, is partly an argument about this book, because this book is how the idea left the laboratory.

Fixed and growth, and what each one makes of failure

In the fixed mindset, as Dweck describes it, an important performance is a measurement. It reveals how much ability you have. If the measurement is high, you are safe, and the rational policy is to keep choosing tasks that will measure you highly again. If the measurement is low, the verdict is about you, and it is stable. Effort, on this view, is a suspicious sign. Someone who had enough ability should not have needed it. Asking for help is a confession. The success of another person is a threat, because rank is comparative and the scale is fixed. Challenge is attractive only when it is likely to confirm a flattering measurement. It is to be avoided when it might produce a damaging one.

In the growth mindset the same performance is information about a process. Ability is something you build, so a difficult task is an opportunity to build it rather than a tribunal. Effort is the means, not the evidence of a deficit. Strategies and help are part of the means, which is why the view is not a romance of sheer exertion. A mistake says something about what to try next. Someone else's success can be a lesson rather than a verdict. Dweck's point is that these are not two personalities in the ordinary sense, cheerful versus gloomy, hard-working versus lazy. They are two interpretations of the same event, and the interpretation decides whether the next action is approach or retreat. A student, an athlete or an employee who already works hard can still be in the fixed mindset if the work is organised around protecting a reputation for talent.

How the book extends the laboratory claim

The experimental spine of the book is the praise research and the intervention research that preceded it. Children who are led to think of a success as evidence of intelligence subsequently avoid challenge and cope worse with a hard problem than children who are led to think of the same success as evidence of effort. Students who are taught that intellectual ability can be developed respond to academic difficulty differently from students who are taught that ability is a gift. Dweck treats these findings as the mechanism, and the rest of the book as the mechanism's reach. If a belief about ability changes what failure means, then any institution that hands out judgements of talent is already in the business of teaching a mindset, whether it means to or not.

That is why the chapters on organisations and on bringing up children carry so much of the argument. A company that sorts people, early and publicly, into the talented and the replaceable is, on this account, training everyone to treat performance as a measurement of a fixed trait. A parent or a teacher who praises a child for being smart is not merely being kind. The praise names the achievement as a property of the child, and the child has a reason to defend the property. The alternative Dweck recommends is to praise the process: the strategy, the concentration, the use of a setback, the decision to ask for a better explanation. Process praise is not warmer praise. It is praise that points at something the child can do again on purpose. The book is at its most practical here, and also at its most exposed, because the practical advice travelled further than the effect sizes later justified.

Change, and the false growth mindset

A large part of the book's appeal is its claim that mindsets are not destinies. People can be moved from a fixed belief towards a growth belief by what they are told about the nature of ability, by how the adults around them respond to mistakes, and by their own practice in interpreting difficulty. Dweck presents this as a matter of learning a new theory and then catching the old theory when it speaks. The old theory speaks, on her account, in sentences such as the conviction that a single poor performance shows what you are, or that effort is something to be ashamed of. The new theory has to be specific enough to generate a next action, not only a mood. Strategy, teaching, and the willingness to be a beginner are the actions. Optimism about human potential, left unspecified, is not a mindset in the sense the research used.

Dweck has since warned, and the warning belongs in any honest account of the book, against what she calls a false growth mindset. Praising effort when the effort is not working, telling a child that everything is possible without giving a strategy, or announcing that one has a growth mindset while still ranking people as natural talents, preserves the fixed theory under a more flattering name. The book already contains the materials for that warning, because its mechanism was never effort-worship. The mechanism was a belief about whether ability can be developed, and a set of practices, including help-seeking and a change of strategy, that make the belief true in particular cases. Readers who took the book as an instruction to applaud struggle itself were reading a thinner book than the one she wrote. The thinner reading is nevertheless the one that many schools adopted, which is one reason the later empirical argument became so sharp.

What kind of evidence a popular book can be

Mindset is a synthesis and an argument, illustrated with people from sport, business and education. It is not a systematic review, and it does not weigh the studies in which a mindset measure barely relates to grades. A reader who wants the size of the effect will not find it here, because the book's rhetorical form is the telling case and the reframed episode, not the average. That form has a strength and a cost. The strength is that the mechanism becomes thinkable: you can watch a belief turn a setback into a verdict. The cost is that a thinkable mechanism feels larger than a modest average effect. The years after 2006 were the years in which the idea spread into schools worldwide, often as a poster and a vocabulary, and the years in which other psychologists began to ask how much of the variance in achievement the vocabulary actually moved.

The fair way to read the book now is as a theory of meaning, not as a promise of transformation. It says that people differ in whether they treat ability as fixed, that the difference changes their goals and their response to difficulty, and that institutions teach one view or the other by the way they praise, select and talk about talent. It does not say, because a book of this kind cannot show, that a brief lesson in the growth view will lift a school. That question belongs to the meta-analyses and to the pre-registered national experiment. They are tests of the book's most practical sentence, not replacements for its account of what the two beliefs do to a person facing a hard task.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Growth mindset: big idea, small effects, starts from this book and then refuses to stop there. The card records Dweck's distinction, the early finding that praise for intelligence can backfire, the worldwide spread of the idea after 2006, and the later evidence that the average link to achievement is weak while a targeted intervention can still help some students. The book is the big idea. Sisk and colleagues, and Yeager and colleagues, are the measurement of the effects. Reading Mindset alongside those papers is the only way to keep both halves of the card. The halves are not a contradiction if the book is read as a claim about meaning and motivation, and the papers are read as a claim about how large a change in achievement that kind of meaning produces. Dweck's own caution against a false growth mindset, praising effort that is not working and forgetting strategy and help, is the card's reminder that even the idea has a counterfeit.
