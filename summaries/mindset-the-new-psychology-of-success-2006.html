The experiment at the center of Carol Dweck's 2006 book Mindset is one she ran with Claudia Mueller and published in 1998. One hundred twenty-eight fifth graders, average age about 11, worked for four minutes on puzzles from Raven's Progressive Matrices. Each child was then told they had done very well and had solved at least 80% of the problems they answered. Their actual average was 5.2 out of 10. One sentence differed. Some heard, "You must be smart at these problems." Others heard, "You must have worked hard at these problems." A third group got the success and no added praise.

After that sentence, 67% of the children praised for intelligence chose later problems that would let them look smart. Ninety-two percent of the children praised for effort chose "problems that I'll learn a lot from, even if I won't look so smart." Everyone then faced a harder set and was told they had done a lot worse. On a third set, matched in difficulty to the first, scores fell by 0.92 problems after intelligence praise and rose by 1.21 problems after effort praise. In another study in the same paper, with 88 fifth graders, 38% of the children praised for intelligence reported a higher score to other children than they had actually earned, against 13% praised for effort and 14% given no extra praise.

The book turns that sentence into advice for school, work, and parenting. Praise the strategy and the effort after a real success, because the Mueller experiments found that "you must be smart" made the next failure more costly. A child, or an employee, who treats ability as a fixed trait will protect the image of being talented and will step back from work that might spoil it. The session-long result is large. The average result on real grades is much smaller. In 2018 Victoria Sisk and colleagues combined 273 estimates from 365,915 people and found a correlation of r = .10 between a growth mindset and academic achievement. Across 43 estimates of mindset lessons, from 57,155 people, the average effect on achievement was d = 0.08.

Source. Dweck, C. S. (2006). Mindset: The New Psychology of Success. New York: Random House. https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/44330/mindset-by-carol-s-dweck-phd/
