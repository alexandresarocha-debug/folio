Coin tossing is the textbook example of chance, but a tossed coin obeys the laws of mechanics. The statistician Persi Diaconis and his colleagues Susan Holmes and Richard Montgomery asked how random a real, hand-caught coin flip actually is.

They began with a coin-tossing machine: a spring launches the coin and it lands in a cup. With careful adjustment, a coin started heads up landed heads up one hundred percent of the time. Their conclusion was that coin tossing is physics, not randomness. The apparent randomness comes from small, uncontrolled differences in how people flip.

They then modelled a coin that spins and also wobbles, or precesses, in the air. The model predicts that a vigorously flipped coin caught in the hand tends to land the same way up as it started. Using high-speed photography of real flips to measure the key angle, they estimated the chance of landing as started at about 0.51. The mean of their estimates was 0.508.

They also looked at other claims. Coins made with lead on one side and balsa wood on the other showed no bias when flipped and caught, but coins spun on a table tend to fall heavy side up. They note that Kerrich's famous 10,000 tosses let the coin bounce on a table, so their analysis does not apply, and that his data look fair for all practical purposes. To pin down a bias near one half with a standard error of one in a thousand, they calculated, would take about 250,000 flips.

What this means in real life: a coin flip is fair enough for deciding who goes first, but it is not perfectly fair, and the side facing up at the start matters a little.

Caution: the 0.51 estimate came from a small number of filmed flips. The large test of it came later, from a team that collected hundreds of thousands of flips.
