Background

Stephen Reicher and Alex Haslam ran their prison study for the BBC in 2002 and published the academic account in 2006, in the British Journal of Social Psychology, as Rethinking the psychology of tyranny. The design is a deliberate answer to Stanford. Volunteers were again assigned to be prisoners or guards, and again lived for days inside a built institution that was filmed. The question Stanford had seemed to settle was whether the assignment itself produces tyranny. Give ordinary people the guard role, the famous lesson said, and they will become cruel. Give others the prisoner role, and they will become passive. Reicher and Haslam thought that lesson confused a role with a group that has come to believe in the role.

Their starting theory is social identity. People act in terms of the groups they take themselves to belong to, and in terms of whether anyone successfully defines what that belonging requires. A label is an invitation. It becomes behaviour when the identity takes, when members agree on what matters, and when leaders embody it. Tyranny, on this view, is something a group achieves. It can fail to appear. The failure is the result that Folio's Stanford card records: the guards did not automatically become tyrannical.

The question

The question is whether a prison set, with inequality built into the roles, is sufficient for oppression. If it is, the guards should cohere and dominate, and the prisoners should submit, without anyone having to persuade them that this is the right way to be. If it is not, the study should show the work that a regime has to do. Guards have to come to see themselves as a group with a shared purpose. Prisoners have to fail to develop a shared purpose of their own. Leadership has to make harshness meaningful rather than merely permitted. A study that watches those processes can come out in more than one way, which is the feature the Stanford legend lacked.

They also wanted the ethics to survive contact with a modern committee. Participants were screened. Independent oversight and clinical monitoring were in place. People could leave. The study was designed to be broadcast, so everyone knew there was an audience. That is a limit, and it is also a reply to Stanford, whose own cameras and whose own demand to perform had been left out of the textbook method section. Visibility was treated here as part of the situation, not as an embarrassment to be edited out of the theory.

How the study was set up

Men volunteered for a social-science project that would be televised, and they were assigned to the two roles. The guards had the resources of a regime: better conditions, control over privileges, the authority to punish within limits that had been set for safety. They were not given a briefing that described the prisoners' misery as the scientific aim, and they were not pushed by a warden when they were reluctant. The prisoners had the poorer conditions and the constraint. The investigators planned changes in the structure as the days went on, so that the study could show how identity responds to the way the system is arranged. In the early phase, the boundary between the groups was presented as permeable. A prisoner might be promoted. Later, that route was closed, and the groups were categories a person could not simply exit by impressing the authorities.

Measures were a mixture of the sort a field study can sustain. Observers and the participants themselves reported identification with their group, willingness to act together, attitudes to the regime, and symptoms of stress. The important outcomes are the shifts in who cohered and who did not, because the theory is about cohesion. A tyranny hypothesis that only counts insults will miss a guard group that is divided, and a prisoner group that is becoming a group. Reicher and Haslam treat those shifts as the data.

What happened

The guards never became a confident regime. They were uneasy with the power they had, they disagreed about how to use it, and they did not develop a shared identity in which toughness was the obvious content of being a guard. Without that identity, the privileges and the punishments sat there, available, and went largely unused as a systematic policy. The prisoners were at first fragmented. Once it was clear that promotion was not a real way out, they had more reason to see themselves as prisoners together. Identification among them rose. They began to act as a collective, to challenge the arrangements, and to make the guards' failure of nerve harder to disguise. The first hierarchy did not collapse because a script said it should. It collapsed because one group had come to believe in itself and the other had not.

The later phase keeps the study from being a fable about the kindness of the oppressed. An attempt to replace the hierarchy with a commune, a shared government of the whole set, ran into disagreement and drift. In that vacuum, a subset of participants moved towards a harsher proposal, a new order that would be stricter than the guard regime they had just outlasted. Support for something more authoritarian became thinkable at the point where the egalitarian project failed to give people a working identity. That sequence is the theoretical payload. Tyranny was not the automatic product of the set. It became more attractive when a group identity had failed and someone was willing to offer certainty and hardness in its place. The psychology of tyranny, as the title has it, is the psychology of how harshness comes to look legitimate, which is a question about leadership and belonging.

How the authors read it

Reicher and Haslam read the study as evidence against the role account and for a social-identity account. People do not become their badges. They commit themselves to groups that give them a definition of who they are, and they withhold commitment from groups that do not. Guards who do not identify as guards will not reliably oppress, even in a building that looks like a prison and a schedule that gives them the chance. Prisoners who come to identify as prisoners will resist, even though the role story expects them to crumble. And when the alternative on offer is a failing commune, some people will listen to a more authoritarian bid, because authoritarianism is one way a group tries to solve a crisis of identity. None of this says that oppression is rare. It says that oppression has authors.

The idea Folio's card asks the reader to carry away is engaged followership, which Haslam and Reicher developed fully in later work and which this study prepares. Harm, on that account, is something people do when they identify with a leader and with a cause they believe is right. The BBC guards who hung back were men for whom no such cause had taken. A guard at Stanford who had been told by the superintendent that toughness was what the study was for had been offered a cause. The two studies, read together, separate the uniform from the leadership. The same lens is what the card applies to Milgram. Participants there, Haslam and Reicher argue, were being recruited into a scientific mission. When a prod instead told them, in effect, that they had no choice and must go on, they typically refused. A bare order was a weak instrument.

Limits

It is one simulation, with a small number of men, built for broadcast as well as for a journal. Participants knew there was an audience. Days in a constructed institution are a long time for an experiment and a short time for a theory of dictatorship. A single run can be vivid and still be a single run. The investigators had a theory, and the planned changes in permeability were interventions in the service of that theory, which a critic can call staging. The fair description is a structured field study whose value is the challenge it poses to inevitability. It shows that a prison set without coaching need not produce Stanford's legend. It does not show what every prison set will do, and it does not measure cruelty with the precision of a laboratory contrast.

The authoritarian turn at the end is easy to under-tell. Readers who want the BBC study to be the anti-Stanford, a proof that groups are naturally decent, will miss the part of the result that matters most for politics. When the collective project faltered, hardness gained a hearing. Social identity theory is not a comfort. It is an account of when people become capable of acting together, including acting together in ways that harm.

Why it matters here

On Folio's card, this paper is the empirical alternative to the Stanford legend, already in hand before Le Texier's archive. Guards did not automatically become tyrannical. Prisoners could organise. Tyranny, where it beckoned, beckoned through a failure of one identity and the offer of another. That is the deep-dive claim, and it is why the card says the current view of Stanford is about leadership and demand rather than about spontaneous role conformity. The BBC study is what the phenomenon looks like when the demand to be cruel is taken out and the group processes are allowed to vary.

Read it next to Le Texier and next to The Lucifer Effect, which are the card's other texts. Zimbardo's book states the situationist lesson at book length and applies it to Abu Ghraib. Le Texier shows that the 1971 guards were coached. Reicher and Haslam show that uncoached guards, in a later and more carefully monitored simulation, did not replay the film. The three together are the reason a reader can retire the basement story without retiring the question of how ordinary people come to take part in harsh systems. The answer the card prefers is identification with a leader and a cause, and this study is where that answer was tried against a prison.
