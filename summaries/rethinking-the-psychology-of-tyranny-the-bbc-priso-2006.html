In 2002 Stephen Reicher and S. Alexander Haslam put fifteen men, screened by clinical psychologists and police checks, into a purpose-built institution for eight days. Five were assigned as guards and ten as prisoners. The guards were shown a timetable of chores, roll calls, and exercise, and told to keep the place running. They wrote the rules themselves. The only hard limit was a list of basic rights, including a ban on physical violence. Nobody told them to be harsh. Cameras recorded the whole period, and the BBC broadcast four one-hour films in May 2002. Daily questionnaires tracked whether the men actually identified with the group they had been given.

On day 3 the experimenters promoted one prisoner to guard, as they had warned they might. After that, movement between the groups stopped. Prisoners who had been trying to earn a promotion as individuals began talking about what “we” should do. Their identification with the prisoner group rose across the days. The guards’ identification did not. Several guards were uneasy about giving orders, they could not agree on a line, and the prisoners’ challenges, from insubordination at roll call to a collective protest about the food, met a divided staff. The guard-and-prisoner system collapsed. The men then tried to run the place as one self-governing commune. They could not handle dissent inside it. By the end of the eight days, resistance to a new and harsher hierarchy was weakening, and some participants were ready to let a tighter regime be imposed.

The practical point is about titles and teams. A badge, a shift chart, and a higher salary do not by themselves make people use power. In this study the men on top had to believe the role was theirs, and agree what it was for, before they would enforce it. The men on the bottom coordinated once individual escape was closed and they shared a complaint. The harsher impulse showed up later, when the shared alternative had failed and people wanted someone to impose order. A workplace that assumes “put them in charge and they will become petty tyrants” is betting on a result this study did not get.

The men knew the cameras were there for television, and there was only one group of fifteen, so the record is a single, public case. What it records is still plain: assigned power sat unused until the people who held it identified with it, and tyranny became more acceptable after the attempt at equality broke down.
