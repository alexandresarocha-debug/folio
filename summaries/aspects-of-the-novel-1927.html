Lectures delivered as if the novelists were contemporaries

Aspects of the Novel is the text of the Clark Lectures E. M. Forster gave at Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1927. He addresses an audience that might have expected a history: the novel rising, the novel maturing, the novel in this decade or that. He declines the history. He asks his listeners to imagine the English novelists at work at once, seated together, so that Richardson can be heard beside James and Dickens beside Woolf without a lecture on who learned from whom. The device is playful, and it sets the terms for the whole book. Forster is interested in the elements a novel uses, story, people, plot, fantasy, prophecy, pattern, and rhythm. He treats those elements as available to any novelist, in any year, once the form exists.

The tone is that of a novelist talking to readers, suspicious of professional criticism and willing to be simple where simplicity will do. He grants at the outset that a novel tells a story, and that this fact is both obvious and, to some refined tastes, a little embarrassing. He will not apologise for it. From that grant he builds distinctions that have stayed in ordinary critical speech for a century. They are useful because he keeps attaching them to books a general reader can picture, and because he is honest when a distinction starts to creak.

Story and plot

Story, in Forster's account, is the narrative of events in their time sequence. It answers the question of what happened next, and it appeals to curiosity. He offers a familiar miniature. A king dies, and then a queen dies: that is a story. Plot is the same sequence with a cause made visible. The queen dies of grief: curiosity is joined by intelligence and memory. The reader is asked to hold the first death in mind and to see the second death as its consequence. A plot can withhold a cause and reveal it later. Mystery, he notes, is a plot device that uses time against the reader, and then has to pay the reader back with a fair disclosure. A story can be long and still be only a story. A plot is a story that has become an argument about why.

The distinction is cleaner in the miniature than in any actual novel, and Forster knows it. Novels mix the hunger for the next event with the hunger for the hidden reason. His point is practical. When a book sags, it helps to ask which hunger it is feeding, and whether it has forgotten the other. He is severe about story that has become mere sequence, the and-then of adventure with nothing to remember. He is equally aware that a novel which offers only pattern, and no desire to know what happens, has left out the primitive power that keeps pages turning. The lectures keep this double loyalty. Forster the craftsman will not abandon the reader who wants to know. Forster the critic wants that wanting to be met by form.

Flat people and round ones

The two lectures on people contain the idea Folio's card centres on. A flat character is built around a single idea or quality and can be summed in a sentence. Forster's example is Mrs Micawber in David Copperfield, who will never desert Mr Micawber, and who does not desert him. Such people are easily recognised when they return. They do a great deal of work in comic fiction, and Dickens, whom Forster loves and doubts in equal measure, is full of them. A flat character is a concentration. The danger is that a book will ask a flat character to do a round character's job, or that a reader will despise flatness because a theory has called it primitive. Forster refuses the despising. He thinks flat characters are a legitimate pleasure and a structural convenience, especially when a novelist needs a stable note against which change can be heard.

A round character has thickness, can surprise, and can contain contradiction. Forster's test has become proverbial because it is usable in a single reading. If a character is incapable of surprising you, the character is flat. If the surprise fails to convince, the character was flat and pretending. The test asks for two responses at once: freshness and retrospective fitness. Jane Austen, in his view, passes it with extraordinary economy. Her people are built from a small number of observed social facts, and they still manage to turn in ways that feel both sudden and earned. He is less sure about some of the great Victorian crowds, where a vivid flatness can be mistaken for depth because the voice is loud. The lectures are full of this kind of judgement, offered lightly and meant to be argued with. The test survives the arguments because a reader can apply it tomorrow without adopting Forster's taste.

Fantasy, prophecy, pattern, rhythm

Fantasy, as the next lecture uses the word, means the fiction that asks us to accept something the ordinary social world does not offer: a ghost, a shifting identity, a deliberate impossibility. Forster includes writers who break the frame in order to tell a truth the frame was blocking. He is interested in the tone such a break requires. A fantastic incident dropped into a novel for decoration insults the laws the rest of the book has been obeying. A fantastic incident that is the book's real law, announced early enough that a reader can consent, is another matter. The lecture is a defence of impurity. The novel is allowed to be more than a report on manners.

Prophecy is his name for a rarer note, the note he hears in Dostoevsky, in Melville, in Lawrence. He means a kind of song in the language, a reach towards universality that can make particular characters transparent to something larger. He distinguishes it from ordinary moral seriousness. A preacher in a novel is not yet a prophet in this sense. Prophecy, for him, is a quality of voice that some readers hear and some do not, and he admits the subjectivity. The lecture is the most personal in the book, and the most willing to say that criticism has to stop explaining and start pointing.

Pattern is the shape of the whole book, visible when you stand back: the hourglass of a James novel, the tightly answering parts of a story that has been too perfectly arranged. Forster admires pattern and fears its tyranny. A beautiful shape can freeze the people inside it. Rhythm is his counter-term, and his example is the little phrase of Vinteuil's sonata as it returns, altered, through Proust. Easy rhythm is repetition with variation, a motif the reader learns to recognise. Difficult rhythm is the feeling that the book as a whole has a movement, as a symphony does, which cannot be reduced to a diagram. He ends on rhythm because it lets him praise form without handing novelists over to geometry.

A critic who keeps the reader in the room

Aspects of the Novel has lasted in part because it is willing to be remembered in sentences. Story and plot. Flat and round. The convincing surprise. The little phrase. Under those sentences is a consistent taste: for the novel that remains about human beings in time, for comedy that does not flatten into mechanism, for mystery that plays fair, for a strangeness that is structural. Forster is modest about theory and immodest about preferences. He would rather reread Austen than submit a category to a system. That modesty is a limitation when you want a history of the form, which he has set aside on principle, or a politics of representation, which these lectures barely imagine. It is a strength when you are holding a particular book and trying to say why a person in it does or does not live.

Writers have used the book as a workshop manual, which would have pleased him and made him suspicious. The suspicion is already in the lectures. Categories are aids. The moment a novelist fills a chart, flat on the left and round on the right, the chart has replaced the page. Forster's own practice in his novels, where lyric passages and social comedy share the chapter, shows how mixed a living book can be. The lectures give you the names for the mixture. They also give you permission to keep reading for pleasure, which he treats as criticism's forgotten duty.

Why it matters here

The card on flat and round characters takes its test from these lectures, and it sends a reader to David Copperfield because Mrs Micawber is Forster's own example of a person built around one declaring sentence. Aspects of the Novel is worth reading past that example. The test about surprise sits in a book that also separates story from plot, defends flatness as a tool, and then asks pattern to leave room for rhythm. Used alone, flat and round can become a compliment and an insult. Used as Forster uses them, they are a way of asking what kind of life a given figure is built to have, and what the rest of the novel needs from that life.
