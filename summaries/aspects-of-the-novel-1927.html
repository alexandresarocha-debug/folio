In the Clark Lectures he gave at Cambridge in 1927, and published that year as Aspects of the Novel, E. M. Forster splits characters into flat and round with one sentence from Dickens. "The really flat character can be expressed in one sentence such as 'I never will desert Mr. Micawber.' There is Mrs. Micawber—she says she won't desert Mr. Micawber, she doesn't, and there she is." She is built around a single idea. She can be funny every time she appears because the reader already knows the whole of her.

His test for the other kind is just as concrete. "The test of a round character is whether it is capable of surprising in a convincing way. If it never surprises, it is flat. If it does not convince, it is a flat pretending to be round." Becky Sharp, in Thackeray, passes it. So do the principal people in War and Peace. A flat character who fails the comic version of the trick is a Sussex farmer in a romance Forster had been reading, whose whole existence is the sentence "I'll plough up that bit of gorse." He says it, he does it, and the reader does not care, because nothing in him was ever larger than the formula.

Forster's other flat specimen is Caleb Balderstone in Scott's The Bride of Lammermoor. Whatever lie he tells or plate he breaks, it is to hide the poverty of his master's house. There is nothing in him for the idea to be fixed into. He is the idea. Forster thought a serious flat character, someone who enters crying for revenge and never does anything else, becomes a bore, and that only round people can carry tragedy for long. He also thought a novel usually needs both, the surprising person set among figures the reader can sum up in a line.

You already use the test on people at work and in fiction. A colleague you can sum up as "she always stays late" is flat until the day she leaves at noon for a reason you believe. A protagonist who only ever wants one thing, and gets it on schedule, produces the boredom Forster found in the farmer. Flat people are useful. Someone has to carry the joke, or the message, or the tea. The book asks you to notice when a writer has tried to pass a sentence off as a life.
