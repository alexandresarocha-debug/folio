In 2013 David Comer Kidd and Emanuele Castano sat people down with a few pages of prose and then showed them photographs of eyes. The test, the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, asks which word names the feeling in the eye region of a face. In the first experiment, 86 people read either a short piece of literary fiction or a short piece of nonfiction. Eyes-test scores were higher after the literary pages, F(1, 82) = 6.40, p = 0.01. A simpler false-belief task given in the same sitting showed no difference.

They repeated the setup across five experiments, changing the comparison. Literary texts were prize stories and canonical fiction. Popular texts were genre pieces from a commercial anthology. In the largest round, 356 people read a story from the 2012 PEN/O. Henry Prize winners, a piece of popular fiction, or nothing at all. Literary fiction again came out ahead on the eyes test, F(2, 352) = 3.10, p = 0.04. In a round with 72 people, accuracy on another mind-reading task, the Yoni test, was 0.89 after literary fiction and 0.85 after popular fiction.

The practical hope, and the headline it produced, was that a few pages of serious fiction before a meeting or a date would make you better at the person across the table. What the experiments measured was a score, right afterward, on naming feelings from eyes. That is a real skill. It is a long way from how you treat someone in the room.

The one-sitting result did not hold up. In 2016 Maria Eugenia Panero and colleagues pooled three labs and 792 people, using the same kinds of texts. After literary fiction the adjusted eyes-test mean was 26.81. After nonfiction it was 27.02, p = .775, d = 0.07. After popular fiction it was 25.99 against 25.78 for literary fiction, p = .363. After no reading it was 26.25 against 26.31, p = .775. A measure of how much fiction people had read over a lifetime still predicted the eyes score. One passage did not.

Source. David Comer Kidd and Emanuele Castano, "Reading Literary Fiction Improves Theory of Mind," Science 342 (18 October 2013): 377–380. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1239918
