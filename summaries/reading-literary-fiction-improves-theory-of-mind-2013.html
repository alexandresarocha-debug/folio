A claim that could be tested in an hour

In 2013 David Comer Kidd and Emanuele Castano published a paper in Science with a title that travelled well beyond the journal: Reading Literary Fiction Improves Theory of Mind. Theory of mind, in the sense they use, is the ability to infer what other people believe, feel, and intend. Their proposal was specific. Literary fiction, they argued, recruits that ability because it presents people whose minds are not obvious. The reader has to work. Popular fiction, on their account, tends to offer worlds that fit the expectations a reader brings, and so it gives theory of mind less to do. Non-fiction and simple absence of reading were further comparisons. The claim was about a temporary effect. Read a short literary text, and for a little while afterward you should be better at tests that ask you to read minds.

They defined the contrast with a procedure they were frank about. There is no meter that yields a number called literariness. They took prize-winning and canonical fiction as the literary side, on the grounds that jurors and a longer critical tradition are imperfect but real judges of the qualities they cared about. They took widely sold genre fiction as the popular side. Reporting on the paper named authors in the literary set such as Chekhov and DeLillo, and popular texts that included commercial romance, a bestselling thriller, and science fiction. The operational cut is open to argument, and the argument matters, because the paper's whole distinction depends on it. What they could do, and did, was hold the cut stable and then randomise which kind of text a person received.

Five experiments, one sitting each

The paper reports five experiments. In each, a participant read a short text, or in some conditions read nothing, and then took tests. The test that anchors the public discussion is the Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, in which you see only the eye region of a face and choose which emotion a word-list best names. Another task, used in one of the experiments, asks for judgements of emotion from faces shown briefly. Later experiments also included measures aimed at cognitive theory of mind, reasoning about beliefs, alongside the affective measures. Experiment 1 compared literary fiction with non-fiction. Experiments 2 to 5 compared literary fiction with popular fiction, and some of those experiments included a no-reading condition. Across the series, the authors reported better performance after literary fiction.

They also measured how familiar people already were with fiction, using an author-recognition test that asks people to pick real writers out of a list salted with fakes. They looked at mood and at how transported people felt by the text they had just read. The design is an attempt to show that the bump, if there is one, tracks the kind of text and survives those extra measurements. The participants were the usual laboratory public, and the reading was brief. A few pages, then a test. The paper's language matches that scale. It speaks of a temporary enhancement, an effect of engagement with a certain kind of art, visible immediately afterward. It does not claim that a term of schooling has been replaced by a quarter of an hour.

Why the result was so easy to tell

The result was easy to tell because it flattered a hope that teachers, novelists, and readers already had. If literary fiction is the difficult kind, and difficulty is precisely the work of inferring other minds, then the difficulty is a kind of practice. Kidd and Castano link that practice to ideas about polyphonic, writerly texts, in which a reader cannot lean on a single obvious perspective. On this telling, popular fiction confirms the social scripts you already own, and literary fiction disturbs them, so the mind that has just been disturbed is briefly more alert to real faces and real beliefs. The Eyes test, though it uses photographs and words, stood in public summaries for empathy itself. That slide was understandable and costly. A score for naming mental states from eyes is a score for that task. Empathy in the sense of care, or of accurate behaviour towards a person in front of you, is a further claim the experiments were not built to settle.

The paper is careful in places where the retelling was loose. It treats the effect as immediate and short-run. It treats the literary-popular cut as a working method forced by the vagueness of the category. It sits inside a longer conversation, which Raymond Mar and Keith Oatley had already framed, about fiction as a simulation of social experience. Kidd and Castano's contribution was to try to show a causal effect in the laboratory, with random assignment. Earlier studies had reported a correlation between a life of reading and a test score. Random assignment is the strength. The dose is the weakness. A single passage can prime a way of thinking. A single passage is a strange stand-in for the habit of reading books.

What a careful reading keeps in view

A careful reading of the 2013 paper keeps four facts in the same frame. First, the outcome is performance on theory-of-mind tasks, above all the Eyes test, administered right after reading. Second, the texts are short, and the literary ones were chosen by reputation and prizes. Third, the authors report the advantage for literary fiction across five experiments with slightly different comparison groups. Fourth, familiarity with fiction was measured because it was already known to relate to these tasks, which means the paper itself recognises a slower story beside the hour-long one. None of these facts is a flaw by itself. Together they describe a provocative laboratory finding with a narrow mouth. The mouth is what later work tried to walk through again.

The paper rewards being read as a methods piece and as a cultural event. As a methods piece, it shows how a literary distinction can be turned into an experimental factor, and what you have to ignore in order to do it. You have to ignore differences of length, of subject, of the pleasure a given reader happens to take, and of everything a real reading life accumulates between one story and the next. As a cultural event, it shows how quickly a temporary score on a face task becomes a headline about becoming a better person. The headline is part of the history of the paper. It is part of why the replications that followed were worth doing, and why their failure to find the same brief effect changed the responsible summary.

Why it matters here

Folio's card asks whether reading fiction makes you more empathetic, and it puts this Science paper at the centre of the recent attempt to answer with an experiment. The card's caution is already in the card: a striking one-session result, widely reported, did not reappear in a larger 2016 replication, and later averages look small. Reading Kidd and Castano is how you see what, exactly, failed to reappear. It was a brief advantage on theory-of-mind tests after prize-winning and canonical fiction, compared with popular fiction, non-fiction, or no reading. It was a serious attempt to catch Mar and Oatley's simulation idea in the act. The attempt is worth knowing because the idea remains interesting at the scale of a reading life, and the hour-long version of the idea was always a harder, stranger claim than the headlines admitted.
