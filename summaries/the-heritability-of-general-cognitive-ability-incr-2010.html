Common sense says that the older we get, the more our experiences should shape us. For general cognitive ability, the core of what IQ tests measure, this study found the opposite, and it found the family home fading too.

C. M. A. Haworth, Robert Plomin and colleagues, based at King's College London, built a consortium of six twin studies from four countries: three from the United States (Ohio, Colorado and Minnesota) and one each from Australia, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom. Together they had about 11,000 twin pairs with cognitive test data, more than all earlier studies combined. Each study used its own tests, so the team built a general ability score within each one.

They compared three age groups: childhood (about 9 years, 2,680 pairs), adolescence (about 12, 4,934 pairs) and young adulthood (about 17, 3,075 pairs). Heritability rose from 41% to 55% to 66%.

The shared environment moved the other way. It explained 33% of the differences in childhood, 18% in adolescence and 16% in young adulthood. The non-shared environment, which in this kind of study also includes measurement error, was 26%, 27% and then 19%. So the family home clearly matters for children's cognitive ability. But its share roughly halves by the teenage years.

The authors' explanation is gene-environment correlation: as children grow up, they increasingly select, modify and even create their own experiences, partly based on their genetic tendencies.

What this means in real life: the family a child grows up in makes a real difference to test scores in childhood, but by the late teens, differences in ability track genes and each person's own path more than the house they grew up in.

Caution: the shared environment did not vanish; 16% at 17 is still a real effect. Six different studies with different tests were pooled. All the samples came from the US, the UK, the Netherlands and Australia. The study measures the spread within these populations, not what a single family can or cannot do.
