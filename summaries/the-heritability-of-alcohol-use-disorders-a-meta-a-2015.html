For decades, single studies gave very different answers to how much alcoholism is linked to genes. Some small twin studies put the figure near zero, others above 0.7. Bradley Verhulst, Michael Neale and Kenneth Kendler at Virginia Commonwealth University wanted one combined answer.

They searched for every separate twin and adoption study of alcohol use disorder, the medical term that covers alcohol abuse and dependence. They found 12 twin studies and 5 adoption studies, the oldest a Swedish twin study from 1960. They rebuilt the data from each paper and fitted one model to all of it.

The two designs work differently. Twin studies compare identical twins with non-identical twins. Adoption studies compare adopted children with the biological parents who did not raise them. Each has its own weak spots, so if both give the same answer, that answer is more believable.

They did agree. Twin studies alone gave a heritability of 0.51. Adding the adoption studies gave a best overall estimate of 0.49, with a likely range of 0.43 to 0.53. The shared family environment added about 0.10. Each person's own experiences accounted for the rest.

The figure barely moved between groups. Men came out at 0.52 and women at 0.44, a gap too small to be sure of, and the model fitted just as well with one figure for both. Studies that interviewed people gave the same answer as studies that used hospital, population or temperance board records. A test for whether different genes act in men and women found no sign of it, though that test was imprecise.

What this means in real life: a family history of alcoholism is a real warning sign, but about half of the differences in risk lie outside the genes. Heritability describes a population. It cannot say whether one person will develop the disorder.

Caution: some of the older studies were small, especially for women, and their individual estimates were very uncertain. The samples came from Sweden, Denmark, Finland, England, the US and Australia, and diagnoses varied from study to study. The estimate applies to those countries and periods, not to every society.

Source. Verhulst, B., Neale, M. C., and Kendler, K. S. (2015). The heritability of alcohol use disorders: a meta-analysis of twin and adoption studies. Psychological Medicine, 45(5), 1061-1072. doi:10.1017/S0033291714002165. Free full text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4345133/
