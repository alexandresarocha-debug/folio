In Misbehaving, Richard Thaler tells the story of a market that would not clear. With Daniel Kahneman and Jack Knetsch, he gave coffee mugs to a random half of a room of students and let the others buy. At Simon Fraser University, 77 students were sellers, buyers, or choosers. Choosers had no mug but could take the mug or a sum of cash at each price, so they were not poorer than the sellers. The median seller wanted $7.12 to give the mug up. The median chooser took cash once the price passed $3.12. The median buyer would pay $2.87. About 12.5 trades should have happened. Three did. Tokens with an assigned cash value, in a companion market, traded in the volume a textbook predicts. The stuck market was about the mug in the hand.

The second story is a savings plan he and Shlomo Benartzi ran at a midsize manufacturing company in 1998. Workers who would not raise their retirement contribution on the spot could agree, instead, that part of each future raise would go to the plan. Of 207 people who had turned down an immediate increase, 162, or 78 percent, joined. Their average saving rate went from 3.5 percent to 13.6 percent over four annual raises, about 40 months. Eighty percent of those who joined were still in at the fourth raise. People who left stopped the future increases. They did not cut the rate back to where it had started.

Both stories are about a change that feels like a loss now. Handing over the mug is a loss today, so the price to do it sits far above the price to acquire one. Cutting this month's paycheck is a loss today, so the contribution stays low. A raise that has not arrived yet is not in the mental account of money you already spend, and signing away a slice of it is easier. Thaler's practical lesson for a workplace or a sale is to move the painful part off the present: the future raise, the default enrollment, the trial that is already in the customer's hands.

The mug result has a limit. In 2005 Charles Plott and Kathryn Zeiler reproduced a gap when they copied the original selling task, medians of $1.50 and $4.50. After they trained people on how the price would be drawn and kept the answers anonymous, the medians in one experiment were $5.00 either way. Some of what looks like a deep attachment to the thing you hold is uncertainty about the rules of the sale.
