Background

By 2015 the phrase behavioural economics no longer needed an apology in a seminar. Richard Thaler's Misbehaving is the story of how that happened, told by someone who spent the previous decades insisting that the anomalies were the evidence. The book is a memoir of a field rather than a new experiment. It follows Thaler from early discomfort with the textbook model of a relentlessly rational agent, through the collaboration with Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, into finance, policy and the Nobel recognition that closed the argument's first life. Folio's prospect-theory card points to it for one job in particular: an account of how prospect theory and the endowment effect entered economics, not as dinner-party observations, but as claims the profession had to answer.

The title is a joke with a target. In the economics Thaler was trained in, to misbehave was to violate the model, and the violation was the agent's fault. The book reverses the scolding. If people systematically refuse the choice the model calls correct, the model is the thing that has misdescribed the choice. The tone is personal, full of rooms and arguments, and the method is narrative. A walkthrough can say what the narrative is for. It should not pretend the book is a data table.

The question

How did a set of psychological regularities become an economics? Thaler's question is historical and strategic at once. Which anomalies were sharp enough to embarrass expected-utility theory and the assumption that money is money, with no memory of how it was got or what it was labelled? Who had to be persuaded, and which kinds of evidence did economists actually find persuasive? What did the new field keep from the old one, and what did it refuse? The book is an answer in the first person. It is also a map of the ideas a reader of the 1979 paper will meet next: mental accounting, the endowment effect, the planner's struggle with a future self, and the slow acceptance of a reference point as a respectable object.

How the book is built

Thaler organises the story roughly as a life. Early chapters collect the irritations that did not fit. People treat a windfall differently from wages. They let a sunk cost drag a decision it should not touch. They fail to ignore small differences the model says are the whole of rationality, and they agonise over large ones the model says are easy. He gives these patterns a family name, mental accounting: money is sorted into buckets, and a dollar in one bucket is not felt as a dollar in another. The buckets are a lay version of a reference point. A loss inside the bucket marked holiday weighs differently from the same sum inside the bucket marked rent.

The middle of the book is the alliance with Kahneman and Tversky, and the experiments that made ownership empirical. Thaler recounts how prospect theory gave him a language for what he had been seeing, and how the mug markets, run with Kahneman and Knetsch, turned a hunch about reluctance into a result economists could not file as chatter. A later stretch moves into financial markets, where the same reluctance and the same narrow framing show up as investors who sell winners and sit on losers, and as prices that drift from the values a cool model would set. The closing movement is institutional: journals, conferences, younger co-authors, and the argument, developed with Cass Sunstein, that policy can take predictable misbehaviour as a design constraint rather than as a reason to scold the public.

What the narrative claims

The claim is not that people are foolish and economists were wicked. It is that the simplifying figure of the rational agent did real scientific work, and then overstayed. Expected-utility theory and the idea that choices reveal a stable preference over final wealth are powerful where the problem is a clean gamble or a thick market with fast feedback. They misfire where the problem is a change from a reference point, a loss that looms, a sum of money wearing a label, or a good that has just become mine. Prospect theory is, in Thaler's telling, the tool that made those misfires predictable rather than anecdotal. The endowment effect is the misfire he keeps returning to, because it struck at a theorem economists used in law and in policy, the claim that initial ownership would be bargained away.

He also claims a method. Behavioural economics, as this book practises it, did not throw out optimisation, equilibrium or data. It changed the inputs. People optimise something that includes losses, narrow frames and present comfort. Markets equilibrate something that includes those people. The fight was about whether those amendments were a mess or a model. Thaler's history says the fight was won by accumulation: one anomaly that survived contact with a sceptic, then another, until the cost of ignoring the pile exceeded the cost of rewriting the chapter.

How to read the memoir

Read it as a guided tour of the card's other documents, with a protagonist. The 1979 paper appears as the theoretical gift. The 1990 experiments appear as the moment a psychological curve became a failed prediction about trading volume. Gal and Rucker's later doubts about loss aversion are not this book's subject, and a reader should not import them backwards into Thaler's confidence. What the memoir does show is how a strong claim travels: from a value function, to a mug, to a syllabus, to a regulatory argument. The confidence is part of the history. So is the resistance, which Thaler records without pretending it was only prejudice. Many of the early objections were the right kind. Show me the incentive. Show me the market. Show me that the gap survives when people understand the rules. The experiments exist because those objections were good.

The book is also a lesson in what a field looks like from inside while it is still rude. Names, seminars and rejected papers are doing explanatory work. An idea can be correct and still unusable until someone translates it into the host discipline's tests. Thaler presents himself as that translator, and the presentation is interested, which is fine once it is noticed. The scientific claims he is translating can be checked against the papers. The charm of the telling is not one of those claims.

Limits

A memoir selects. Episodes that display the author's persistence are likelier to be scene-length than episodes in which a clever behavioural story died. Readers who want the graveyard of failed anomalies will not find a complete one here. Readers who want a measured review of how often loss aversion appears, at what stakes, will not find that either. Gal and Rucker's question is a later question. Treating Misbehaving as the last word on the size of loss aversion would confuse a participant's history with a meta-analysis.

There is a limit of politics as well. The later chapters lean towards designing choices so that predictable error counts less, the programme that came to be called nudge. That programme borrows the psychology. It adds a claim about whose framing of the choice ought to prevail, and that claim is not settled by a mug experiment. A walkthrough of this book can say that Thaler carried prospect theory into policy argument. It should not pretend the 1979 paper, or the 1990 markets, elected a theory of government.

What the book leaves open

The history ends, as histories of this sort do, on a victory. By the time of writing, behavioural ideas were in finance, in public policy and in the prize citations. Open questions are visible in the cracks of that victory. How large is the asymmetry between losses and gains once inertia is measured separately? When does a market teach the endowment effect away, and when does it amplify it? Which mental accounts are stable enough to build a policy on, and which collapse as soon as the label is laughed at? Thaler's story explains why those questions became askable inside economics. The papers on the rest of the card are where they get asked.

The book is also silent, in the way a celebration is silent, about the replication troubles that struck other parts of social psychology in the same years. Prospect theory's central choice patterns are not the effects that collapsed. A reader of Folio's replication path should still notice the difference in genre. Misbehaving is an insider's history of ideas that largely held. It is not an audit.

Why it matters here

The card's job for this book is narrow and useful. Prospect theory can be read as an equation. The endowment effect can be read as a classroom market. Neither reading explains how either result became something an economist had to teach. Misbehaving is that explanation, told from the side of the room that was trying to get the results in. Read Kahneman and Tversky for the value function, the 1990 paper for the mugs and the tokens, and Gal and Rucker for the argument that loss aversion has been asked to explain too much. Read Thaler for the path those ideas walked, and for a reminder that a reference point had to be fought for before it could be cautioned against.
