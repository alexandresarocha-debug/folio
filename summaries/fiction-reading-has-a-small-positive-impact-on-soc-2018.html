David Dodell-Feder and Diana I. Tamir pooled every experiment they could find, published or not, that had people read fiction and then take a social-cognition test. The 2018 meta-analysis covers 53 effect sizes from 14 studies: 1,615 people in the fiction conditions and 1,843 in the comparison conditions, who read nonfiction or read nothing. Fiction readers did better. The average advantage was small, g = .15, with a 95 percent confidence interval from .02 to .29, p = .029. Counting the unpublished data with no exclusions applied, the figure was g = .16.

The average moves when single studies are taken out. One result, from Johnson, Jasper, Griffin, and Huffman in 2013, was an outlier at g = 1.51. Removing it lowered the overall effect to g = .13, p = .039. Removing Kidd and Castano's 2013 Science paper, the famous claim that one literary passage helps, pushed the pooled result to p = .053. The Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test, photographs of eyes and a menu of emotion words, supplied 32.1 percent of the effects in the pool. Most of the people tested were young. The mean age across samples was 27.9.

In ordinary life that is a small shift on a lab score, not a new character. It might show up as a slightly sharper guess about what a stranger feels. It is not evidence that a novel will make someone kinder to a patient, a customer, or a child. Dodell-Feder and Tamir say as much. They ask for longer reading than a few pages, a check on whether anything transfers to real social behavior, and a look at how the effect would work if it is real.

The pattern fits the failed one-sitting replications without erasing the average. A single prize story, read once, has not reliably moved the eyes test. Across many experiments, fiction still comes out a little ahead of nonfiction or of no reading. The edge is small enough that dropping one well-known paper puts it on the border of statistical significance.
