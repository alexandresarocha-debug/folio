Putting the experiments in one frame

In 2018 David Dodell-Feder and Diana I. Tamir published a meta-analysis in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General whose title states the size of the result before the argument begins. Fiction reading has a small positive impact on social cognition. The question they pose is causal and narrow. Scholars in the humanities and the social sciences had long credited stories with making people more attuned to one another. Correlational studies had found that people who read more fiction tend to score better on tests of social understanding. Correlation leaves the direction open. Their paper asks whether the experimental record, in which reading is actually assigned, shows a gain relative to a comparison condition.

They gathered experimental data, published and unpublished, and ran a multilevel random-effects meta-analysis. The pool they report is 53 effect sizes drawn from 14 studies. The comparison is fiction reading set against non-fiction reading or against no reading. The outcome is performance on social-cognitive measures: tasks that ask a person to infer mental states, and related measures of social understanding, some of them performance tasks and some of them self-reports. The Reading the Mind in the Eyes Test is the single most common instrument in the pool, which means the literature they are summarising leans on the same face-and-word task that made the 2013 Science paper famous. The meta-analysis is an average across that family of attempts, weighted and modelled so that several effects from one paper do not pretend to be several independent literatures.

A small average, and a wide scatter

The average they obtain is small and, in their analysis, statistically significant. In the metric they use, the summary effect is about 0.15 to 0.16, a shift that would be easy to miss in any one modest experiment and easy to over-describe in a headline. They re-estimated the model using unpublished data with fewer exclusions and got the same general picture. The result survived a set of sensitivity checks. One study in the pool produced an effect large enough to count as an influential outlier; removing it shrank the average a little and left the direction in place. Leave-one-out checks told a similar story. The finding is a property of the pool, a slight tilt towards a benefit for fiction, visible when many noisy experiments are allowed to vote.

The scatter around that tilt is part of the result. The authors report substantial heterogeneity. Effects in the literature run from negative to large and positive, and a good share of the variation sits inside studies, where one paper may contain several comparisons that do not agree. A reader who wants the meta-analysis to say that every fiction experiment works, or that literary fiction in particular works the way Kidd and Castano hoped, will not find that sentence. The contrast coded here is fiction against non-fiction or against silence. Genre, prestige, and the literary-popular cut that organised the 2013 paper are details of individual studies, dissolved into a broader factor called fiction. The small positive impact is an impact of that broader factor, on the measures researchers happened to use, in the samples they could recruit. Many of those samples were students. Many of the outcomes were laboratory tasks.

Publication bias, and what they say about it

A meta-analysis of a fashionable idea has to ask whether the file drawer is doing the work. Studies that find a tidy benefit are easier to publish than studies that find nothing, and an average built only from the tidy ones will flatter the idea. Dodell-Feder and Tamir tested for that pattern. Their report says the effect does not appear to be an artefact of publication bias, and they note that unpublished datasets were part of the pool from the start. This is a more reassuring check than a shrug, and it is still a check on the studies they could see. A bias test can fail to detect a distortion. What it can do, and what they say it did, is withhold the convenient explanation that the whole tilt is a publishing illusion. The tilt is small enough that it does not need an illusion to be unsurprising. A genuine, slight advantage is exactly the sort of thing single laboratories will sometimes catch and sometimes miss.

That fact is how their paper sits beside Panero's replication. A multi-lab attempt can fail to show a unique immediate benefit of one literary passage and still be compatible with a small average benefit of fiction across many designs. The designs are different. Panero asked whether literary fiction beats three specific alternatives on one test, in one sitting, in a pre-registered spirit of repetition. Dodell-Feder and Tamir ask what the experimental literature, taken as a literature, tends to find when fiction is compared with non-fiction or with no reading. Both answers can be true. The first says the celebrated version of the claim was too sharp. The second says a blunter version, assigned fiction set against non-fiction or against silence, has a small experimental footprint. The footprint is real in their numbers and modest in their prose.

What they ask the next experiments to do

The discussion is as useful as the estimate, because it refuses to promote a small laboratory tilt into a theory of citizenship. The authors recommend three kinds of improvement. Manipulations of reading should be stronger than a brief excerpt if the claim of interest is about reading in any substantial sense. Outcomes should include real-world social functioning, so that a gain on an eyes test has to show that it travels into behaviour with people. Mechanisms should be tested, because an average effect that might come from attention, mood, verbal practice, or actual perspective-taking is an average still waiting for an explanation. These recommendations are a map of what the 14 studies could not jointly settle.

Read against Mar and Oatley's account of fiction as social simulation, the meta-analysis is a measured audit. Simulation is a proposal about the function of stories over the long run of a culture and a life. An effect of around 0.15 on a mixed set of laboratory tasks is what you get when you look for a trace of that proposal in the experiments so far. Traces can be real and still too small to reorganise a curriculum, and too dependent on artificial reading to stand in for the habit of finishing books. Dodell-Feder and Tamir give the trace a size. The size is the contribution, and the modesty of the size is the part a summary has to keep.

Why it matters here

Folio's card on empathy and fiction uses this meta-analysis to cool the 2013 headline without pretending the experimental cupboard is empty. The cooling is quantitative. Across 14 studies and 53 effects, assigned fiction reading sits a little above non-fiction and above no reading on social-cognitive scores, and the authors do not think publication bias invented the difference. The card's phrase about small effects is this paper's own conclusion, including the warning implied by a number that small. Read it after Kidd and Castano and after Panero, and the three pieces lock. A sharp one-session claim about literary fiction failed to replicate. A broader average across experiments remains, and it is small. Anything you want to believe about a life of reading has to be argued on evidence this audit does not pretend to be.
