The physics model of Diaconis, Holmes and Montgomery predicted that a flipped coin caught in the hand lands the same way up as it started about 51% of the time. Testing a bias that small needs an enormous number of flips. A team led by František Bartoš and Eric-Jan Wagenmakers, most of them at the University of Amsterdam, decided to collect them.

In all, 48 people flipped coins of 44 different currencies and denominations, recording the side facing up before each flip and the side that landed. Each person collected sequences of 100 consecutive flips, and people were encouraged to swap coins so that effects of the person and of the coin could be separated. The total was 350,757 flips.

The coins landed on the same side they started more often than not: the estimated probability was 0.508, with a 95% credible interval of 0.506 to 0.509. That matches the earlier prediction closely. When the starting side was random, heads and tails were equally likely: the share of heads was 0.500. There was no sign that any coin favoured heads or tails.

Two further findings were interesting. People varied a lot in how strong their same-side bias was, which fits the idea that it comes from the wobble in each person's flip. And the bias shrank as people flipped more coins, which the authors suggest may be because practice produced cleaner, less wobbly flips.

What this means in real life: if you want a fair call, don't let the caller see which side is up before the flip.

Caution: this is a preprint, not yet a peer-reviewed journal paper at the time of writing. Nearly all the flippers were co-authors who knew the hypothesis being tested. The effect is small: under one percentage point of advantage.

Source. Bartoš, F., Sarafoglou, A., Godmann, H. R., et al. (2023, revised 2025). Fair coins tend to land on the same side they started: evidence from 350,757 flips. arXiv preprint 2310.04153 (version 4). https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.04153
