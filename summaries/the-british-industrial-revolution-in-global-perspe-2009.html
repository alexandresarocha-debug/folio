Around 1780, Robert C. Allen calculates, a spinning jenny paid in England and did not pay in France or India. He puts the rate of return at 38 percent in England, against a return of about 15 percent that an investor would have required. In France the return comes out at 2.5 percent. In India it comes out at minus 5.2 percent. The machine was the same machine. James Hargreaves's jenny let one person spin several threads at once. What differed was the price of the worker relative to the price of the machine. English wages were high, and the jenny was cheap there. Where labor was cheap, or the machine was expensive, a spinner with a wheel was the rational choice. Allen's conclusion is sharp. The jenny was worth inventing in Britain because it was worth using in Britain. French and Indian manufacturers were not confused, and they were not waiting on a better culture. At their prices, mechanizing cotton spinning would have lost money.

He generalizes from the jenny to the Industrial Revolution. Britain was a high-wage economy with unusually cheap coal close to its workshops. Inventions that saved labor and burned fuel were profitable there and unprofitable in societies where workers were cheap and fuel was dear. The steam engine, the substitution of coal for wood, and the mechanization of textiles are, on this account, responses to a local price list. Institutions and scientific culture may have mattered at the margin. They are not, in his telling, what made a profitable invention in Lancashire and a bad investment in Bengal.

The practical implication is that "why didn't they invent it" is often the wrong question. People invent and adopt what pays at the wages and fuel prices they actually face. A labor-saving device is a gift in a place where labor is the scarce, expensive input. It is a pointless expense where labor is the cheap one. Development advice that urges a poor country to copy a rich country's machines, without asking what a worker costs relative to a machine, repeats the mistake Allen thinks the old question made.

The jenny arithmetic has been challenged on its own assumptions. Ugo Gragnolati, Daniele Moschella, and Emanuele Pugliese, in the Journal of Economic History in 2011, pointed out that Allen held the spinner's output fixed, so that the jenny's effect was to cut her work year to a third. If she kept working the same number of days and produced three times as much yarn, they argue, the capital cost would be spread over more output and the jenny would have been profitable in France as well as in England. Profitability would still have been higher in England. It would no longer mark a unique British opportunity. Nicholas Crafts has also noted that coal, the other half of Allen's slogan, does not enter the cotton calculation at all.
