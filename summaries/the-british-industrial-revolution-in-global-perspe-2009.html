Why Britain, and why machines that saved labour

Robert Allen's The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective, published in 2009, is an economist's history of the first industrial revolution. It accepts the premise, sharpened by the California School, that the explanation has to survive a comparison with other advanced regions, especially in Asia. It does not accept Kenneth Pomeranz's further claim that those regions were still level with England until coal and colonies broke a shared ecological ceiling in the late eighteenth century. Allen's opening claim is about prices. Britain, and England in particular, entered the eighteenth century as a high-wage economy with unusually cheap energy. Those two prices made it profitable, in Britain and not elsewhere, to invent machines that substituted capital and coal for labour. The industrial revolution was a response to a wage and price structure. It was not a sudden gift of English ingenuity, and it was not the automatic result of having scientists. Other countries had clever artisans. Britain had the incentives that made a particular kind of cleverness pay.

The high-wage economy before the factories

The first half of the argument is a reconstruction of the pre-industrial economy. Allen argues that England became a high-wage economy over the early modern centuries because of commercial success, urbanisation, and agricultural change, not because a factory system already existed. London's growth, the expansion of trade, including trade connected with empire, and a productive agriculture that released labour and fed cities, pushed up the demand for workers. Wages in England, measured against the cost of subsistence and against the price of capital and energy, stood high relative to much of Europe and to the advanced parts of Asia. He is careful about what high means. It does not mean that English labourers were comfortable by a modern standard. It means that labour was expensive relative to other inputs, so a producer had a reason to economise on it. The same commercial success that Pomeranz folds into the later story of ghost acres is, for Allen, already visible in the wage structure before the spinning jenny.

Cheap energy is the other price. Coal in northern and midland England was abundant and, because of geology and the cost of transport, cheap at the places where industry could use it. Fuel in London and in the textile districts was a smaller share of costs than fuel in cities that depended on wood brought from a distance. Allen treats this as a fact about Britain that a theory of culture cannot imitate. A society can esteem useful knowledge and still face a fuel price that makes a coal-fired, capital-intensive technique a folly. Britain esteemed, or at any rate produced, inventors. The prices told them which inventions to attempt. The early industrial revolution, in his account, is a cluster of responses to those prices. Machinery in cotton, the use of coke in iron, and the improvement of the steam engine are labour-saving and energy-using. That bias is the signature of the British factor prices, and it is what a purely scientific theory of the period does not predict, since a scientific culture could just as well have produced inventions that saved fuel or saved capital.

Invention, improvement, and the path of diffusion

Allen distinguishes the moment of invention from the later life of a technology, and the distinction is one of the most useful in the book. At British prices, a crude early machine could already be profitable. It was worth building the spinning jenny, worth tinkering with it, worth investing in a design that wasted coal by the standards of a fuel-scarce country. In a low-wage, expensive-energy economy the same crude machine lost money, so nobody there had a reason to be the first. Once the machine existed, decades of improvement made it more productive and more frugal. The improved version could then be profitable at lower wages and higher fuel prices. Diffusion followed invention with a lag, and the lag makes the industrial revolution look, misleadingly, like a British secret that was eventually shared. On Allen's telling there was no secret that other cultures were unfit to understand. There was a machine that became worth copying only after British prices had paid for its adolescence.

The global perspective is this sequence seen from the places that adopted late. Indian and Chinese textile industries were sophisticated. They were not stupid for failing to invent the jenny in the eighteenth century. At their factor prices the jenny was not the rational gamble. They adopted mechanised spinning when the improved technology and the competitive pressure, including the pressure of British exports and of imperial policy, changed the calculation. Allen's framework therefore refuses both a triumphalist story in which Britain invented because Britain was advanced in some general cultural sense, and a story in which the absence of early machines elsewhere is evidence of a blocked society. The absence is evidence about prices. The later adoption is evidence that the technology, once cheap enough, was not culturally imprisoned.

Where science and empire sit in his account

The conclusion is an explanation of timing and place. The industrial revolution happened in Britain in the eighteenth century because that was where a high-wage, cheap-energy economy had emerged, at a moment when artisan skill and a commercial culture could respond to the prices with machinery. Allen does not deny science a role, particularly in the later development of the technologies and in the nineteenth-century broadening of industry. He denies it the role of prime mover for the first wave. The prime mover is induced innovation, the economic idea that the direction of invention follows the relative prices of inputs. Empire sits in the background of the high wages rather than at the centre of the factory. Trade, including the trade in colonial goods and the trade in enslaved people that made some of those goods possible, helped to make England the kind of rich, urban, high-wage economy in which the prices arose. Allen's coal is local. His demand is partly global. A reader who wants a single villain or a single virtue will not get one. The book is a price history with a political setting.

The evidence is a large comparative file of wages, prices, and output, much of it assembled in Allen's own earlier work, together with the technical history of the particular machines. The famous illustrations, the profitability of the jenny at English and at French wages, the energy cost of different iron processes, are meant to show that the incentive story can be calculated and not merely told. The calculations depend on the wage and price reconstructions, which are themselves scholarly objects with margins of error. The direction of the argument has been more persuasive than any one break-even figure. Labour-saving bias in the British inventions is a fact about the technology that any rival theory has to explain.

The debate with Pomeranz and with Mokyr

Set beside The Great Divergence, Allen's book is both an ally and a critic. It is an ally on coal. A mineral energy regime, available in the right place, is central to both accounts, and both reject the idea that Britain won because its markets were uniquely real. It is a critic on timing. Allen's wage comparisons are part of the body of work which suggests that north-western Europe had a lead in living standards before the factories, so that the late parity Pomeranz argued for cannot be the baseline. If English labour was already expensive relative to Jiangnan, the divergence was underway, and the industrial revolution amplified a difference rather than creating one out of a tie. The criticism does not restore the old story in which Europe had been destined for centuries by its culture. A wage gap has causes, commercial and agricultural, and those causes are historical rather than civilisational.

Set beside Joel Mokyr's culture of growth, the argument is a division of labour. Mokyr asks why sustained technological progress became a normal feature of European society, and he answers with the Republic of Letters, useful knowledge, and political fragmentation. Allen asks why the first wave of industrial machines was British and labour-saving, and he answers with prices. A culture that generates many ideas does not tell you which ideas are built. A price structure that rewards labour-saving does not tell you why ideas were plentiful rather than, as in earlier booms, a burst that then stalled. The two explanations are complementary in the way the card on this path suggests, and they conflict only if each is asked to be the whole cause. Allen's limit is the complement of his strength. He is less interested in the institutions that kept knowledge public, and less interested in politics except as they show up in trade and in wages. A reader who thinks the patent system, the guilds, or the fiscal-military state did independent work will find those mechanisms thinner in his account than the wage series. The book earns the right to be thin. It has a precise question, and the prices answer it more directly than a general history of European civilisation can.

Why it matters here

The card pomeranz-divergence presents the late, coal-and-colonies account of the industrial divergence and then names Allen's book as the major wage-and-energy alternative. This is that alternative. High British wages, cheap British coal, induced labour-saving invention, and a diffusion lag as the machines improved are the claims the card is summarising when it says that later quantitative work puts the European lead earlier than Pomeranz allowed, while still leaving energy at the centre. Folio uses the book as the bridge between a comparison of cores and a mechanism inside the British economy. Pomeranz explains the room the organic economy lacked. Allen explains why the particular machines that filled that room were built in Britain first, and why they were the kind of machines that saved labour and burned coal. The culture-of-growth argument sits beside both, and neither of them has to be discarded for the third to be worth reading.
