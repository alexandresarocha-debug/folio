James C. Scott's date for the first states is the embarrassing one. In Against the Grain (2017) he puts the first stratified states in the valley of the Tigris and the Euphrates at only around 3100 BCE, more than four thousand years after the first domesticated crops and the first settled life. People had been planting grain, and living for part of the year in one place, for millennia without a taxman, a wall, or a king. The standard story, in which farming leads promptly to villages, cities, writing, and the state, squeezes that gap into a step. Scott's reading of the archaeology is that the gap is the fact that needs explaining. Sedentism began in rich wetlands, where people could gather, fish, and plant a little, and still leave. Full dependence on a few cultivated grains came later, and the state later still.

His reason for the delay is the tax. Wheat and barley ripen above ground, all at once, where an official can see the field, measure the harvest, and put it in a store. Roots and tubers sit underground and can be pulled as a household needs them, which makes them a poor base for a levy. Scott's line is that only the cereal grains can serve as a basis for taxation. Once a population was concentrated on that kind of field, the early state could count it, feed soldiers and scribes from the store, and punish flight. He argues that the walls around those cities were as much to keep taxpayers in as to keep raiders out. The people inside, on the skeletal evidence he gathers, paid in their bodies. Farmers who lived on the grains were shorter and less well nourished than the foragers around them, and the women were more often anemic. States of this kind were also fragile. Disease, crop failure, and flight could empty them. Scott thinks that for many of the people inside, the collapse of such a state was an improvement.

The use of the argument, when someone is deciding what "the rise of civilization" was, is to separate planting from being governed. A family can grow barley and still have no state. A state, on this account, arrives when a grain crop is visible and storable enough to support people who do not grow it, and when the growers can be stopped from walking away. Writing, in the same telling, begins as a way to record rations and dues, not as literature. The epic comes after the account book.

Specialists have pushed on the tax rule. A review in the American Journal of Archaeology (2019) calls the claim that only cereals can be taxed an open question: labor, herds, and trade were levied too, and irrigation towns existed well before Uruk. The health contrast in the skeletons can be real without proving that nobody ever entered a grain state except by force. Scott is candid that the early state was a late and brittle arrangement. He is less interested in the reasons people sometimes stayed.
