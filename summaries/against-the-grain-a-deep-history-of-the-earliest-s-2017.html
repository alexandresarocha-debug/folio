The standard story, reversed

James C. Scott’s Against the Grain, published in 2017, is a short book with a deliberately simple target. The standard story of civilisation says that people domesticated plants and animals, that farming then produced a surplus, and that surplus produced villages, cities, states, writing, and the rest of the apparatus we were taught to call progress. The sequence is treated as an ascent, and the ascent as a choice that, once made, no reasonable person would unmake. Scott argues that the sequence is wrong in its timing, wrong in its geography, and wrong in its moral. Domestication of plants and animals long predates the first states. The first states, in the southern Mesopotamian alluvium and in a few comparable settings, were late, fragile, and based on a very particular kind of farming. A great many people, for a very long time, lived in the gap: cultivating without being subjects, mixing hunting, fishing, foraging, and planting, and, when states did appear, often running away from them. The book is a deep history of early states written by a political scientist whose sympathies are with the people who stayed outside.

He is not an archaeologist of Mesopotamia, and he says so. The book is a synthesis of other people’s specialist work, pushed into a political argument he had been developing for decades in Seeing Like a State and in his studies of upland southeast Asia. The argument is that states need legibility. They need resources they can see, count, store, and seize. Grain provides that. A history of the earliest states that starts from this requirement looks different from a history that starts from the idea of civilisation. It looks like an account of a niche technology of extraction, successful in river valleys under specific conditions, unnecessary and often unattractive everywhere else.

Domestication before the state

The opening movement separates domestication from state-making by a gap that, on the archaeological chronology he uses, is measured in thousands of years. People were shaping the reproduction of plants, animals, and landscapes long before anyone was collecting a tax. Sedentism, too, appears in rich wetlands where several food sources overlapped, without kings and without the later package of walls, scribes, and conscription. Scott’s account of the southern Mesopotamian marshes is the centrepiece. The first states arose not in a barren desert that had to be conquered by heroic irrigation, but in an abundance of reeds, fish, wild grains, and waterfowl, a landscape that already supported people. Irrigation and plough agriculture were intensifications inside that abundance, not the rescue of humanity from hunger. The moral of the standard story, that the state is the alternative to starvation, is, on this chronology, difficult to tell with a straight face.

He folds human beings into the domestication story rather than leaving them as its authors. Living with crops and herds changed bodies, work rhythms, and diets. The domus, the household complex of people, animals, stores, and waste, created densities at which new diseases could hold. Scott’s plague chapter, which he builds as an epidemiological argument rather than as a narrative of any single epidemic, is there to remove the glow from early urbanism. Crowding plus domesticated animals plus stored grain is an excellent environment for pathogens that foraging bands, spread thin, could not have sustained. The first towns were not only achievements of coordination. They were disease pools, and they seem often to have been demographic sinks, consuming people faster than they produced them and replacing the dead by capture. A state that needs a constant inflow of subjects is a state that people have reasons to avoid.

Why grain, and why walls

The analytical core of the book is the political economy of the cereal. Wheat, barley, rice, and later maize share a set of properties that a tax collector can love. They ripen at roughly the same time, so the harvest is a visible event. They stand above ground, so the crop can be surveyed before it is cut. They can be measured in standard units, stored for years, and transported to a granary or a garrison. A state that assesses a field of barley can know, within the usual limits of corruption, what it is owed. Compare a tuber. Cassava, yams, or potatoes ripen less obediently, sit underground where an assessor cannot see them, and rot on a schedule that is awkward for a magazine. Populations that live on roots are, by Scott’s hypothesis, harder to govern. He thinks it is not an accident that the classical early states are grain states, and that zones of tubers and of shifting cultivation have so often been zones of weak or absent state power. The argument is the deep-historical version of the legibility thesis in Seeing Like a State. What cannot be counted cannot be ruled, and grain is the first great technology of counting food.

Walls, in this light, have a double use that the standard story underplays. They keep raiders out, which is the use in every textbook. They also keep cultivators in. A population that can walk away to the wetlands, with its knowledge of how to live on fish and wild plants, is a population that can escape the corvée, the debt, and the epidemic. Early state elites, Scott argues, were preoccupied with holding labour, which is why slavery, debt bondage, and the forced resettlement of captives appear with the first archives. The documents of the earliest states are, to a remarkable degree, documents about grain, land, and dependent people. Barbarism, the name those states gave to the people outside, often meant a preferable tax rate. The outside was not a void. It was the alternative political economy, and it was close.

Collapse as a possible improvement

Because he has described states as fragile machines for concentrating grain and people, Scott can redescribe collapse. The archaeological record of early states is a record of frequent breakdown: centres abandoned, literacy interrupted, populations dispersing. The standard gloss is disaster, a dark age. Scott asks: dark for whom? A scribe’s unemployment and the end of monumental building are losses to the historical record and to the elite. They are not automatically losses to a cultivator who keeps the land and loses the tax. Dispersal into smaller settlements can mean better health, a more varied diet, and the end of a labour regime. He does not claim this for every collapse, and he is aware that some endings were violent, hungry, and unwelcome. He claims that the presumption should be reversed. The absence of state records is evidence about records, not a direct reading of welfare. “Against the grain” is among other things a plea to stop taking the archive’s point of view as the species’ point of view.

The last movement of the book extends the thought into what he calls a golden age of the barbarians, the long period in which states existed but could not yet enclose the world. Pastoralists, foragers, and escapees traded with states, raided them, served them as mercenaries, and retreated out of reach. They were not relics of a stage the state had superseded. They were produced by the state, as its frontier, its victims, and its necessary partners. Only much later, with gunpowder, standing armies, and the closing of frontiers, did the option of staying outside narrow towards what a modern person would recognise as universal administration. The earliest states were not the destiny of the species. They were one arrangement, locally advantageous to their elites, chronically short of people, and surrounded for millennia by populations who had looked at the arrangement and declined it.

Where specialists push back

The book has been argued with, as Scott expected a synthesis this sweeping to be. Archaeologists have objected that the early state was more various than the grain-and-wall model, that some complex polities do not fit the Mesopotamian template, and that the welfare consequences of collapse are not uniformly pleasant. Andean societies, which administered landscapes that included tubers and maize together, complicate any claim that cereals are the unique substrate of the state. Epidemiological claims about the first cities are plausible and unevenly documented. There is also a political objection that is really a disagreement about emphasis. Scott’s sympathy for the fugitive can lead him to romanticise life outside, which had its own coercions, and to understate the goods, insurance against local famine, a wider peace, a craft specialisation, that even an extractive centre sometimes provided. None of these objections restores the old ascent narrative. They limit the neatness of cereal determinism. The core chronological claim, that domestication and even sedentism do not promptly produce states, is widely accepted. The political reading of grain as a technology of appropriation is the part that has entered the conversation as his own.

Why it matters here

Folio’s card on Against the Grain is this argument in brief: farming long before states, wetlands rather than a desperate invention of agriculture, cereals as the taxable crop, walls that hold subjects in, disease in the new densities, and collapse as a possible relief. The card pairs the book with Seeing Like a State, and the pair is the right one. The earlier book shows how modern states make societies legible with surnames, cadastral maps, planned cities, and scientific forestry, and how those projects fail when they ignore local knowledge. Against the Grain finds the same project at the beginning: legibility as the demand that food come in a form an official can measure, and failure as the ordinary condition of the first attempts. Read this book for the deep history, and read it as a political argument wearing archaeological clothes. Scott is asking why the state should be treated as the successful climax of the species, when for most of the years since farming began it was a local, coercive, and regularly abandoned experiment.
