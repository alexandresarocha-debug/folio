The usual story of civilization runs in a straight line. People domesticated plants, farming produced a surplus, and the surplus produced villages, cities, and states. James C. Scott's Against the Grain (2017) moves the dates. On the chronology he uses, the first stratified states on the Tigris and Euphrates appear only around 3100 BC, more than four thousand years after the first crop domestications and the first settled villages. People had been planting, herding, and living in rich wetlands long before anyone collected a tax. The early state was late, local, and fragile. It is a poor candidate for the institution that rescued humanity from hunger.

What made a state workable, in his account, was the shape of the crop. Wheat, barley, and the other cereals ripen together, stand where an assessor can see them, and can be measured, stored, and hauled to a granary. Scott's line is blunt: only the cereal grains can serve as a basis for taxation. A root such as cassava sits underground and rots on a schedule that ruins a magazine. Walls, he argues, kept cultivators in as well as raiders out. The early archives are full of grain, land, and unfree labor because the state was short of people and could not afford to let taxpayers walk back into the marshes.

A "dark age" changes shape under that description. When a center is abandoned and the scribes stop, the loss is certain for the archive and for the elite who ate the tax. It is an open question for the cultivator who keeps the field and drops the levy. Scott allows that some endings were hungry and violent. He wants the presumption turned around. Missing state records are evidence about records.

Specialists have cut the claim down. A 2019 review in the American Journal of Archaeology left the rule that only cereals can be taxed as an open question, and a review in the Indian Economic and Social History Review pointed to labor, tribute, and tree crops, including the date palm in Mesopotamia and the olive and the vine in the Mediterranean. The gap of several thousand years between domestication and the first states is the part that has held. Grain as a technology of appropriation is Scott's own argument, and it is the part still disputed. A history that treats the first kingdoms as the obvious improvement of the species is reading the file the tax office kept.

Source. James C. Scott, Against the Grain: A Deep History of the Earliest States (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2017). https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300240214/against-the-grain/
