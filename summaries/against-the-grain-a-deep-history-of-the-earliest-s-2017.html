The first states in the valley of the Tigris and the Euphrates appear around 3100 BCE. Crop domestication and settled life in the same part of the world are more than four thousand years older. James C. Scott's Against the Grain (2017) puts that gap at the front of the book, against the story in which farming promptly produces a surplus, the surplus produces a city, and the city is the happy ending. For most of those four millennia, people planted and also hunted, fished, and moved. The early state was late, local, and easy to leave.

What made a state possible, on his account, was not farming in general. It was grain. Wheat and barley ripen together, stand where an official can see them, and can be measured, stored, and moved to a granary. A tax collector can assess a field of barley before it is cut. A field of roots is a worse prospect. Cassava, yams, or potatoes sit underground, ripen less obediently, and rot on a schedule that is awkward for a magazine. Scott's claim is that the classical early states are grain states because grain is the first great technology of making food visible. The walls around a place like Uruk did the usual work of keeping raiders out. They also kept cultivators in. A population that knew how to live on fish and wild plants in the wetlands could walk away from the corvée, the debt, and the epidemic. The early archives are, to a striking degree, lists of grain, land, and dependent people. Slavery and the resettlement of captives show up with the first records because the state was chronically short of bodies.

The bodies it did hold paid a price that the monuments do not record. Scott gathers the skeletal evidence: people who settled down to grain were smaller and less well nourished than the foragers around them, and the women were more often anaemic. Living with crops, herds, stored grain, and each other's waste, in what he calls a late Neolithic multispecies resettlement camp, was an excellent way to catch a new disease. Early towns often look, on this reading, like places that consumed people faster than they produced them and replaced the dead by capture. Collapse, which the written record treats as a dark age, could be a relief for the person who kept the field and lost the tax. He does not claim that for every ruin. He claims that the absence of scribes is evidence about scribes, not a direct reading of whether ordinary people were better off.

The use of the argument is as a check on the word civilization. When a history treats the state as the point of the harvest, it is taking the archive's side. Grain made a taxable staple. It did not make kings inevitable, and the people outside the walls were not a leftover from an earlier stage. They were the alternative.

David Graeber and David Wengrow, in The Dawn of Everything (2021), granted the gap and then pointed to the Trypillia settlements in Ukraine, large planned towns around 4000 BCE with little sign of a king. Growing grain and living in a crowd did not, by themselves, produce a tax state. Specialists have also noted that early Mesopotamian records count animals, dates, and labor alongside barley. The state was never a barley machine only.
