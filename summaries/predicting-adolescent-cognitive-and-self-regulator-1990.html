The follow-up that turned a waiting room into a life story

Yuichi Shoda, Walter Mischel and Philip Peake published Predicting adolescent cognitive and self-regulatory competencies from preschool delay of gratification in 1990. The title is the claim that made the marshmallow test a public parable. Children who, years earlier, had waited longer for a larger treat were, as adolescents, rated as more competent by their parents, and they had higher SAT scores. A behaviour in a nursery, seconds and minutes of holding out while a sweet sat in view, appeared to reach forward into school achievement and into the kind of person adults said the child had become. Folio's card tells this as the famous part of a research programme whose original focus had been different.

The original focus matters, because the 1990 paper is a longitudinal claim laid on top of experiments about strategy. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, at Stanford's Bing Nursery School, Mischel and his colleagues had offered children one treat now or two if they could wait, often for up to fifteen or twenty minutes, while the experimenter was out of the room. Those studies asked what helped children delay. Distraction helped. Thinking about the treat in a cool, abstract way helped. Staring at its tempting features did not. The children were inventive: some covered their eyes, some sang. The research was about techniques. The 1990 paper is about prediction. It takes the individual differences in how long children had waited and asks whether those differences line up with life a decade later. A reader who collapses the two projects will think Mischel set out to rank destinies.

The card's caution, and the reason Watts, Duncan and Quan revisited the test in 2018, is already visible in the shape of this sample. The follow-ups were small. The children were largely the sons and daughters of Stanford staff and students. A correlation in a small, advantaged group can be real and still be a poor basis for a general theory of who succeeds. The 1990 paper is the document that states the correlation. It has to be read with its sample in view, and with the later, larger revisit beside it.

What the preschool measure actually was

Delay of gratification, in these studies, is an observed wait in a specific situation. The child knows the contingency. The experimenter leaves. The treat, or a signal of the treat, is present in a way the design controls, because presence and abstraction had been the experimental variables. The seconds the child lasts are the score. That score is a sample of behaviour, one occasion or a few, in a room arranged by psychologists. It is a single occasion rather than a trait compiled across many situations, and it is a long way from a school grade. Treating it as a pure index of willpower is an interpretation.

Shoda, Mischel and Peake are nonetheless doing something more careful than the parable admits. They are asking about prediction from a behaviour, not announcing that the behaviour is a virtue one is born with. A predictive relation can arise because the waiting expresses a stable capacity, because the waiting and the later competence share a cause in the home, or because both. The 1990 design, given who the children were, has limited power to separate those possibilities. Almost everyone in the sample came from an educated, well-resourced university community. The range of family background is compressed. Within a compressed range, a correlation between waiting and later SAT scores can look like evidence that individual differences in delay are what matter, simply because the larger differences in circumstance have been designed out by the recruitment. That is a feature of the sample.

The outcomes themselves are worth pausing on. Parental ratings of adolescent competence are judgements by adults who have known the child all along, and who may already see patience as part of what they mean by competence. SAT scores are a standardised academic measure with its own dependence on schooling, coaching, and the verbal and quantitative culture of the home. A correlation with both is impressive in a small sample, and it is ambiguous. It says that children who waited longer tended to be children who, years on, looked more successful on these particular indicators. The correlations leave open whether waiting caused the success, and whether a parent who wanted to raise a child's scores should train the wait. Those causal sentences are the parable. They are an extra step the correlations do not take.

How a correlation became a moral

The cultural career of the finding is part of the paper's meaning, because Folio's card is written against the career as much as against the statistics. A child, a marshmallow, an empty room, and a later triumph fitted a wider appetite for character as the explanation of unequal outcomes. Schools, parents, and essayists could take that moral and run. Mischel's own later book, The Marshmallow Test, pushes a different moral, closer to the original experiments: strategies can be learned, attention can be redirected, the capacity is a skill. The 1990 paper is the hinge. It supplied the numbers the character story wanted, and it came from a scientist whose experimental work had been about skill.

The hinge has a statistical fragility that the story could not use. Small longitudinal samples are sensitive to who happens to be in them and to how a few individuals sit on both variables. A striking correlation in such a sample is a reason for a larger study. It is a reason to close the question. The 2018 revisit is that larger study. Watts, Duncan and Quan, with over nine hundred children drawn more broadly, found that the association between delay and later achievement was about half the size of the original, and that controls for family background, early cognitive ability, and home environment reduced the remaining association by roughly two thirds. The 1990 result is what they were revisiting. Read after 2018, it looks like a real signal, inflated by a narrow sample and by the absence of the controls a wider sample makes possible.

Calling the estimate inflated leaves the authors' honesty about their own sample untouched. The follow-up reported what it found in the children it had. The error was in the generalisation, much of it performed by readers rather than by the authors, from those children to children as such. A scientific literature is responsible for the generalisation too, when it cites a small advantaged sample as the evidence that self-control is the key to success. The 1990 paper is the citation. Using it well means quoting it as a finding about a particular cohort, with a particular nursery's procedures, and then asking the question Watts and colleagues asked of it.

What the earlier experiments still hold

The strategy experiments are the part of the programme the longitudinal controversy should not be allowed to eat. If distraction and abstract thought help a child wait, that is a fact about the moment of waiting, demonstrated by assigning children to different ways of thinking. Those assignments do not depend on the SAT. They suggest that the wait is a cognitively prepared act, sensitive to attention, and therefore sensitive to what an adult has helped a child practise. A parent or a teacher who takes that lesson can work on the techniques without believing that a four-year-old's minutes are a verdict. The 1990 paper borrows its prestige from those experiments, because the delay score is meaningful only if one understands it as a measure of something the child is doing. The prestige flowed, in public, from the score to the life story, and the techniques became a footnote.

Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin's 2013 study adds a further hold on the moment itself. Children who had just watched an experimenter break promises about art supplies waited much less than children who had watched the experimenter keep faith. The delay score Shoda, Mischel and Peake used as a predictor is a score that, in another room, moves when trust moves. In a sample of Stanford families, trust in strange adults and in promised rewards may have been high and fairly even, so differences in waiting mostly reflected something else. In a broader population, trust and resources vary, and the same score mixes them with whatever capacity the strategy experiments isolated. The 1990 correlations are correlations with a mixed score, estimated where several ingredients of the mix varied less than they do in the country. That is a kind sentence and a limiting one.

A reader can therefore keep three propositions at once. Preschool delay, in this cohort, lined up with later ratings and SAT scores. The lining up was estimated in a small and particular group. The behaviour being measured is something children accomplish with strategies, in an environment whose reliability they have reason to notice. The parable kept the first proposition and spent the other two. Folio's card spends all three, and it sends the reader back to this paper so the first proposition is seen in the form the authors actually published, before the cartoon of a single marshmallow and a single destiny replaces it.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, The marshmallow test: willpower or circumstance?, identifies this 1990 paper as the follow-up that made waiting famous as a predictor of adolescent competence and SAT scores. The card then qualifies the fame with the sample, small and drawn from Stanford families, and with the 2018 revisit, in which the association shrank by about half and then by roughly two thirds more under controls for background, early ability, and home. Read Shoda, Mischel and Peake for the original predictive claim, held at the size and in the population they actually studied. Read Watts, Duncan and Quan for the claim under a wider lens. Read Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin for the experimental evidence that waiting tracks whether adults have kept their word. The 1990 paper is the source of the parable. The rest of the card is the reason the parable now has a smaller voice.
