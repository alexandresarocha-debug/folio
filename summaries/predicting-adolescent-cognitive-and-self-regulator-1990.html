Between 1968 and 1974, Walter Mischel and colleagues sat 653 preschoolers at the Bing Nursery School, on the Stanford campus, in front of a choice. The child could ring a bell and have a smaller treat now, or wait until the experimenter came back and have a larger one. The treats were things like marshmallows or pretzels. Waiting past 15 minutes was scored as 15 minutes. About a decade later the parents of the children who could be found were asked for ratings and, where they had them, SAT scores. Yuichi Shoda, Mischel, and Philip K. Peake, writing in 1990, had 185 children with at least one follow-up measure. The average age at the original test was 4 years and 4 months, and the average wait in that group was 512.8 seconds. Parents reported SAT scores for 94 children. The Educational Testing Service found scores for 69 of those 94, and the parent reports correlated .94 with the scores in the testing service’s files.

The prediction worked in one condition and not in the others. When the treats were left in sight and nobody suggested a way to cope, seconds of waiting lined up with later scores: the correlation was .57 with the SAT quantitative score and .42 with the SAT verbal score, in the 35 children from that condition who had scores. In that same condition the verbal correlation was .02 for the 17 boys and .74 for the 18 girls. When the treats were covered, or when the experimenter had suggested a strategy, the SAT correlations were not the positive ones the reputation of the test depends on. Delay, the authors argued, indexed a child’s own ability to manage attention only when the child had to invent that management, with the reward sitting there.

A parent or a teacher who treats “waited for the marshmallow” as a measure of character is using the one version of the test in which a small, well-off Stanford sample showed a link to a college-entrance exam. The same child, with the treat out of sight or with a tip about what to think about, was not revealing the same thing. Using the wait to decide who is disciplined, who gets a chance, or whose parent has done the job, imports a result from 35 children and from one arrangement of the room.

Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan, and Haonan Quan, in 2018, repeated the question in 552 children whose mothers had not finished college. The bivariate link to achievement at age 15 was about half the size of these SAT correlations, and about two-thirds of it disappeared once family background, early ability, and the home were taken into account.
