Kurt Vonnegut liked to graph a story, time along the bottom and the hero's fortune up the side. He said the University of Chicago had rejected a master's thesis built on those graphs. In 2016 Andrew J. Reagan, Lewis Mitchell, Dilan Kiley, Christopher M. Danforth, and Peter Sheridan Dodds asked whether a large shelf of fiction really does fall into a few such lines. They started from Project Gutenberg's collection of roughly 50,000 books and kept English works classed as fiction, between 20,000 and 100,000 words, downloaded more than 40 times. After dropping books whose titles looked like poems or collections, and stripping front and back matter, they had 1,327 books. Through each book they slid a window of 10,000 words and scored the window with a dictionary, labMT, that rates words as happier or sadder. The result is not a plot. It is a curve of how positive or negative the language is as the book goes on.

Three different methods, a matrix decomposition, a clustering, and an unsupervised learner, kept finding the same six curves. A rise: "rags to riches." A fall: "tragedy," or "riches to rags." A fall then a rise: "man in a hole." A rise then a fall: "Icarus." A rise, a fall, and a rise: "Cinderella." A fall, a rise, and a fall: "Oedipus." The first mode, the simple rise and its opposite, the fall, accounted for 30 percent of the variance in the mean-centered curves. The names are shapes of the word-scores, not claims that those novels retell the old stories. On their list of the books closest to a fall, Jane Austen's Lady Susan is near the top, and Romeo and Juliet is number 8. A computer that only knows whether words lean happy or sad will file a play and an epistolary novella together if the language darkens at a similar rate.

They then compared shapes with download counts. The arcs with the higher medians were Icarus, Oedipus, and a mode they read as two "man in a hole" shapes in sequence. The simpler rise and the simpler fall, which covered more of the books, were not the most downloaded. The authors say outright that downloads are only a rough proxy for success. A book is downloaded because it is assigned, because it is short, or because it is famous, not only because its emotional line is satisfying. They also note that a sentiment dictionary usually does worse than chance on a single sentence. Irony, negation, and a wedding the reader already fears will fool a window that only averages word scores. What survives those limits is still a real finding about this shelf. The emotional course of the language is not a haze of unique scribbles. A handful of rises and falls, and a few combinations of them, cover most of the variation. That is a fact about word happiness in one public-domain corpus. It is not a list of the only plots a writer is allowed to use.
