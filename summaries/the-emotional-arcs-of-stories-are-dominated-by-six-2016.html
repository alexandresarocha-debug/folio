Vonnegut's graphs, taken as a testable claim

The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by six basic shapes, published in 2016 by Andrew Reagan, Lewis Mitchell, Dilan Kiley, Christopher Danforth and Peter Sheridan Dodds, asks whether a playful idea about plot can be made into a measurement. Kurt Vonnegut had proposed that stories be graphed, with time on one axis and the hero's good or ill fortune on the other. He told the story of a master's thesis at the University of Chicago that, on his account, was rejected, and he later put the graphs into lectures and into Palm Sunday. A person falls into a hole and climbs out. A person gains, loses and gains again. Cinderella rises, falls, and rises further. The graphs are memorable because they treat shape as the thing stories share, underneath their settings. They are also, as Vonnegut offered them, a performance. Reagan and his colleagues take the performance seriously enough to ask whether a large library of novels, read by a machine in a deliberately crude way, really does cluster into a few such shapes.

The crudeness is the method. A computer cannot, in the sense a critic means, follow a plot. It can, given a lexicon that scores words for how happy or unhappy they are, slide a window through a novel and record the average emotional tone of the words. The resulting curve is an emotional arc in a narrow sense: not who did what to whom, but whether the language, as it proceeds, leans positive or negative. If those curves are idiosyncratic, the Vonnegut hunch fails in this operational form. If they cluster, the hunch survives as a claim about the emotional course of a corpus, which is not the same thing as a claim about plot, and which is still a claim worth having. The paper is an argument in digital humanities, and it is candid about living in that field's argument about what measurement does to literature.

What was measured, and how the shapes were found

The corpus is a large set of English fiction from Project Gutenberg, more than 1,300 works, filtered so that the books are long enough for a curve to mean something and so that the set is fiction rather than the whole miscellaneous archive. Through each book the authors compute a sentiment time series and smooth it. Smoothing is a choice with consequences. A window that is too sensitive turns every local death and wedding into a spike. A window that is too smooth turns every book into a slow wave and can manufacture an arc the chapters do not have. On the smoothed curves they use decomposition and clustering to ask which shapes account for most of the variation. The question is not which curve is the most beautiful. The question is which curves keep reappearing, so that a small menu describes a large shelf.

The menu they find has six basic shapes, and the names are chosen so that a reader of Vonnegut can recognise the geometry. Rags to riches is a rise. Riches to rags is a fall. Man in a hole is a fall followed by a rise. Icarus is a rise followed by a fall. Cinderella is a rise, a fall, and a rise. Oedipus is a fall, a rise, and a fall. These are shapes of the sentiment series, so the Oedipus shape is not a claim that those novels retell Sophocles, and the Cinderella shape is not a claim that they end at a ball. The names say what the line does. A book can belong to a shape because its language darkens and then brightens, whatever the causes of the darkening are. The paper's central result is the domination itself. The arcs are not a haze of unique scribbles. They are dominated by these six.

Downloads, and the idea of a preferred shape

A second, more adventurous result concerns attention. The authors compare the shapes with how often the books are downloaded, and they find that some shapes, among them Icarus and Oedipus, are associated with more downloads. The suggestion is that readers do not only sort themselves by genre and by author. They also, in the aggregate, linger with certain emotional trajectories, particular combinations of rise and fall. A rise followed by a fall, or a fall that recovers and then fails again, may be more compelling, in this archive, than a simpler line. The suggestion fits a long critical hunch that reversal is more interesting than accumulation. It also goes beyond anything the download count can firmly show. Downloads are a weak proxy for pleasure. A book may be downloaded because it is assigned, because it is famous, or because it is short. A shape may be associated with downloads because famous books happen to have that shape, rather than because the shape made them famous.

The cautious form of the result is still useful. In this corpus, shape is not independent of popularity. The emotional geometry and the public's choices are correlated for some geometries. That is a finding about a particular public-domain collection and a particular measure of attention. It is not a recipe for writing a successful novel, and the paper is less persuasive when it is quoted as one. A writer who aimed a manuscript at the Icarus curve would be aiming at the average tone of words, which is not the same target as a plot that turns. The result belongs with the descriptive claim. These are the shapes that dominate, and some of them draw more of this audience.

What a sentiment arc is not

The strongest objection to the paper is the objection the authors' own method invites. The emotional tone of words is not the plot. A word associated with unhappiness lowers the curve even when the reader is delighted that the villain has met it. A wedding raises the curve even when the reader knows the marriage is a disaster the narrator has not yet admitted. Irony, free indirect style, and negation all make local sentiment a poor guide to the experience of reading. Smoothing, which is required if one wants a shape rather than a seismograph, adds a further distance. Two books can share an arc and share almost nothing else, including the causal structure that Aristotle would have called the plot and that Freytag would have divided into rise, turn and fall. The paper shows that sentiment trajectories cluster. Anyone who hears that result as a discovery of the six plots of world literature has changed the subject.

Where the six shapes sit in a longer argument

Set beside Freytag, the paper is a descendant and a departure. Freytag's pyramid is one shape, a rise to a turn and a fall to a catastrophe, specified for five-act tragedy and justified by the logic of an action. The six arcs include something like that geometry and also its inversions and its compounds. Nothing in the sentiment method can see an exciting force or a moment of final suspense. What it can see is that, if you grant the method, the library is not infinitely various in its emotional course. Set beside Vonnegut, the paper is a vindication of a limited kind. The rejected thesis, whatever its actual fate at Chicago, proposed that the shapes were real and few. A corpus study finds shapes that are real in the operational sense and few in number. Set beside a critic's close reading, the paper is a complement rather than a competitor. Close reading can say why one Oedipus-shaped novel uses its falls as it does. The corpus study can say that the fall-rise-fall course is one of the courses English-language fiction, in this archive, keeps taking.

The limitation of the archive should stay in the claim. Project Gutenberg favours older works that have gone out of copyright, in English, and of the kinds of books that were scanned and classified as fiction. A domination observed there is not a domination observed in oral narrative, in contemporary fiction, or in drama. The six shapes are a description of a shelf, not a law of narrative. Their interest comes from how much of that shelf they describe, and from the continuity with a much older habit of drawing stories as lines. The habit runs from Aristotle's change of fortune, through Freytag's pyramid, through Vonnegut's chalk talk, to a decomposition of sentiment series. At each step the line means something thinner than it did in the step before, and at each step it covers more examples. The 2016 paper is the step at which the line becomes a measurement and the examples become a corpus.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, From Freytag's pyramid to the six shapes of stories, ends on this paper. After Freytag's five parts and Vonnegut's graphs of fortune, the card reports that a University of Vermont team led by Andrew Reagan used sentiment analysis on more than 1,300 works of fiction from Project Gutenberg and found six basic shapes: rags to riches, riches to rags, man in a hole, Icarus, Cinderella and Oedipus, with some shapes, such as Icarus and Oedipus, associated with more downloads. That is the paper's public result, stated at the right size. The card's deep dive supplies the caution the result needs. Sentiment is not plot, computational arcs have been criticised for oversimplification, and the Syuzhet debate is the local example of the criticism. Read Reagan and his colleagues for the measurement and for the six names. Read Freytag for a shape that was meant as a technique of tragic construction. The card is the argument that these belong in one history, provided the differences among them are kept visible.
