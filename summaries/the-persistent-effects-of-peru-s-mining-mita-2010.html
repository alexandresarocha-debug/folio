In 1545 the Spanish found that the mines around Potosí, high in the Andes, held more silver than any other place in their empire. To the northwest, near Huancavelica, there was a mine of mercury, which was needed to refine the silver. In 1573 the colonial government set up a labour system for these mines, called the mita. About 200 communities in the region had to send one-seventh of their workers, mostly farmers, to the mines. The system ran until 1812.

Melissa Dell, then a PhD student at MIT, asked whether anything of it remained. Her paper appeared in Econometrica in 2010. The difficulty is that places subject to the mita might have differed from others for other reasons, such as geography. So she used a method called regression discontinuity: she looked only at districts close to the border of the mita area, where the land, crops and ethnic makeup of the people are similar on both sides. The only thing that changes at the line is whether the district had to send workers.

She found that, today, household consumption is about 25 percent lower in the former mita districts, and the share of children with stunted growth is about 6 percentage points higher. That is a gap measured in the early 2000s, nearly two centuries after the system ended.

To find out why, she used historical records. After 1573 the districts outside the mita often had haciendas, large rural estates with many workers and secure property rights. Within the mita districts the Spanish mostly let small landholders stay, without strong rights. Over time the owners of big estates were better able to push for public goods such as roads to markets. Her data show that mita districts historically had fewer large landowners and lower educational attainment, and that today they are less connected to the road network and more of their residents are subsistence farmers. She also spent a month in Peru interviewing people. As MIT News reported, one peasant told her that other people grow corn just like them but are richer because they can take it to market, while the government never built roads for them.

What this means in real life: policies can leave traces that outlast the policy. It also changes a common story. Big estates are usually blamed for inequality in Latin America, but here, where small farmers had weak rights, the hacienda districts got more public investment.

Criticism and limits: the result is local, about the districts near one boundary, and persistence studies in general are open to questions about statistical method and about how much history can be compressed into a single cause. The paper tries to answer this by tracing intermediate steps in the records.

Source. Dell, M. (2010). The Persistent Effects of Peru's Mining Mita. Econometrica, 78(6), 1863-1903.
