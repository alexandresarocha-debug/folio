In the early 1970s Mark Lepper, David Greene, and Richard Nisbett watched preschool children at the Bing Nursery School on the Stanford campus. The children were between 40 and 64 months old. Teachers set out magic markers and good drawing paper, materials the rooms did not usually have, and observers behind a one-way mirror recorded who drew. Children who had already spent more than four minutes with the markers were invited, one at a time, into a room the experimenters called the surprise room.

Some children were shown a Good Player Award, a card with a gold star and a red ribbon, and asked whether they would like to draw in order to win one. Others drew for six minutes and received the same award only afterward. A third group drew and received nothing. One to two weeks later the markers returned to the classroom, and observers who did not know the assignments timed free play. Children who had drawn in order to win the award spent 8.59% of that free time with the markers. Children who had never heard of an award spent 16.73%. Children who had been handed the award as a surprise spent 18.09%. The expected-award children had also drawn worse pictures in the session itself. Three judges, blind to the condition, scored those pictures 2.18 on a five-point scale, against 2.85 for the surprise award and 2.69 for no award. The three groups produced about the same number of pictures.

The comparison inside the study is the part worth using. Children who got a prize they had not been promised kept drawing at least as much as children who got nothing. Children who had agreed to draw for the prize spent about half as much free time on the markers later, and their pictures at the time were judged a little worse. A promised sticker for reading, a payment for practicing an instrument a child already likes, and a classroom chart that turns play into a job sit with the first group. A surprise that arrives after the drawing is done sits with the second.

Source. Lepper, M. R., Greene, D., and Nisbett, R. E. (1973). Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward: A test of the "overjustification" hypothesis. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 28(1), 129–137. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0035519
