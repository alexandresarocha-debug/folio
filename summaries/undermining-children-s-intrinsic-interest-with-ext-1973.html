A certificate for an activity children already liked

Mark Lepper, David Greene and Richard Nisbett published Undermining children's intrinsic interest with extrinsic reward in 1973. It is the study Folio's card tells when it introduces the undermining effect, also called the overjustification effect. Preschoolers who already enjoyed drawing were promised a certificate, a tangible award, for doing the drawing. Other children drew without that promise. Later, when drawing was simply one of the things available in free time and no award was on offer, the children who had expected the certificate drew less than the children who had never been promised one. A reward attached to a beloved activity had made the activity less likely once the reward was gone.

The direction is the surprise, and it is the same kind of surprise as the dissonance studies in which a larger payment produced less attitude change. Common sense says that a prize should make an activity more attractive, or at least leave it as attractive as it was. The preschoolers who had drawn for a certificate seemed to like drawing less, measured by what they reached for when nobody was watching and nothing was being paid. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett offered overjustification as the name. The children already had a good reason to draw, their own interest. The certificate was an extra, excessive justification. When they later asked themselves, in effect, why they had been drawing, the certificate was an available answer, and the interest was no longer needed as the explanation. Remove the certificate and the explanation goes with it.

The study became a foundation for self-determination theory, though the theory's full statement came later. Ryan and Deci's 2000 paper treats the undermining effect as one of the findings a theory of motivation has to absorb, and Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 meta-analysis of 128 experiments is the attempt to say how general the finding is. The 1973 paper is the concrete case those later works are generalising. A walkthrough should keep the case concrete: the children, the drawing, the expected award, and the free-time test.

Why expectation is the hinge

The card's sentence is careful about expectation. The children who drew less were the ones who had expected a certificate. Expectation is the hinge because it changes the meaning of the activity while the activity is happening. A child who knows the certificate is coming can draw in order to get it. A child who is simply drawing, and who might receive something later without having been led to draw for that reason, is still drawing for the reason they had at the start. The overjustification account predicts a difference between those positions. The promised award relocates the cause. An award that plays no role in the decision to draw does not. Later reviews in this literature, including the 1999 meta-analysis, treat expectation as one of the features that separates rewards which undermine interest from rewards which do not.

The free-time test is the other hinge, and it is easy to skip in a retelling that stops at the award ceremony. The measure of interest is what the child does when the award is no longer available and the adults are no longer asking. If the researchers had only asked the children whether drawing was fun while the certificate was still in view, they would have learned something about politeness and about the power of a prize in the hand. By waiting, and by watching spontaneous play, they asked whether the activity still drew the child when it had to draw the child by itself. Less drawing in that window is the undermining. The measure is behavioural: a change in the chance that the child returns to the crayons.

The activity had to be one the children already liked. Undermining intrinsic interest presupposes the interest. A reward for an activity a child finds dreary is an ordinary piece of inducement, and this design is not a test of it. Drawing was chosen because, in these nurseries, it was something children did without being paid. That choice is a strength of the study and a boundary. The result speaks to the risk of paying people, including small people, for what they would have done from pleasure or curiosity. It does not speak, by itself, to the question of how to get a boring or aversive task done. Self-determination theory later had more to say about that second question, including the claim that reasons differ in how far a person can endorse them.

Overjustification, and the theory that adopted it

Overjustification is a cognitive story. The child looks at their own behaviour and at the circumstances and infers a motive. When the circumstances include a salient prize, the inferred motive is the prize. Intrinsic interest is discounted, in the way a shopper discounts an explanation that is unnecessary once a simpler one is on the table. The story is close to other ideas of its decade about how people read the causes of behaviour, and it does not require the child to be cynical or spoiled. A perfectly ordinary inference will do. I drew, and there was a certificate for drawing, so the certificate is why. The later loss of interest is the cost of that inference once the certificate has ended.

Self-determination theory keeps the phenomenon and offers a slightly different engine. Expected tangible rewards, contingent on doing the task, undermine interest because they frustrate autonomy. They move the perceived source of the action outside the self. The 1999 meta-analysis found that this kind of reward reliably reduced intrinsic motivation across a large experimental literature, while verbal praise tended to enhance it. Praise, on the theory's reading, can support competence without taking the action's source away. The certificate in the 1973 study is the tangible, expected, task-contingent case, which is exactly the case the meta-analysis later identified as the reliable one. A reader can prefer the overjustification inference, the autonomy account, or some mixture, and still treat the preschool result as the pattern those accounts were built to explain.

The pattern is what made the study famous outside psychology, because parents, teachers, and managers recognised the risk. A star chart for reading, a bonus for work someone used to find absorbing, a prize for play: each can be heard as a cousin of the certificate. The hearing is sometimes too quick. The 1973 study is about an expected tangible award for an activity that was already interesting, followed by a drop in spontaneous engagement. A word of specific praise, a surprise, or a reward for a task nobody liked are different cases, and the later meta-analysis exists because those differences mattered. Folio's card is written at the resolution the evidence supports. Expected tangible rewards of the task-contingent kind undermined interest.

Limits of a nursery afternoon

The study is one procedure with young children and one activity. Preschoolers are in the middle of learning what activities are for, and a certificate from an unfamiliar adult may loom larger for them than a similar token would for an adult with a settled love of drawing. The free-time window is short. A dip in one session is evidence of a shift in interest at that moment, The dip stays inside that session, and a life of art is a larger question. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett gave the field a clear experimental idea and a result that invited replication. The 1999 review is the record of how that invitation was taken up. The invitation's success, a reliable effect of the specified kind across many experiments, is what allows the card to treat undermining as a finding rather than as an anecdote.

The controversy belongs in the picture too. Eisenberger and Cameron argued in 1996 that negative effects of rewards were limited and could be avoided, particularly when rewards carried information about a standard. Their argument is a reason to read the 1999 reply, which sorts rewards rather than banning them. Nothing in the preschool study shows that telling a child their drawing is good will make them draw less. The card's summary of the meta-analysis says the opposite tendency for verbal praise. Holding the 1973 result up as a ban on encouragement is a misreading. Holding it up as a warning about promised tangible pay for passionate play is the reading the design earns.

There is a wider shelf on which this warning sits beside other famous claims that later replications have complicated. The undermining literature's own later decades include argument as well as confirmation, and a reader trained by Folio's replication cards will ask whether a 1973 effect, however often cited, has the kind of preregistered support the field now prefers. The 1999 meta-analysis is the strongest traditional answer, a large set of experiments pointing the same way for a defined class of rewards. It is still an answer from the published literature of its period. The right posture is the card's posture. Teach the certificate study as the classic demonstration, teach the distinctions among rewards as the mature form of the finding, and keep the dispute visible so that a nursery result is not asked to settle every argument about pay.

Why it matters here

Folio's card, Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness, begins the reward story with this experiment. Preschoolers who expected a certificate for drawing later drew less when drawing was free. The story continues in the 1999 meta-analysis, where expected tangible task-contingent rewards undermined intrinsic motivation across 128 experiments and verbal praise tended to enhance it, and in Ryan and Deci's 2000 article, where the effect becomes one consequence of thwarting autonomy and where praise becomes a possible support for competence. Read the 1973 paper for the original scene, which is still the scene people remember, and for the idea of overjustification that made the scene theoretical. Read the later works before you generalise the certificate to every reward a person will ever receive. The card's claim is narrower than the folklore, and this study is the folklore's source as well as the theory's first clear exhibit.
